## Chapter 62: The Unasked

For three days, the Wastes were empty.

They walked from first light until the light went, across plates of black glass that rang faintly under their boots like the lid of a pot, and around ridges of dark rock, and over seams of frozen dust that crunched like sugar. Yan Shuang walked in front with her one hand in her coat pocket, reading the glass the way a fisherman reads water: this plate is thin, go left; this seam is a crevasse under the frost, step wide; this ridge has a hollow on the lee side where the wind doesn't reach, we sleep there.

At night they slept in the lee of the ridges in a tight circle, with Iron Wall Zhao's shield set up against the wind like a door, and Old Seven standing at the edge of the firelight facing north, and Hundred-Arrow Lin somewhere out in the dark, a drift of ash that you only saw when the starlight caught the iron arrowhead in her chest.

The Mo twins argued in whispers until Su Mingli looked at them. Bai Lu checked everyone's feet for frostbite every evening, very seriously, with a torch in her teeth. Qin Hao cleaned the rifle he didn't need. Sergeant Sokolova didn't speak at all.

And Lingyue sat beside Yuanshu each night at the edge of the fire, with her sword across her knees, looking north, not talking. Which, he had learned, was how she said she was glad you were there.

---

On the fourth morning, the glass began to change.

It was subtle at first. The plates grew smaller, more broken, crazed with fine cracks like the glaze on an old bowl. The bones half buried in them grew more frequent. Not beasts now. Smaller. Human-sized. A hand, frozen into the glass with its fingers spread. A skull, face up, staring at the sky. A boot, with the bones of a foot still in it.

Then, around noon, the plates stopped being plates at all.

The ground ahead was a wide shallow valley, kilometers across, running from the southern horizon to the northern one. And the glass in the floor of the valley was not smooth. It was trampled. Cracked and powdered and ground down into a broad pale road of crushed glass and dust, a hundred meters wide, running straight as a ruler from south to north, as if something enormous and heavy had been dragged along it for years.

Yan Shuang stopped at the top of the valley's southern slope.

She took her hand out of her pocket and pointed. Not at the road. Up it. North. To where, very far away, almost at the limit of sight, a faint gray haze lay across the floor of the valley like dust kicked up behind a herd.

"There," she said. "That's what the trucks always went around."

---

They walked along the ridge above the valley for two hours before they caught up with it.

The haze grew. The gray smudge became a gray cloud, low and long, hanging over the pale road. And under the cloud, filling the valley from one side to the other, moving north at the pace of a slow walk, was a crowd.

Yuanshu stopped on the ridge and looked down.

They were dead.

All of them. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Skeletons, mostly, gray and brown with age, some still wrapped in rags of uniform or coat or blanket. Some with flesh still on them, dark and dry as old leather, the way the Sleepers had been. Soldiers. Farmers. Old men. Women. Children. A man in the remains of a postman's jacket with an empty bag over his shoulder. A woman in what had once been a wedding dress, gray now, trailing in the dust. A small skeleton, very small, holding the hand of a larger one.

They walked.

That was all they did. Shoulder to shoulder, filling the whole hundred-meter width of the road, a slow gray river of bones flowing north across the Wastes. Not fast. Not slow. Each foot rising and falling in the same rhythm as every other foot, so that the whole vast crowd moved together with a sound like a long, dry, endless exhalation: the scrape of ten thousand bone feet on crushed glass, over and over, rolling across the valley.

None of them looked up at the ridge.

None of them looked at anything at all.

They faced north and walked.

---

> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **They are raised.**
>
> **Every one of them. Death Law. Old. Very old. Thirty-one years.**
>
> **It is Lu Xuanming's.**

The notebook was a weight against his ribs. He did not need to take it out. He knew the words.

*I raised them. Not three. Not thirty. Everyone. Every dead body in three cities, all at once. I didn't ask them.*

*I can't put them down. The dead are still walking and I can't make them stop.*

"The history books say they fell," said Mo Xing, very quietly, behind him. His voice shook. "When he died. All ninety million. They fell where they stood. Everywhere. All at once. We learned it in second year. There was a test."

"Everyone who could see them fell," said Yan Shuang. She had sat down on a rock on the ridge and was rolling a cigarette one-handed, very slowly, without looking at the valley. "Nobody came out here to check."

> **Not ninety million.**
>
> **I am counting. As well as I can. The valley is twelve kilometers long and they fill it.**
>
> **Perhaps two hundred thousand. Perhaps a little more.**
>
> **I think these are the ones who were already walking north when he died. Following him. Following his last order.**
>
> **And nobody ever gave them another one.**

---

Su Mingli stood at the edge of the ridge with her arms folded and her face like stone.

"Thirty-one years," she said.

Nobody answered her.

"Thirty-one years," she said again, and her voice was very low, "walking. North. Across this. Without stopping. Without resting. Without anyone ever coming." She looked down at the small skeleton holding the larger one's hand. "I fought in the Black Tide. I was nineteen. I stood on the wall and watched them come out of the cities after he raised them, millions of them, walking north toward the thing in the sky. I cheered." She closed her eyes. "Then the war ended, and the history books said they fell, and I believed it, and I never once asked."

> **She is shaking.**
>
> **I have never seen her shake.**

---

Yuanshu went down the slope.

Nobody stopped him. Old Seven came behind him, two paces back, as always, spear grounded. The crushed glass slid and crunched under his boots. At the bottom, he stopped at the edge of the pale road, three meters from the nearest of the dead, and the gray river of bones went past him, scrape, scrape, scrape, close enough that he could feel the cold coming off them.

None of them turned.

A soldier in the rags of a gray Huaxian greatcoat. A farmer with a scythe still in his hand, the blade rusted to nothing. A grandmother with her head wrapped in what had been a red scarf. A boy of perhaps twelve, his school bag still on his back, the straps rotted through and held together with a knot someone had tied thirty-one years ago.

The smell was not the smell of death. It was dust and cold and old cloth. Like the inside of an attic.

He went quiet. All the way down. And he looked at them, the way Seed looked: not at the bones, at what was in them.

Very little.

A gray ember, in each. Faint. The last of what Lu Xuanming's law had kindled in them thirty-one years ago, burning down slowly, the way a candle burns down. Not a person. Not anymore. Just the order. *Walk.* Repeated. Over and over. Ten thousand kilometers of *walk.*

And under it, in some of them, very far down, like the waving at the bottom of Andrei Belov's well, something else.

> **They are still there.**
>
> **Not many. Not much. In some of them.**
>
> **A little bit of someone. At the bottom. Under the order.**
>
> **Yuanshu, I think some of them are very, very tired.**

---

He reached out.

He did not touch anyone. He did not reach into anyone. He held his open hand out over the edge of the road, palm up, the way you'd hold your hand out to a strange dog, and he did what he had done in a cemetery on the first day, and on a frozen pass, and on a mountainside full of graves, and in a crater under a ring in the sky.

He asked.

*My name is Zhou Yuanshu. I'm like the man who raised you. I'm sorry nobody came. Is there anything you want?*

The river of bones went past. Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.

Nothing.

Then, in the middle of the road, perhaps ten meters in, something happened that made Old Seven's flame flare white behind him.

A skeleton stopped.

Just one. A soldier. Tall. Broad-shouldered even as bones, in the rags of a Huaxian Defense Corps greatcoat from thirty-one years ago, with the faded red stripe of a sergeant still on what was left of one sleeve. It stopped mid-stride, one foot raised. And the dead behind it, without slowing, without looking, simply flowed around it, the way a river flows around a stone.

It stood there in the middle of the gray river for a long moment, with its foot in the air.

Then it put its foot down.

And very slowly, with a sound like dry sticks bending, it turned its skull.

Not north.

South. Toward the edge of the road. Toward a boy standing three meters away with his hand held out.

---

> **...**
>
> **It heard you.**
>
> **One. Out of two hundred thousand.**
>
> **It heard you, and it is trying to answer, and it does not remember how.**

Behind Yuanshu, on the crushed glass, Old Seven had gone completely still.

Not the stillness of a soldier on guard. Something else. His gray flame had shrunk down to a single bright bead in the cage of his ribs. His skull was turned toward the sergeant in the middle of the road. And his spear, the spear he had not let go of for thirty-one years, was slowly, very slowly, tilting down, until its point rested in the dust.

> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **Look at the sergeant's left hand.**

He looked.

The left hand of the dead sergeant hung at its side. Two of its fingers were missing. Old breaks. Clean. And on the remaining three, very faint under thirty-one years of dust, so faint you'd never see it unless you were looking for it, was a pattern of small white scars.

The kind you get from a spear shaft. Gripping it, every day, for years. The kind Old Seven had on his own hands.

The kind a spear captain teaches his men to earn.

> **The eastern line.**
>
> **Third Battalion.**
>
> **Yuanshu, I think this is one of his.**

Old Seven took one step forward, onto the edge of the pale road. Then another. The gray river of the dead went past him, scrape, scrape, and did not see him, and he did not see them. He walked through them as if they were mist, two meters ten of white bone and gray flame, until he stood in the middle of the road in front of the sergeant with two missing fingers.

The two dead soldiers looked at each other.

Then Captain Qi Zhenshan of the Third Battalion, who had held the eastern end of the wall alone on the last night of the Black Tide, raised his right hand in a crisp, perfect, thirty-year-old salute.

And slowly, with a sound like dry sticks breaking, his sergeant raised a hand with three fingers on it, and tried to remember how to answer.
