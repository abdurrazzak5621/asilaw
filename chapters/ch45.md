## Chapter 45: WARDEN

Doctor Iris Kwan came to the Ossuary at eleven o'clock at night, in the rain, with no umbrella.

Iron Wall Zhao saw her first. He was on his stool by the bronze door, the way he was every night after closing, a broad old skeleton sitting very still under the streetlamp with his great empty shield of rusted iron across his knees and rain running off its rim. A small figure came up East Market Road out of the dark, alone, in a gray raincoat with the hood down and her hair plastered flat to her head, holding something against her chest with both arms.

She stopped at the foot of the steps.

The shield turned toward her. Slowly. The rain hissed on the iron.

She did not run. She looked up at it, and she was shaking, from cold or from something else, and she said in careful Huaxian, "I'm sorry. I don't know the right way to ask. May I come in?"

Iron Wall Zhao considered this.

Then he stood up off his stool, which was a thing he never did for anyone, and opened the bronze door for her with one rusted edge of his shield, and stood aside.

---

Yuanshu came down the marble stairs in his socks with a towel.

She was standing in the middle of the dark banking hall, dripping on the floor, under the one lamp that stayed lit over the board. She was looking up at the numbers. *1,031,877.* The four columns. The Quiet column, longer every week.

She took the towel without seeming to notice it.

"I had a speech," said Doctor Kwan. "On the plane. I practiced it for eleven hours." She pushed her wet glasses up her nose with the back of her wrist. "I don't remember any of it."

"That's all right. We have tea."

"Do you have anywhere without windows?"

He looked at her. At the thing she was holding against her chest. It was a laptop, wrapped in two plastic bags, the kind you get at a supermarket.

> **She has turned her phone off and taken the battery out. It is in her coat pocket, in pieces.**
>
> **Her heart rate is one hundred and twelve.**
>
> **She is not here as Meridian Dynamics.**

"The vault," said Yuanshu.

---

The vault room was round and low and lined with steel drawers that had once held other people's gold and now held paper copies of the Ledger, a million names in Sister Ning's careful filing. There was a table in the middle. Two chairs. Old Seven stood by the round steel door with his flame turned low.

Doctor Kwan unwrapped her laptop from its two plastic bags on the table, very carefully, the way you'd unwrap a wound.

"You need to understand what WARDEN is first," she said. "Not what it does. What it is." She opened the lid. Her hands were not quite steady. "It's a prediction engine. You feed it data about a person, and it tells you what that person will do. That's all. That's the product."

"Data from where?"

"Everywhere." She typed. "Phones. Cameras. Purchases. Medical records. Travel. Social media. And for awakeners, law signatures. We have satellites. Six of them. In orbit since eleven years ago. They were launched to watch Rifts. They have very good instruments for picking up law activity anywhere on Blue Star." She swallowed. "And they record everything. Every flare. Every surge. Every awakener using power anywhere on the planet, for eleven years. That's WARDEN's foundation. Eleven years of the whole world's law signatures. Nobody asked anybody."

The screen lit her face pale blue in the dark vault.

"Including yours," she said. "Including your first day. The Awakening Hall. The Stele. Your mother's last day at the Institute." She looked up at him. "I'm sorry. I thought you should know that first."

---

He didn't say anything for a moment.

> **That is how they predicted my aura at the Concordia office. They had a record.**
>
> **They have been watching all of us, the whole time.**

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because of what else it saw." She turned the laptop around to face him.

On the screen was a map. Lingzhou. The city laid out from above in gray and black: the river, the wall, the harbor, the long curve of the old quarter. And over it, in a thin, steady pulse, a single red dot.

It blinked once. Then stopped.

A date in the corner of the screen ticked forward one month. The dot blinked again.

Another month. Again.

"Eleven years," said Doctor Kwan quietly. "Once a month. Every month. Never missed. A law transmission from a single point in Lingzhou, very short, very tight, aimed straight up. Different from anything else we've ever recorded. WARDEN flagged it in its first year as an anomaly and nobody ever looked at the flag. It was one dot among four billion." She zoomed in. The city swelled on the screen. Streets. Rooftops. A long, low building by the river with a tall thin mast on its roof. "Until I went back through the logs, after the Concordia office, after what you said. I wanted to see what WARDEN knew about you. And I found this."

The red dot sat on top of the mast.

The Rift Research Institute.

---

The vault was so quiet that Yuanshu could hear the rain on the street above, through a meter of stone.

> **The weather mast.**
>
> **Gu installed it eleven years ago. He told Lu it was for monitoring Rift weather.**
>
> **Once a month, for eleven years.**
>
> **The same law as the threads. The same law as the transmitter in the tower block, and the scout.**

"There's more," said Doctor Kwan.

She typed again. A graph appeared. A jagged line, rising and falling, and below it another line, smoother.

"WARDEN correlates everything with everything. That's what it's for. So I asked it to correlate the transmission with every other event in Lingzhou's record." She pointed at the screen. "This line is the transmission's strength. This line is the number of people in the city who were reported missing, admitted as unexplained comas, or registered as hollows." She was quiet for a moment. "They match. Almost perfectly. Every time the transmission got stronger, a month later, more people went hollow. Over eleven years."

"It was reporting," said Yuanshu.

"It was reporting." She nodded. "Telling something up there what was here. What was ready."

---

He sat back in the old steel chair.

He looked at the red dot on the gray screen, blinking on top of a building he had walked into a hundred times. Where his mother had worked for nineteen years. Where a kind man in a gray cardigan kept a lemon tree on his windowsill and watered it on Thursdays.

"This is it," he said quietly. "This is proof."

"Yes."

"Gu installed the mast. The mast transmits on the same law as the threads. The transmissions predict the hollows. It's on a satellite record nobody could fake." He looked at her. "With this, the Bureau doesn't need to wonder about him anymore."

"Yes," said Doctor Kwan.

She didn't look happy.

"And to use it," she said, "you have to stand up in front of the Concordia Council and say: *Thank you, WARDEN. Thank you for watching the entire planet for eleven years without anyone's permission. Thank you for recording every awakener's every breath. Without your enormous, illegal, unaccountable eye in the sky, we would never have caught him.*" She took off her wet glasses and held them in her lap. "And then the Atlantic Federation stands up after you and says: *Exactly. That's what WARDEN is for. Imagine what it could do with the Ossuary too.* And they win. And the Data Act passes. Because you just proved it works."

> **She is right.**
>
> **The proof against Gu is also the best argument anyone has ever made for the Data Act.**
>
> **We cannot use one without giving them the other.**

---

Neither of them spoke for a long time.

Old Seven shifted his weight by the vault door. His spear-butt clicked softly against the steel floor. Somewhere above them, through the stone, a car went by on East Market Road with its tires hissing on the wet tar.

"Why did you bring it to me?" said Yuanshu finally. "You could have given it to Hale. You'd have been a hero at Meridian."

"I know."

"Then why?"

Doctor Kwan looked at the steel drawers around the walls. A million names. Folded into paper. Filed by hand.

"When I was nine," she said, "my grandmother died in a Rift collapse in the Atlantic Federation. They never found her. There was a database, after, of everyone who'd been in the area. Phone signals. Camera footage. They used it to identify the missing. It was a good database. It helped." She turned her glasses over in her fingers. "Three years later, an insurance company bought it. They used it to raise premiums on every family whose relatives had been in Rift zones. Including my mother's." She put the glasses back on. "Nobody asked my grandmother. Nobody asked us. The data was the data. That's how data works."

She looked at him through the clean lenses.

"You said, at the Concordia office, *It's how it works now.* I've been thinking about that sentence for forty days. I came because I'd like it to be true." She almost smiled. "And because I didn't know what else to do with this. I can't unsee it. I can't give it to Hale. And I can't give it to the Council, because I'll be handing them a loaded gun and asking them politely not to fire it."

---

Yuanshu looked at the screen for a long time. At the red dot, blinking.

Then he looked at the drawers.

"Who's in it?" he said.

Doctor Kwan blinked. "What?"

"The data. The eleven years. Every flare. Every awakener." He turned to her. "The proof that the mast transmits is made of everyone it ever watched. Everyone in Lingzhou. All the hollows. Their families. Me. My mother." He paused. "Who asked them?"

"Nobody. That's the whole..." She stopped.

"Then I'm not going to use it," said Yuanshu. "Not like that."

She stared at him.

"You can't be serious. It's the only evidence you have. He's going to be on the island. In the room. Testifying. And you have proof in your hand..."

"I know." He closed the laptop lid, very gently, the way you'd close a book someone else was reading. "And if I win by using something taken from people who never said yes, then I've already lost. Then the Charter's just a thing I believe in when it's convenient." He slid the laptop back across the table to her. "Keep it. Keep it safe. Don't give it to anyone. Not Hale, not the Council, not me."

"Then what was the point?"

"The point is I know now." He looked at the vault door. North. "I know what the mast is. I know Gu built it. I know it reported every month for eleven years. I don't need to prove that to anyone yet. I just need to know where to look." He stood up. "And there's another way to get proof. One that somebody actually agreed to give."

---

At the bronze door, at midnight, in the rain, Doctor Kwan stopped with the laptop wrapped in its supermarket bags against her chest.

"Can I ask you something?" she said. "As a scientist."

"Sure."

"There's a part of WARDEN I never understood. The core architecture. The prediction layer. Hale's people brought it to us seven years ago, finished, as a 'reference model' from a private partner, and told us to build around it." She frowned out at the rain. "It doesn't work like anything I've ever designed. It doesn't calculate what people will do. It's more like..." She searched for the word. "It's more like it *remembers* what people do. As if it had known a great many people, a long time ago, very well."

The rain hissed on Iron Wall Zhao's shield.

> **Seven years ago.**
>
> **The year your mother went down to the basement.**

"Who was the partner?" said Yuanshu.

"I never found out," said Doctor Kwan. "The invoice was paid in gold."
