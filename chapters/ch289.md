## Chapter 289: Four Thousand Seats

The Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds sat on the third morning, and it was the Awakening Hall.

He knew it the moment he walked in. Not the size; it was a hundred times larger, a bowl of black stone so vast that the far side of it was blue with distance, open at the top to the morning sky of Anthos. Not the light; it was lit from every seat, four thousand of them, each one burning in the colors of its House or its world. Not the people: Paragons in white and gold, Matriarchs in deep red, Fleet Captains in their Houses' colors, the Speakers of eight thousand five hundred and eighty-eight worlds. All of them with a face on a fine chain at their throat, cut from the Lens, three gold marks, four.

It was the shape.

Tiers, curving up into the distance. An aisle, going down. And at the bottom, at the center, on a round platform of black stone, a place to stand. The place you stood to be told what you were.

He stood on it.

---

He was not alone. He had asked who would come, the night before, in the Lens chamber of the flagship, where they had all gathered because it was the only room on the ship nobody from the twenty-nine could reach without walking past a dead captain with one arm. And everyone had said yes before he finished asking.

So they stood on the platform at the bottom of the Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds together.

Old Seven at the front, one-armed, two meters ten, in rusted armor, with his spear grounded in his one hand. Facing out. Into four thousand seats. Behind him, in a line, the one behind holding the one in front, sixty shapes of gray ash: the Third Battalion of the Eastern Line, who had come down from the flagship on a ship the size of a bus because the keeper could not lay a road out here, and had walked into the Assembly in a line, and stopped, and stood at ease. Lingyue, with Xiaodeng in the sling and her grandfather's sword on her back and a lamp in her hand, held. Tam and Seri, barefoot, holding hands. Bai Lu, with her bag, who had said she was not letting a month-old baby into a room full of Paragons without a doctor. Iselde, without a face.

And beside him, at the edge of the platform, the First of the Ladder.

Not in white with gold at the collar. Not in white at all. In a gray coat, plain, of the kind the C's wore in the arrays, which Halvarde had given him that morning without saying anything. With a cracked black disc in his pocket. Standing on the platform where he had been read at twelve, beside a boy whose stone had gone black.

The Assembly went silent when he walked in.

Seed said that had never happened. Seed said that in nine thousand years, the Assembly had never gone silent for anything.

---

The Speaker sat at the front of the lowest tier, in a seat of white stone, above the platform. Aurelle Teller. Old. Very old; older than Corvane, older than Severin, with white hair and a face like carved bone and a disc at her throat with four gold marks. And dark. Seed said so. All the faces of all the Paragons had darkened on the ninth night, when a fog-ringer sat down in the Lens, and they had not recovered. The light that moved in the stone like water had gone out of every one of them.

She looked down at the platform.

"*Dacian Severin,*" she said. With a grade. Seed carried it. "*Formerly SSS. Formerly First.*"

"*Aurelle,*" said Severin. Without one.

The Speaker of the Assembly did not move. But something in the four thousand seats did. A sound. Not a word. The sound four thousand people make when a man they have knelt to all their lives speaks to the Speaker without her grade, in the Assembly, in front of them.

"*You will answer to the Assembly,*" said Aurelle Teller. "*For the Lens. For the cores. For the Ladder.*"

"*Yes,*" said the man in the gray coat. "*I'm here to.*" He looked up at her. "*But first, I'd like to ask the Assembly something.*"

---

He did it badly.

Seed said so afterward. Not unkindly. Seed said the First of the Ladder asked the Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds a question the way a man who has never been asked anything asks his first one: too fast, and then too slowly, and with the grade creeping back into his sentences and being taken out again, one word at a time, while four thousand people watched him do it.

"*Ninety-two days ago,*" said Dacian Severin, "*I went to an orchard in the Shallows to take an instrument and restore the Ladder. The instrument was a boy. And a thing in his head. And a girl in a stone. And a fog-ringer. And a question.*"

He stopped.

"*On the ninth night, the fog-ringer asked me what I wanted. In the Lens. In front of everything the Lens has ever been cut into. You heard him. Every one of you. Your faces went dark because the root was asking, not reading.*"

Silence.

"*He asked you too,*" said the former First of the Ladder. "*All of you. What you wanted. And you answered. Alone. In your cabins and your Houses and your beds. And someone heard.*"

He looked up at four thousand seats.

"*I'd like to know what you said,*" said Dacian Severin.

---

Nobody answered.

Four thousand seats. Paragons, Matriarchs, Fleet Captains, the Speakers of eight thousand five hundred and eighty-eight worlds. Nobody said anything. The bowl of black stone was so silent he could hear the wind over the open top of it, high up, in the morning sky of Anthos.

> **They do not know how.**
>
> **They have never answered a question in the Assembly. The Assembly does not ask. It decrees. Every word ever spoken in this room has had a grade in it.**
>
> **They answered alone, on the ninth night, because nobody could hear them.**
>
> **They do not know how to answer in front of each other.**

And then, from the fourth tier, from a seat burning deep red, a woman stood up.

Sixty-one. Tall. Gray hair cut short. A long coat of deep red with gold at the collar. And at her throat, on a fine chain, a disc of black stone with three gold marks, dark.

"*Corvane Varr-Anthos,*" she said. Her voice carried. Every seat could hear it; the bowl was built so. "*Matriarch of the House of Anthos.*"

She did not give her grade.

The sound went through the four thousand seats again. Louder.

"*I would like to be asked,*" said the Matriarch. "*Here. In front of the Assembly. What I want.*" She looked down at the platform. Not at Severin. At the boy. "*I have an answer. I have been practicing it for ninety-one days.*"

---

He looked up at her.

At the tall woman in red in the fourth tier, who had come down a road of light into a courtyard on Blue Star and been asked by a woman in a wheelchair whether she had asked her daughter anything. Who had sent the same clumsy mark down a stone to a room full of children learning to wear shoes for ninety-one days without missing one. Who had told him, first, before anyone, about the Lens.

"*Matriarch,*" said Zhou Yuanshu. In the Federation's language. Without the grade. "*I'm going to ask you something. First I'm going to tell you what I'll do with your answer.*"

She waited.

"*Whatever you say, I'll say it back. Exactly. So the whole Assembly hears it twice. Once from you and once from someone who was listening.*" He held the staff. "*That's all I'll do. Nobody here has to do anything with it. You can say no. You can say ask me tomorrow.*"

The Matriarch of the House of Anthos stood in the fourth tier of the Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds and did not say anything for a long moment.

"*What do you want?*" said Yuanshu.

---

"*I want to open the arrays of the House of Anthos,*" said Corvane Varr-Anthos.

She said it steadily. Her hands were at her sides. Her voice carried to every seat.

"*All of them. Every ship. Every cell. Asked first. And the children who come out, I want them asked what they want, and if they want to go home, I want them taken home rowed, and if they don't, I want them kept in the House of Anthos as children of the House, not as cores, with the name of the House, which they have earned more than anyone born to it.*"

The sound in the four thousand seats was not a sound any more. It was something else.

"*And I want the stone on Anthos,*" said the Matriarch, "*the one in the reading-hall below the House, where every child of Anthos has been read for nine thousand years, where my daughter Maelis was read and marked E and given to the fold, to ask.*"

She did not stop.

"*Nobody is in it. I cannot go into it. I am alive.*" Her voice did not change. "*So until someone can, until someone chooses to, I want to stand beside it. On every reading morning. Every child. Before the stone reads them, I want to ask them myself what they want to become. And if they say they don't want to touch it, I want to be the one who says: then don't.*"

She looked down at the platform.

"*I said yes for my daughter once,*" said Corvane Varr-Anthos. "*Without asking. On the morning. In the box. I will spend the rest of my life asking other people's.*"

She sat down.

---

He said it back.

All of it. Exactly. The way he had told her he would. In the Federation's language, without the grade, standing on the platform at the bottom of the Assembly, so that every seat heard it twice. Once from her. Once from someone who had been listening.

When he finished, nobody moved.

And then, from the ninth tier, from a seat burning iron gray, a man stood up.

A Fleet Captain. Forty, perhaps. In the gray of the House of Iron. With a disc at his throat with two gold marks, dark.

"*Haskel Iron,*" he said. Without his grade. His voice was shaking. "*Fleet Captain. The Iron Tower.*" He swallowed. "*My brother is in the third array of my ship. I put him there. He was an F. I was an S. I signed the order.*" He looked down at the platform. "*On the ninth night, the fog-ringer asked me what I wanted, and I said: to see my brother. And nobody heard it but me and him.*"

He stood in the ninth tier with his hands shaking.

"*I'd like it heard,*" said Haskel Iron. "*Twice.*"

---

They came for nine hours.

Not all of them. Not most. Of four thousand seats, Seed counted three hundred and eleven who stood up. One at a time, in the Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds, in front of each other, without their grades, and said what they had said on the ninth night when a fog-ringer in a stone asked them and only they had heard.

*To see my brother.*

*I don't know. I have never not known anything. I would like it heard that I don't know.*

*To stop.*

*To have been asked. When I was twelve.*

*To be first.*

That one, too. Eleven times. Seed counted. Eleven Paragons and Fleet Captains of the twenty-nine Houses stood up in the Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds and said, in front of everyone, without their grades, steadily, that what they wanted was to be first. And he said it back to each of them. Exactly. So that it was heard twice.

> **The Collector said that was the easiest want it ever kept.**
>
> **It is very hard to say it out loud in a room. I can see their faces.**
>
> **I think it is the first time any of them have heard it.**

And every time, at the end, he said the same thing. To each one. *Thank you. Nobody here has to do anything with it.*

---

At the end of the ninth hour, the Speaker of the Assembly stood up.

Aurelle Teller. In white, with four gold marks on a dark disc at her throat, from the seat of white stone at the front of the lowest tier. Old. Very old. She had not moved for nine hours. She had sat in her seat above the platform with her hands folded and watched three hundred and eleven people stand up one at a time and say what they wanted without their grades, and a boy from an orchard in the Shallows say it back to each of them.

She looked down at him.

"*You have turned my Assembly into your stone,*" she said.

With a grade. Seed carried it. Then, slowly, deliberately, the way a woman of great age takes off a ring she has worn so long that her finger has grown round it, she said it again.

Without.

"*You have turned my Assembly into your stone.*"

"*No,*" said Yuanshu. "*It was always a stone. Somebody just came in and sat down.*"

The Speaker looked at him for a long time.

"*The twenty-nine Houses ask the Assembly to break the Lens,*" said Aurelle Teller. "*And restore the Ladder. And choose a new First.*" She did not look away from him. "*The Assembly will decide tomorrow.*"

She paused.

"*The Speaker would like to know,*" she said, "*what the ungraded one wants. Before the Assembly decides.*"

---

He stood on the platform at the bottom of the bowl of black stone, under four thousand seats, with the staff in his hand.

He thought about the north wall on the ninth night over Blue Star, when a fog-ringer in a stone had asked eight billion people at once what they wanted, and he had stood in his old place and not known, for the first time since he was eighteen. And the step of the groundskeeper's hut at the edge of the War Memorial Cemetery, at dusk, when Seed had asked him, and he had known. Two wants.

*To go and ask them.*

*And to come home.*

He had done the first. He was doing it.

"*I want to send a mark,*" said Zhou Yuanshu.

The Speaker's face did not change.

"*Through the Lens. Tomorrow. Before the Assembly decides,*" he said. "*To every face in the Federation. Every disc on every chain round every throat that was cut from the root.*" He looked up at four thousand seats, at four thousand dark discs on fine chains. "*Not a question. A mark. The kind the stones used to send. Except this one would say something true.*"

"*What would it say?*"

"*I don't know yet,*" said Yuanshu. "*My sister's writing it.*"

---

> **I am sending it now.**
>
> **To Xiaoyu. Through the Lens. Ichiro is tying it. He says it will be clumsy because he is excited.**
>
> **Down the net. Through the knot in the deep water. Down Wen Jia's thread. Into our stone.**
>
> **It says:**
>
> **THE ASSEMBLY SITS TOMORROW. EVERY FACE IN THE FEDERATION WAS CUT FROM THE LENS. ICHIRO CAN SEND A MARK TO ALL OF THEM AT ONCE.**
>
> **WHAT SHOULD IT SAY? IT HAS TO BE TRUE. YOURS ARE.**
>
> **— Y.**

And from very far away, on a world in the Shallows, in the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road, at two in the morning Lingzhou time, a girl of seventeen, nearly eighteen, who had just asked the Collector what it wanted and been told *tomorrow*, watched letters form in the air over a warm black stone in a hand that was not the stone's, and read them. And sat down on the floor of the platform in front of it, beside the body of a nun holding a lamp, with a marker in her hand, uncapped.

And did not write anything for a long time.
