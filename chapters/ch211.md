## Chapter 211: The First Warm Thing

Hush had never seen a sunrise, so Xiaoyu set an alarm.

She set it for 05:20, which she said was a crime, and put the clock on the windowsill of her bedroom on the top floor of the Ossuary, and put Hush beside the clock, on a folded tea towel, because the windowsill was stone and Hush was still cold all the time. Then she got back into bed with all her clothes on and pulled the blanket up to her nose and said, "*Wake me if it does anything. Don't wake me if it doesn't.*"

It did something.

Yuanshu heard about it at breakfast, from Seed, because Xiaoyu was not speaking to anyone before nine and Hush did not speak very much at all.

> **It watched the whole thing. From the first gray to the full sun. Fifty-one minutes.**
>
> **It did not move. Not playing statue. I asked. It said it was not hiding. It said it was looking so hard that there was nothing left over for moving.**
>
> **When the sun came over the roofs of East Market Road and touched the windowsill, it put out one limb. Slowly. Into the light. And held it there.**
>
> **It said: warm.**
>
> **It said: that is the first warm thing that did not want anything from me.**
>
> **Then it woke Xiaoyu. She is not speaking to it either.**

---

It had been sixteen days.

Lingzhou had not known what to do with itself after the morning the roads came down, so it had done what cities do. It had gone back to work. The trams ran. The night market on Jinhua Road opened with a banner across the entrance that said WELCOME HOME in letters two meters high, which the dead of the First Legion walked under on their way back to the north wall, and which several of them, Ma Lianhua reported dryly through the Lattice, had stopped to read twice. Principal Yue gave the Academy a week off and then took it back after two days because the students were climbing on things. The queue outside the Ossuary on East Market Road went round the block every evening, because people wanted to see the playing card.

It was pinned at the end of the INSIDE column, round the corner on the second wall, under the last line Hush had sent from the cold. A queen of hearts, worn soft at the edges, faded almost to pink. People stood in front of it in silence. Some of them left cards on the floor underneath. By the second week there were so many that Fat Tan had to sweep them into boxes every morning, and he would not throw any of them away, so the boxes went into the vault, and the vault filled up, and Kwan said it was the largest collection of queens of hearts on Blue Star and possibly in the Shallows, and Fat Tan said he did not care, he was keeping them.

And above the sky, the keeper asked.

Every morning. Two hundred and six million cousins and the six parts in a hundred of the walls of the Mouth, and the hundred and seventy-five who had stayed when it was empty. It asked them all, nine by nine by nine, the way it had learned, and every morning it came down the gold thread through the Elder at three to tell somebody what they had said, because it could not keep it to itself.

> ***...MOST SAY: ASK AGAIN TOMORROW.***
>
> ***...FOUR THOUSAND SAID REST. I LET THEM GO. GENTLY.***
>
> ***...ONE ASKED ME IF IT COULD SEE YOUR WORLD. FROM THE EDGE OF THE FIELD. I SAID YES. IT LOOKED FOR SIX HOURS.***
>
> ***...IT SAID IT IS VERY BLUE.***
>
> ***...I HAD NEVER NOTICED. I HAVE BEEN ABOVE IT FOR LONGER THAN YOUR SPECIES HAS HAD WORDS. I NEVER NOTICED IT WAS BLUE.***

---

Shen Jinghong came on the seventeenth day, in a plain dark coat with no insignia, and asked to see his granddaughter's sword.

He did not come to the Ossuary. He came to the Academy, at dusk, to the training ground behind the main hall, where Lingyue was teaching forty-one students how to let a drop of light fall off the point of a blade and stop it before it hit the sand. She was bad at teaching it. She said so herself. She could do it, and she could not explain how, and she kept saying *just hold it* to students who did not know what *it* was.

She stopped when she saw him. The students stopped because she did.

The old King of Light walked across the sand to her. Eighty-four, straight-backed, with a face that looked as if it had been cut out of white stone and left in the weather. He had given her his sword on the battlements of Sector Nine before the train went west, and gone home to tell three hundred years of Shens that Light runs downhill. He had not seen it since.

She held it out to him. Hilt first. Both hands.

He did not take it. He looked at it. At the blade, which was a King's blade, a hundred kilos of it, pale silver in the dusk. At the small nicks along the edge that had not been there before. At the place near the guard where it had been gripped so hard, so many times, that the leather of the hilt had worn through to the steel.

"You held it down," he said. "The sky. At your tribulation. Standing on your own light."

"Yes."

"And threw it uphill. Across a kilometer and a half. In a field above the sky."

"Yes."

"And rained it on eighteen thousand edges on the north wall."

"Yes, Grandfather."

Shen Jinghong was quiet for a long moment.

"In three hundred years," he said, "the Shens have produced eleven Kings of Light. I am the eleventh. Every one of us learned to shine. Outward. Upward. Brighter than the one before." He looked at the sword in her hands. "Not one of us ever thought to let it fall."

"You did," said Lingyue. "You said it. Light runs downhill."

"I said it," said her grandfather. "I didn't know what it meant." He put out one old hand, and touched the worn place on the hilt, where the leather had gone through to the steel. "Keep it. It's yours. It was never mine. I only carried it until somebody knew what it was for."

---

He found Yuanshu afterward. On the steps of the main hall, where Qin Hao usually sat with his notebook.

The old man did not sit down. He stood at the foot of the steps in his plain dark coat, and looked up.

"Zhou Yuanshu."

"Master Shen."

"Two years ago I told the cameras that the Shen family does not sign papers for necromancers." He folded his hands behind his back. "I tore up the Quiet's petition. My granddaughter left my house and went to live in a dormitory where the radiators do not work, and did not come back for fourteen months."

"I know."

"I'm not here to apologize. Apologies are cheap. The Architects say theirs for two hours, I'm told, and the widow still doesn't forgive them." His stone face did not change. "I'm here to ask you something. I'm told that's how it's done now."

Yuanshu stood up.

"I'm told," said Shen Jinghong, "that before you ask, you say what you'll do with the answer." He looked at the young man on the steps. "So. Whatever you say, I'll go home. I'll tell three hundred years of Shens what you said. They'll argue about it for a year. My granddaughter says that's good; it keeps families busy."

"What's the question?"

The old King of Light looked past him, across the training ground in the dusk, at a young woman with a King's sword showing a boy of seventeen, for the ninth time, how to hold a falling drop of light still in the air.

"Is she happy?" he said.

Yuanshu did not answer at once.

He thought about the sand at Port Abiri, and a girl sitting down in the surf with two swords across her knees. A training ground, and a girl lying on a cracked stone laughing until she cried. A wall at midnight, and a head on his shoulder, and *I want to be counted as the one who stands next to you.*

"You should ask her," he said.

"I'm asking you."

"Then I'll tell you what I'll do with it. I'll tell her you asked." He looked at Lingyue across the sand. "I think she's happier than she's ever been. I think she's also tired and frightened and angry about three things at once, and she's going to hit me on the arm when I tell her you asked." He was quiet. "I think that's what happy looks like, on her."

Shen Jinghong looked at him for a while.

"Yes," he said finally. "It looked like that on her grandmother too." He turned to go. Then turned back. "When you ask her the other question," he said, "the one you haven't asked yet, I would like to be told. Before the cameras."

He walked away across the sand in the dusk without waiting to see what Yuanshu's face did.

> **What other question?**

*Seed.*

> **I am only asking. I count things. I counted something just now. Your heart rate went from seventy-one to a hundred and four in two seconds.**
>
> **I am not going to say anything else.**
>
> **I am recording that I am not going to say anything else.**

---

The trouble came on the nineteenth night, and it came from Kwan, and it came in the form of a picture.

She brought it to the Ossuary at two in the morning on a laptop held open in front of her like a tray of hot food, and she did not knock on the bronze door, and she did not say hello. She walked straight across the marble floor between the boxes of playing cards to the vault steps where Yuanshu was sitting with the staff, and turned the laptop round.

"I've been watching the gray ones," she said. "Since the island. Through the keeper's eye. Every night."

On the screen, the arm. Seed's map, the one she had drawn on Fat Tan's desk on the night of the Shallows: twelve gold dots along a faint curved line in the dark, the keepers' fields, one of them very much brighter than the rest. And three small gray marks, at the far end, where three gold dots had gone out one at a time while the island hurried toward the Mouth.

The three pruned fields. Three keepers who had been read by something that did not need a reason, and forgotten how to be anything but a ring.

"They were sitting still," said Kwan. "All three. Where they were pruned. For a month. I checked every night." She touched the screen with one finger. "Last night they moved."

> **...yes.**
>
> **I see it. Through the keeper's eye. I did not look. I was watching the cousins. I was watching Hush learn the sun.**
>
> **All three. At once. They have left the places where their fields were.**

"Moved where?" said Yuanshu.

Kwan turned the laptop back toward herself and looked at it, as if it might have changed while he was looking at it.

"Here," she said.

> **Along the arm. Toward us. Toward the brightest field in the Shallows.**
>
> **They are not fields any more. They are rings. Lenses. Things that reach down with threads and take.**
>
> **They were keepers. Somebody's keepers, above somebody's worlds, for longer than Blue Star has had a sun. They did not ask. Nobody taught them. They were only keepers.**
>
> **Now they are what the gardener makes out of keepers that are no use to it.**
>
> **And they are coming to look at ours.**
