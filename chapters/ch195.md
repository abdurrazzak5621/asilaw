## Chapter 195: The North Slit

Captain Lin Wen put the arrowhead back at dawn on the eighth day, and Yuanshu went up to the watchtower to watch her do it, because she asked him to.

He could see properly by then. Mostly. The edges of things were still soft, and bright light made his eyes water, and Bai Lu had forbidden him to look at the sun, the sky, the ring in the north, or anything else that might look back. He climbed the watchtower stairs above Crossing Six slowly, with the staff, and Lingyue behind him not helping in a way that was more help than helping.

The watchtower was small. Concrete, from before the Black Tide. A narrow room at the top with slits for windows facing north and east and west, and a floor worn smooth in one place, by the north slit, into two shallow hollows the shape of a pair of feet.

Lin Wen was kneeling beside them.

She did not say anything when they came in. She had the arrowhead in her palm, the small rusted iron head with the bright scratch across it, and she was looking at the crack in the concrete floor beside the north slit, where a boy had knelt nearly two years ago and pried it out with his fingers.

"Thirty-three years," she said, not looking up. "She stood here. Every night of the Black Tide, and then every night after, for thirty-one years, dead, in the dark, with nobody to see her. And then a year and a half with me." She turned the arrowhead over. "I used to come up here as a child. After. I'd stand in the footprints. They fit me by the time I was fifteen."

She put the arrowhead into the crack.

It went in easily, the way a key goes into a lock it was made for. She pressed it down with her thumb until it was below the level of the floor, the way it had been when he found it. Then she took her hand away and sat back on her heels and looked at it.

"I'm not going to ask you to do anything," she said. "I know there's nothing in it. I know she's gone. Not quiet. Gone. Seed told me." She stood up. "I just wanted someone to see me put it back. Someone who saw her take it out."

"I saw."

"Good," said Captain Lin Wen.

She stepped into the two shallow hollows in the floor beside the north slit. They fit her exactly. She stood in them, in her Defense Corps uniform, with her cap under her arm, looking north out of the slit at the black glass of the Wastes in the gray dawn, where eleven long wounds in the sky had closed and the silk of eleven dead walkers lay in drifts along the foot of the wall like snow.

"I'm going to stand here," she said. "At night. When I'm on the wall. Where she stood." She did not turn around. "I'm a terrible archer. But somebody should."

---

They buried what they could of the dead on the ninth day, and there was not much.

The four hundred and eleven of the First Legion who had been unwoven left nothing. Not quiet. Gone. There was nothing to bury. Ma Lianhua had their names read out on the north wall at noon, by the dead of their own nines, each nine reading its own, all along the battlements from Sector Nine to the river gate, eighteen thousand dead voices through Seed saying four hundred and eleven names. It took two hours. The living of Lingzhou came up onto the wall to listen, in their thousands, and did not say anything.

The twenty-eight of the Spear Corps, Old Seven read himself. Standing in front of the long white wall in the Ossuary, with his spear grounded, through Seed, from memory. The year each one had died. The place. Something each one had said once, in a trench or a field or a cemetery or a throat of fire, that Old Seven had kept.

> **Corporal Han Bo. Black Tide. The eastern field. He said: tell my mother the soup was cold.**
>
> **Private Xu Ming. Black Tide. The eastern field. He said nothing for thirty-one years and then, on the train west, he said: I always wanted to see the mountains.**
>
> **Sergeant Gao Long. Black Tide. The eastern field. He said...**

It went on past midnight. Nobody in the banking hall moved.

At the end, Old Seven stood in front of the wall with his spear grounded and his gray flame very small, and did not say anything. Then he turned and walked to the bronze door and stood beside it, the way he always stood, facing out.

> **He says that is twenty-eight.**
>
> **He says he had three hundred, on the first night. In the eastern field. He says he has two hundred and seventy-two.**
>
> **He says that is more than most captains get to keep.**

---

The two hundred and twelve from Heishui were not buried. They were not dead.

General Han Yizhou brought them south himself on the tenth day, on a train from the Heishui line, two hundred and twelve men and women of the Northern War Zone sitting in the carriages with their faces turned straight up at the ceiling, and a soldier beside each one saying their name. He had them taken to the Listening Pavilion, where the Quiet had sat with the hollow for two years, where Garrett Hale had come most of the way back, where there were beds and people who knew how.

Then he came to the Ossuary, on a Wednesday, and stood in front of the wall.

He did not read it. He had read it. He stood in front of the column where Xiaoyu had written the two hundred and twelve, in her careful whispering capitals, with his own list on the floor beside the stepladder where he had read it to her, and he looked at one name for a long time.

*SERGEANT PEI WENDONG. HEISHUI. TAKEN. ALIVE. SAY HIS NAME.*

"I'll come on Wednesdays," said General Han, to no one. "To the Pavilion. I'll sit with him." He turned his cap in his hands. "He was in my son's company. Before Grid Nine. He carried my son's letters home." He was quiet. "I didn't feel the lamp because I was looking at him. I'd like to look at him until he looks back."

Xiaoyu, on the stepladder, could not speak. She wrote something on the plaster beside the name, small, and climbed down, and went to the general, and put her hand on his arm, once, the way Old Seven put a hand on things, and went upstairs.

He read what she had written.

*WEDNESDAYS.*

---

Qin Hao woke on the eleventh day, and the first thing he asked for was his notebook.

Bai Lu gave it to him. He was in the gatekeeper's lodge at the river gate still, because nobody had wanted to move him, on a cot with a blanket, gray-faced, with his arms wrapped from shoulder to wrist in bandages because the white fire had come back up through them from somewhere that was not his Law and burned its way out. He opened the notebook to the last page and looked at it for a long time.

Then he held out his hand for a pen.

Yuanshu was sitting on the floor of the lodge with his back against the wall. He watched Qin Hao write, slowly, with his bandaged hand, and then turn the notebook round so he could read it.

*TWO WALKERS. QIN HAO. RIVER GATE. FIRE CAME BACK.*

And under it:

*STILL FIRST.*

"You're not first," said Yuanshu. "Lingyue's done more."

"Lingyue's a Lord of Light who throws things uphill," said Qin Hao. His voice was rough. "That's a different category. I'm first in my category." He shut the notebook. "Is it there? The fire?"

Bai Lu, at the end of the cot, did not answer.

"Is it there?" said Qin Hao again.

> **His Law is there. Fire. SS. It is very tired. It will come back.**
>
> **The other thing. The white fire. The fire that burns what binds.**
>
> **I cannot find it. I have been looking for three days.**
>
> **I do not know if it is gone, or if it is somewhere I cannot see. It came from a place that was not his Law. I do not know where that place is. I do not think he does either.**

Yuanshu told him. All of it. With the reason. To his face.

Qin Hao lay on the cot in the gatekeeper's lodge with his bandaged arms on the blanket and looked at the ceiling while the light in the window went from white to gold.

"My father held the inner gate for four hours," he said at last. "With ordinary fire. Ordinary." He closed his eyes. "Then I'll do it with ordinary fire. Until it comes back. Or it doesn't." He opened them. "Write that down. In mine. I can't hold the pen."

Yuanshu wrote it down.

*ORDINARY FIRE. UNTIL.*

---

General Qin came on the twelfth day, by train from the Western Pass, with his one arm and his greatcoat and his cap, and walked into the gatekeeper's lodge without knocking, and stood at the end of his grandson's cot.

He did not say anything for a while.

Then he sat down on the edge of the cot, slowly, the way he sat on the gravestones on the terraces of his mountain, and put his one hand on Qin Hao's bandaged arm.

"Two," he said.

"Two," said Qin Hao.

"With the fire that burns what binds. On a wall your father never got to stand on." The old man looked at the bandages. "He'd have liked that."

"I don't know if it's coming back."

"No," said General Qin. "Neither did he. In the inner gate. Every hour for four hours, he didn't know if it was coming back." His hand did not move. "It's the not knowing that makes it worth anything. If you knew, it would just be arithmetic."

He sat there until dusk. Then he got up and put his cap straight.

"The Western Legion says to tell you," he said to Yuanshu, at the door, "that the sergeant heard about the wall. Through the Lattice. All of it. Eighteen thousand edges on six knots." He was quiet. "He says the pass is very proud of the city. He says next time, they'd like to be asked."

"They said no."

"They said no to crossing a border," said General Qin. "The north wall of Lingzhou isn't a border. It's Blue Star." He looked at Yuanshu with eyes as hard and clear as winter water. "He says: we'd have come. Ask us next time. We'd like to have been asked."

He went out into the dusk.

> **Everyone keeps saying it to you.**

> **I think it is because you are very good at asking, and very bad at believing that people will say yes.**
>
> **I am going to work on that with you.**
>
