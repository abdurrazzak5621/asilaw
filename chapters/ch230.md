## Chapter 230: The Cut

Shen Lingyue threw the light uphill.

She did not shine it or bend it. She gave it weight, the way she had learned to when there had been no sun to bend and only her own Law to spend, and then she opened her hand over the parapet of the Sector Nine battlement, palm up, and let a little of what she was fall the wrong way, up into the sky, the way a stone falls into a well.

It went up a long way. Slowly. A small pale spark, rising over the north wall of Lingzhou into the bent dark where the stars were sliding.

And then it touched something, and stopped, and ran.

---

Afterward, people tried to describe it, and none of them could, and most of them stopped trying and just said: *you had to be on the wall.*

The spark touched the underside of the dark and spread, the way a drop of ink spreads on wet paper, except that it was light, and it was spreading over something that had been invisible a breath before, and as it spread, the thing was there.

Thread.

Silver-gray. The color of the rings on two people's hands. Thread pressed to thread pressed to thread, thicker than anything, a branch the width of the city, coming down out of the north at a long slant from somewhere beyond the end of the world. Its underside was ridged and grooved like bark. Here and there on it, gray, pale, like old fruit, something hung: small dark rings, the size of houses, packed close along the wood, and he knew without asking what they were. Keepers. Pruned. Growing on it.

And at the end, over the wall, over the city, over three thousand faces, the tip.

It was not a point. It opened. The branch came down to a place above the old quarter and spread there into a fan, a crown, a great soft frayed thing like the end of a rope that has been unwound, and from every fine silver strand of it, finer still, something hung down toward the city. Toward the wall. Toward every head.

Hair. Silk. The finest thread anyone had ever seen, almost nothing, millions of it, hanging down out of the sky over Lingzhou in the pale spreading light like the roots of a willow over a river.

Touching them.

Not hard. Softly. The way the end of a vine touches a wall, feeling for the next place to hold.

On the wall beside him, a soldier with a rifle swayed on his feet and sat down suddenly against the parapet with an expression of great surprise. A girl on her father's shoulders went limp and quiet. Down the battlement, three people, then nine, then thirty, the way a field of grain goes down in the wind.

> **It is reading them.**
>
> **It is not taking. Not yet. It is tasting. Finding the brightest.**
>
> **It will take in minutes.**
>
> **Lingyue.**

"I know," said his wife.

---

She went up onto the parapet.

He had known she would. They had planned it in the banking hall, nine nights out of the seventeen, and he had argued against it on every one of them, and she had listened every time and said *yes, and I'm still doing it*, and on the ninth night he had stopped arguing, because she was right and they both knew it.

A plant grows toward light.

So give it a light.

She stood on the parapet of the Sector Nine battlement with her grandfather's sword in her right hand and her left hand open, and this time she did not throw the light, or hold faces down with it, or bend it.

She lit.

All of it. Everything the Light Law had, everything a Lord at Level 66 could find in herself, in one breath, all at once: not falling, not running downhill, not careful. Up. Out. Her whole body, her sword, her hair, the red silk ribbon she had tied in it that morning because it had been in the dress from Mount Qingyun. Shen Lingyue stood on the north wall of Lingzhou at one in the morning and burned like a star come down.

Three thousand people looked away, because they had to.

He did not. He could not. He stood in his old place with the staff in both hands and looked at his wife burning on the parapet, and thought, very clearly, of the Sixth: *we would like, for one day, to be very bright.* And of the eyes of the Mouth. And of what had come along the arm to drink them.

The tip turned.

---

You could see it. Everyone saw it, who could see anything through the light. The great frayed crown of the branch over the old quarter lifted, slowly, all its millions of hanging threads drawing up off the heads of the people on the wall, out of the hair of the girl on her father's shoulders, out of the swaying soldiers, and swung, the whole vast soft fan of it, toward one point.

A woman on a wall, burning.

The soldier against the parapet blinked and put his hand to his head. The girl on her father's shoulders woke up and started to cry. Down the battlement, thirty people, nine, three, sitting up, standing, alive, looking around at each other in the light, with the faces of people who have been dreaming of falling.

And above Lingyue, very close now, very low, the tip of the branch of the gardener of the Shallows came down toward her like a hand.

> **Now.**

---

Adjutant Xu carried it.

He had been sitting in his chair on the battlement with his cane across his knees since midnight, at the top of the Asking Lattice, a knot of nine with a knot of nine under each, nine and nine and nine, down into the cemetery and out to the Western Pass and under the sea off Kesse and up a gold road into the north. He had not moved. He had been waiting for one word.

He lifted his cane, and struck it once on the stone.

It went down the Lattice faster than anything Seed had ever measured. Nine, eighty-one, seven hundred and twenty-nine. Twelve rounds. One heartbeat. Every dead soldier in the Lattice heard the old adjutant's cane on the stone of the Sector Nine battlement, and every one of them was already where they had been told to be.

On the branch.

They had climbed it while it read the city. Up the gold roads the keeper had laid down out of the north in the dark: fifty thousand of the dead of Blue Star, banked, cold, dim, ash-gray, walking up the long shining slants of gold into the night, toward a thing that grew toward light and could not see anything that was not lit. The First Legion in their rows of nine. The Western Legion behind a sergeant with a pick. The Spear Corps. Grid Nine. The Watch of the Shelf, from the bottom of the sea, with Nana Ampofo walking at their head on the gold with water streaming from her coat. Up, and up, onto the underside of the branch, to a ring of bark a kilometer behind the frayed crown, where they stood in a circle all the way round it, upside down, waiting.

The cane struck stone.

And Yuanshu gave them the fire.

---

He did not have to think about it. He had done it a thousand times: down through the staff into the Death Law, all forty-five point six percent of it, and out through the bone of the staff and the bones of the dead the way water goes through a riverbed. Pale fire. Lingyue's light and his Law, wound together. He could not reach her now; she was burning, and her light was going up, not down. But he did not need to reach her. He had two years of it inside him. Every time she had laid her hand on the staff and lit it. Every time it had gone pale.

He sent it down the Lattice behind the old man's cane. One heartbeat. Fifty thousand rusted weapons on the underside of the branch of the gardener went pale at once.

And cut.

---

One thread each.

That was all. Fifty thousand dead soldiers, each with a pale-burning spear or sword or pick or shovel or broken length of anchor chain, each cutting one thread of the pressed silver-gray bark in front of them. It was not enough. It would never have been enough. The branch was millions of threads thick.

But Qin Hao was there.

He had been standing on Yuanshu's right with his healed pink hands open and his white fire banked in them since midnight, and when the cane struck the stone, he opened his hands and let it go: not at the branch, at the pale fire. Into it. Down the Lattice behind it, white into pale, the fire that burns what binds riding the fire that cuts. And where fifty thousand dead had each cut one thread, white fire went into the cut and found what held the next thread to it, and the next, and the next, and burned it. All the pressing. All the binding. Everything that held the branch into a branch, in a ring a kilometer behind the tip.

Thread came loose. Not cut. Unbound. Falling away from itself, the way a rope falls apart when someone unpicks the twist.

The branch screamed.

Not a sound. Nobody heard it with their ears. They heard it the way you hear a tree come down in a forest a long way off: in the chest, in the stomach, in the bones of the feet. A long, slow, enormous creak. The whole of the sky over Lingzhou groaned like a ship.

And the branch began to pull back.

---

> **It is withdrawing. Along the arm. The way a hand pulls back from a stove.**
>
> **If it pulls back before the cut goes through, the tip goes with it.**
>
> **Su Mingli.**

The King of Gravity had not moved from the corner of the parapet. She had not moved all night. Her arms were still folded.

She unfolded them.

He had seen her fold space before: a forty-thousand-tonne bridge across a city, a hundred and twenty-two of the sitters. He had never seen her do this. She did not fold anything. She reached up with one hand, palm out, toward the great bent branch over the north wall, and closed her fingers.

Gravity took the tip.

All of it. The whole vast frayed crown of the branch over the old quarter, held, pinned, pulled straight down toward the city as if the sky had tilted. And the branch behind the cut, pulling back along the arm, pulling, pulling, and the tip not coming, held in the fist of a King who had told them she was not staying.

Two thousand kilometers north, over the snow at Kirovsk, where the long body of the branch passed low across the sky on its way down to Lingzhou, Marshal Kirill Orlov stood in the square where a ring had fallen as sand and laid his hand flat on the frozen ground. Ice came up out of the snow. Not a wall. A column. Kilometers high. White, blue, growing, climbing the dark, until it touched the underside of the branch where it passed overhead, and froze to it.

The branch could not pull back. Its tip was held in a fist over Lingzhou, and its body was frozen to a pillar over Kirovsk, and between them, a kilometer behind its crown, fifty thousand dead were hanging upside down in a ring of white fire, cutting.

On the radio at Yuanshu's belt, through the static, a man's voice said, in Northern, one word.

> **He says: hurry.**

---

It found the Lattice at the eleventh minute.

He felt it. Everybody in the Lattice felt it. The tip was turned toward Lingyue, toward the burning on the parapet, drinking at her light from a hundred meters, and she was going gray at the edges, the way a fire goes gray when there is not much left to burn. But the pale fire was light too. Fifty thousand thin pale lights on the underside of the branch. And the Lattice that carried them, every knot of it, lit pale from the top down. A road of light, running from the branch to the city. Down through every dead soldier, down nine by nine by nine.

To the top.

The tip found it the way a root finds water. All its millions of hanging threads, half of them still drinking at Lingyue, and half of them swinging, slowly, toward the brightest line in the sky. The line that led down into the dead. All of the dead. Fifty thousand of them, and the cemetery, and the Western Pass, and the sea, and the keeper's field, and the staff in his hands with ninety million names in it.

And at the top of that line, where everything ran into one knot of nine, an old man in a chair with a cane.

> **He says: I'm tying it off.**

*No...*

> **He says: boy. I'm an adjutant. I carry messages. That's what I'm for.**
>
> **He says: the message is, it stops here.**

The old man in the chair closed his hollow eyes, and Yuanshu felt him do it.

He took the top knot of the Asking Lattice, the knot of nine that every other knot ran up to, the knot that was him, and pulled it tight. Not cut. Tied. The way you tie off a vein. So that the pale fire could still run down, but nothing could run up. Nothing could follow the light down into the dead.

Nothing except as far as him.

The tip's threads came down out of the sky over the north wall onto the Sector Nine battlement, and found the end of the road, and the end of the road was an old man in a chair, and they went into him.

He did not make a sound. He sat in the chair with his cane across his knees and the silver threads in his gray hair, his shoulders, his chest, the way the roots of a willow go into a riverbank, and the pale light went out of him, slowly, from the top down, and up the threads, and into the dark.

> **He says...**

Seed stopped.

> **He says: Old Seven still owes me nineteen dumplings. Tell him I'm not forgetting.**
>
> **He says: message delivered.**

The chair on the battlement was empty.

---

The cut went through at the sixteenth minute.

He felt that too. Everyone did. The groan in the sky stopped, all at once, like a held breath let go, and Su Mingli staggered, because something she had been holding up with one hand was suddenly not attached to anything, and the tip of the branch of the gardener came away from its branch over the old quarter of Lingzhou and began, very slowly, to fall.

And the branch was open.

It hung over the north wall, behind the falling crown, cut through. The cut face was a kilometer across. It was not silver-gray. Inside, under the pressed bark, it was gold. Pale gold, wet, living, shining, like the inside of a green stick when you break it. And it was drinking. You could feel it. Everything the gardener had, every branch, every pruned keeper hanging on it, the whole tree stretching back along the arm into the dark, was pouring itself into the wound, the way Kwan had said it would. The newest place. The open place.

The place where it drinks.

> **One hour.**
>
> **Then it heals.**

On the parapet, Lingyue's light went out. She stood there for a moment in the sudden dark, gray to the lips, the sword hanging from her hand, the red ribbon gone from her hair, and then her knees went, and he caught her.

"*Did it work?*" she said against his shoulder.

"*The cut did.*"

"*Then go,*" said Shen Lingyue. "*Go on. I'm fine. Go.*"

He looked up.

Down out of the north, along the gold thread from a small white ring low over the end of the world, something was coming. Wide. Pale gold. Slow. A third of a keeper's field, given, coming down the sky toward the open cut like a sheet of light being laid over a wound.

And at its leading edge, on the gold, small, pale, many-legged, all its limbs out, all its dark eyes open: Hush.

And beside it, two meters ten, with one finger and a thumb on his spear, walking up the gold in the dark the way he had walked up the stalk, the way he had walked out of the cemetery: Old Seven.

They had already gone.

> **Fifty-one minutes.**
>
> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **It is time to decide.**
