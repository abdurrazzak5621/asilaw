## Chapter 293: The Reading Morning

The reading-hall below the House of Anthos was older than the Assembly, and smaller, and it had been built so that the children could not see the door.

He understood that when he walked in. A round room of black stone, cut down into the rock under the House, with a domed ceiling and no windows, and a single stair coming down into it from above, curving, so that by the time you reached the bottom you could not see where you had come in. Tiers of stone benches round the walls, for the families. A box above them, carved, gilded, for the House. And in the center, on a round platform of black stone, driven point-down into the floor, ten meters high: a stone.

Black. Warm. Its runes turned down.

And beside it, with her hand flat on it, in deep red, a woman of sixty-one who had been standing there since dawn.

---

It was the first reading morning since the Assembly.

Every year, on the first day of the cold season, the children of Anthos who had turned twelve came down the curving stair into the reading-hall below the House, one at a time, in white, and put their hands on the stone, and were read, and marked, and given a face cut from the stone on a fine chain, while their families watched from the benches and the House watched from the box. Nine thousand years. Corvane had come down that stair. So had Iselde. So had Maelis, at twelve, and been marked E, and given to the fold.

Today, four hundred and six children of Anthos were on the stair.

And the families were on the benches, as they always were. And the box above the benches, carved and gilded, for the House, was empty.

Corvane Varr-Anthos had locked it. She said so, when he came down the stair with Lingyue and the baby and Old Seven one-armed behind him, and Iselde, and Maelis, holding her sister's hand. She said that anyone who wanted to watch the children from above could watch from the roof. She said it in the Federation's language, without the grade, and did not take her hand off the stone.

"*He's quiet,*" she said. Looking at the stone. "*He's been quiet since dawn. I keep asking him if he's ready.*"

> **He says: I am ready. I have been ready since dawn. She keeps asking.**
>
> **He says: tell her to stop asking and let them come down.**

"*He says you can let them come down,*" said Yuanshu.

The Matriarch of the House of Anthos looked at the stone for a long moment.

"*He would,*" she said. "*He was never patient.*" And then, to the stone, quietly, the way you would say it to someone standing next to you: "*I'm going to ask them first. Before you. That's what I said in the Assembly. You'll have to wait your turn.*"

> **He says: I know.**
>
> **He says: I've never had to wait my turn in my life. I'd like to try.**

---

The first child came down the stair at nine.

A boy. Twelve. In white. Thin, with his hair combed flat and his hands clenched at his sides, the way children's hands were clenched on reading mornings on every world of the Federation for nine thousand years. He came round the last curve of the stair and stopped at the bottom and looked at the stone, and at the benches full of families, and at the empty box above them, and then at the woman in deep red standing beside the stone with her hand on it.

He did not know who she was. Seed said so. He was from a street of tailors on the east side of the city, and his family was graded C, and he had never seen a Matriarch except on screens.

He started to kneel.

"*Don't,*" said Corvane Varr-Anthos.

The boy stopped, halfway down.

"*Please,*" said the Matriarch. Without the grade. "*You don't have to. Stand up. I'm going to ask you something. First I'm going to tell you what I'll do with the answer.*"

The boy stood up. Very slowly. Staring at her.

"*Whatever you say,*" said Corvane, "*I'll tell the stone. And then the stone will ask you too. There's someone in it now. He'll ask you what you want to become. And whatever you say, he'll open that. Not what he reads. What you say.*" She did not take her hand off the stone. "*And if you don't want to touch it, you don't have to. You can go back up the stair. Nobody will mark you. Nobody will give you a face.*"

The boy stared at her.

"*What do you want?*" said the Matriarch of the House of Anthos.

---

He did not answer for a long time.

Nobody hurried him. The families on the benches did not move. Lingyue stood at the foot of the stair with Xiaodeng awake in the sling, looking round with the cloudy unfocused eyes of a child of two months at a round room full of people. Old Seven stood at the bottom of the stair, facing out, up it, toward the four hundred and five children still waiting on the curve.

"*I don't know,*" said the boy at last. Very small. "*Nobody said we'd be asked. They said the stone would tell us.*"

"*That's all right,*" said Corvane. "*Do you want to touch it?*"

He looked at the stone.

"*Is it going to tell me what I am?*"

"*No,*" said the Matriarch. "*It's going to ask.*"

The boy looked at the black stone for a long moment. Then he walked up the three steps of the platform, slowly, with his hands clenched, and stopped in front of it, beside the woman in red, and put his hand on it.

**[What do you want to become?]**

The letters formed in the air above the stone, in the reading-hall below the House of Anthos, in the Federation's script, in a hand that was not the stone's. Seed said it was a hand that had signed ten thousand orders and was learning, slowly, to write something else.

The boy looked up at them.

"*I want to make coats,*" he said. "*Like my mother. She makes the best coats on the street. Everybody says.*" His voice went very small. "*I was afraid it would say I was too bright to make coats. My cousin was S. They took her to the Academy. She doesn't make coats.*"

The stone was quiet for a moment.

**[Then make coats.]**

**[Chosen.]**

**[Talent: — not marked —]**

And under it, smaller, in the same hand, the hand that had signed ten thousand orders:

**[I'd like one. Gray. Like a C's. If you have time.]**

---

The boy took his hand off the stone and stood there staring at the letters. Then he turned round, and looked at the benches, where a woman in a tailor's apron had stood up with both hands over her mouth.

He went down the steps and across the floor of the reading-hall to her. Not running. He was twelve, and in white, on a reading morning, and nobody on Anthos had ever run on a reading morning. Walking. Fast.

And she caught him.

> **He says: one.**
>
> **He says: four hundred and five to go.**
>
> **He says: tell Corvane she asks them too slowly.**

"*Tell him,*" said Corvane Varr-Anthos, with her hand on the stone, and her face doing something he had seen it do in a courtyard on Blue Star, when a woman in a wheelchair asked her whether she had asked her daughter anything, "*tell him I'm going to ask every one of them exactly that slowly. And so is he.*"

---

They came all morning.

All afternoon. Four hundred and six children of Anthos, down the curving stair into the reading-hall below the House, one at a time, in white. And at the bottom of the stair, a woman in deep red asked each of them first what they wanted, and told them they did not have to touch the stone, and some of them did not, and went back up the stair, and their families came down off the benches and met them at the top. And some of them did, and the stone asked them what they wanted to become, and they said.

*A healer. A pilot. Something that makes things grow. I don't know.*

*A dancer. My father says it's not a real thing. The stone said it is.*

*I want to work in the yards. With the children who came out of the walls. I want to teach them to swim.*

*I don't know.*

*I want to be first.*

That one, too. Twice. Seed counted. And the stone was quiet for a long time each time, and then wrote, in the hand that had signed ten thousand orders: **[I was first for two hundred years. Ask me what it was like. Then decide.]**

And both of them, Seed said, stood there with their hands on the stone and asked. And the stone told them. And neither of them chose *first* in the end. One chose *I don't know yet*. The other chose something Seed did not translate, because the girl who said it asked it not to, and Seed said that was the first time anyone had ever asked it not to translate something, and it had liked being asked.

---

At five in the afternoon, when the last of the four hundred and six had gone up the curving stair and the families had gone with them and the reading-hall below the House of Anthos was empty except for a woman in red with her hand on a stone, and a boy with a staff, and a woman with a sword and a baby, and a dead captain with one arm, and a former Listener-Captain without a face: Maelis let go of her sister's hand.

She walked across the floor of the reading-hall to the platform.

She did not go up the steps. She stopped at the bottom of them and looked at the stone. The stone that had read her at twelve on a reading morning twelve years ago, with her mother in the box, and marked her E, and the House had given her to the fold.

Her mother, beside the stone, did not move.

"*Mother,*" said Maelis.

Corvane Varr-Anthos looked down at her daughter, twelve in the body and twenty-three inside, at the bottom of the platform steps.

"*You asked me what I want,*" said Maelis. "*Ninety-one days. Ninety-four now. Every day. And on the seventy-first, I told you.*" She looked at the stone. "*I'd like to tell him too.*"

The Matriarch of the House of Anthos stood very still with her hand on the stone.

"*He's in there,*" she said. "*The First. Severin. He asked to be. He's been asking all day.*"

"*I know,*" said Maelis. "*I watched.*"

She went up the steps.

She stood in front of the stone beside her mother and looked at it for a long time. Then she put her hand on it. Small. Twelve years old. Flat. Beside her mother's.

**[What do you want to become?]**

Maelis Varr-Anthos looked up at the letters in the air above the stone that had marked her E.

"*Thirteen,*" she said.

---

The stone was quiet for a long time.

Seed said afterward that it did not know what was happening in the stone during that time. Nobody did. It was between a girl who had been twelve for twelve years and a man who had been first for two hundred, inside a black stone below the House of Anthos where both of them had been read.

And then the letters came.

**[I can't open that. It isn't a Law. Nothing on the tree inside me makes anyone thirteen.]**

Maelis did not take her hand off the stone.

**[But you don't need a stone for it.]**

**[You only need a year.]**

**[So I'm going to open nothing.]**

**[Chosen.]**

**[Talent: — not marked —]**

And then, under it, in the same hand, smaller, the hand that had signed ten thousand orders and one last one, in a corridor, badly, like a word in a book:

**[Come back next year and tell me what thirteen was like.]**

**[I'll ask.]**
