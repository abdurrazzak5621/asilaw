## Chapter 61: The Last Gate

The north gate of Lingzhou had not been opened for walkers in thirty-one years.

Trucks went through it. Armored convoys, survey teams in sealed vehicles, the Defense Corps on patrol, the night-market vans before the Bureau shut them down. But nobody walked out of it. There was a sign on the inside of the gate, painted in red on the gray stone, so old that the paint had faded to the color of rust:

*BEYOND THIS POINT, THE CITY CANNOT PROTECT YOU.*

At five in the morning, in the dark, in the cold, with frost on the cobbles and breath steaming, the gatekeepers turned the great iron wheel, and the north gate of Lingzhou groaned open on its hinges, slowly, wide enough for people to walk through two by two.

On the other side was the Wastes.

---

They had come to see them off. Not twenty thousand this time. Su Mingli had asked for no crowds. But the street inside the gate was full anyway, quietly, all the way back to the market. People in coats and scarves, holding candles. Unlit. The Quiet in the front row with Sister Ning in the middle, small and straight with her hands folded around her candle. Fat Tan, enormous in a padded coat, holding an umbrella over her even though it wasn't raining. Lu in his suit, with Doctor Kwan beside him in a borrowed Bureau parka two sizes too big.

And at the very front, at the line painted across the cobbles where the city ended, Xiaoyu.

She did not have a scowl this time. She had his green flask, mended with white tape, and she put it into his hands without a word and closed his fingers around it.

"Jasmine," she said. "Hot. You'll skip breakfast. I know you."

"I won't."

"You will." She looked past him at the open gate, at the black plain beyond it going gray in the first light. Then she looked back at him, and her eyes were fierce and dry. "Bring her home," she said. "Or I'm coming to get both of you."

---

There were twelve of them. Nine living, three dead.

Su Mingli at the front, in a long gray coat, with nothing on her back at all, because a King did not carry a pack.

Yuanshu behind her, with Lu Xuanming's black leather notebook inside his coat and his training spear across his shoulders and his mother's flask on his hip.

Shen Lingyue, with her sword. Qin Hao, in the dark green of the Western War Zone, with his grandfather's warrant in his breast pocket and a rifle he didn't need slung over his shoulder because the old general had insisted. The Mo twins, Xing and Shi, small and identical in matching gray parkas, carrying one pack between them and arguing about who would carry it next. Bai Lu, with a medical bag that was much too big for her, which nobody had been allowed to carry.

Old Seven, two meters ten of bone, with his spear and his gray flame. Iron Wall Zhao, broad as a doorway, with his great empty shield. Hundred-Arrow Lin, a drift of ash and dark air with an iron arrowhead for a heart, who did not walk so much as move from place to place when nobody was looking.

And the guides.

---

The first was a woman of about fifty named Yan Shuang.

She was short and broad and weathered as a fence post, with gray hair cropped close to her skull and a face the color of old leather from thirty years of wind. She wore a sheepskin coat and fur-lined boots and carried an enormous pack as if it weighed nothing. Her right hand was missing. Not recently. The sleeve was folded and pinned, and she did everything with her left with the ease of long habit, rolling a cigarette one-handed against her thigh while she waited at the gate.

Su Mingli had found her. In the Bureau's records. In Fat Tan's ledger.

She had driven the trucks.

---

She had told Yuanshu herself, three weeks ago, in the vault under the Ossuary. Standing. She had refused a chair.

"Eleven years," she'd said. "For Northern Gate Remains Recovery. Driving. North to the Wastes, south to the market. I knew the routes. Every ridge, every glass field, every crevasse. That's why they hired me." She had looked at the steel drawers full of the Ledger. "Bones, mostly. Old dead, Black Tide dead, beast bones. I told myself it was salvage."

"And the hollows?"

"I drove those too. The last three years." She had not looked away from him. "They sat in the back of my truck in rows. Looking north. I talked to them on the long drives. I told myself they couldn't hear me." She had lifted the pinned sleeve. "Lost the hand to frostbite on the last run. Before the Bureau came. Doctor said it was the cold. I think it was the one thing in my life that ever made sense."

"Why do you want to come?"

"Because I know the way." Yan Shuang had shrugged. "The way the trucks went. All the way north. To where they picked them up. I can take you there faster than anyone alive." She had paused. "And because I wrote my name in your book last week. YES. If I die out there, I want to be useful." Her weathered face had not changed at all. "I'd like to have been useful once."

He had looked at her for a long time.

Then he had said yes. And Sister Ning, by the door, had written it down.

---

The second guide was Sergeant Vera Sokolova of the Northern Union.

Marshal Orlov had sent her, with a letter. She was tall and pale and silent, in white winter fatigues, with ice-gray eyes and a scar through one eyebrow. She had been on the relief team that reached Grid Nine after the outpost died. She had walked through the frozen common room. She had helped cut forty-one boys out of the ice.

She shook Yuanshu's hand at the gate and said, in careful Huaxian, "The Marshal says you are to come back alive, and that this is an order, and that he knows you will not obey it, and that I am to make you." She let go. "I will try."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me." She looked out at the plain. "Thank me at Grid Nine. If we arrive."

---

Su Mingli did not make a speech.

She stood at the painted line on the cobbles with her back to the city and looked north for a long moment. Then she turned, once, and looked at the people with their candles, and at Sister Ning, and at Xiaoyu.

"Light them when we're past the first ridge," she said. "Not before. If something's listening, I'd rather it heard the city saying goodbye than us."

Then she stepped over the line.

The rest of them followed her. Two by two. Out through the gate in the dark, past the faded red sign, onto the frozen cobbles of the old road that ran out from the wall for half a kilometer and then simply stopped, as if the people building it had looked up one day and decided there was no point.

Behind them, the gatekeepers turned the iron wheel. The north gate of Lingzhou groaned, and swung, and closed.

---

The Wastes began where the road ended.

Yuanshu had seen them from the wall a hundred times. He had fought on them, at Sector Nine, at the edge of the training grounds. He had never walked out into them with the wall at his back and nothing in front.

It was a plain of black glass.

Flat. Endless. Gray-black and shining under the dawn, cracked into great irregular plates like a dry lakebed, each one a meter or ten meters or a hundred across, with seams between them full of drifted gray dust and frost. It ran away north to a horizon so far and so flat that you could see the curve of the world in it. Here and there, a ridge of dark rock broke the surface like the spine of a sunken animal. Here and there, half buried in the glass, something white.

Bones. Beasts, mostly. Huge things, ribs like the arches of bridges, skulls the size of cars, frozen into the glass where the Black Tide had left them thirty-one years ago and never come back to collect them.

The wind came across it from the north, cold and clean and smelling of nothing at all.

> **Residue.**
>
> **Everywhere. Under the glass. In every direction.**
>
> **It is like the cemetery on the first day, Yuanshu. But the cemetery was a field.**
>
> **This is an ocean.**

---

They walked until the sun came up.

It rose on their right, enormous and red, over the edge of the glass, and the whole plain lit up at once like a sheet of black water catching fire. Every plate of glass blazed copper. Every seam of frost went pink. Their fourteen shadows stretched away to the west across the glass, absurdly long, like the shadows of giants.

At the first ridge, a low spine of dark rock three kilometers out, Su Mingli stopped and turned around.

Yuanshu turned with her.

Behind them, the wall of Lingzhou ran along the southern horizon, gray and small and very far, sixty kilometers of it from end to end. And on top of the wall, above the north gate, very faintly, one by one, then in dozens, then in thousands, small gold lights were coming on.

Candles. All along the battlements. Spreading east and west from the gate along the top of the wall like a fuse burning in both directions, until the whole northern face of the city was lined with a thin bright thread of gold, glittering in the cold blue dawn.

Nobody in the expedition said anything.

Yan Shuang took her unlit cigarette out of her mouth and looked at the wall for a long time. Then she put the cigarette away in her coat pocket, unsmoked, and turned north.

"Three hundred and forty kilometers," she said. "Eleven days, if the glass is kind. It's never kind." She hitched her pack. "Grid Nine's further. Much further. We'll worry about that after." She looked at Yuanshu with her weathered, unreadable face. "There's something you should see before we get there. Day four. The trucks always went around it." She paused. "I never told anyone about it. Nobody would have believed me."

"What is it?"

Yan Shuang looked out over the endless shining plain.

"The road north," she said, "is already crowded."
