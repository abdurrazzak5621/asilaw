## Chapter 124: Night Watch

Fat Tan found him in nine hours, with a pencil.

"Eleven names," he said, laying the paper flat on the Bureau conference table at four in the morning. "Northline Security. A Bureau contractor. Night watch at the Institute, the week the crate came to Dock B." He tapped the list with one thick finger. "Ten of them have alibis that a child could check. Shift logs, door cards, girlfriends, a man who was in hospital with his appendix. The eleventh has an alibi too. That's how I knew."

Lu Zhengtian, at the head of the table, looked up from his tablet.

"Explain."

"It's perfect," said Fat Tan. "Too perfect. Every door card on time to the minute. Every shift log in the same handwriting, same pressure on the pen, same little hook on the sevens. For eight months." He sat back, and the chair complained. "Nobody's that regular, Director. I kept a ledger for twenty-two years. I know what a real one looks like. Real ones have mistakes in them. Real ones have a day where somebody was hungover and wrote the date wrong. This one's been written by someone who wanted it to look like nobody was ever there."

He turned the paper around and put his finger on the name.

*Pei Shuren. 34. Northline Security, Grade 2. Night watch, Dock B.*

"He moved out of his flat the morning after the Charter passed," said Doctor Kwan, from the other end of the table, where she had three laptops open and her glasses pushed up into her hair. "Paid the last month in cash. Left the furniture. The landlady said he was the nicest tenant she ever had." She swallowed. "She said he always asked how her knee was."

Yuanshu had been sitting in the corner of the conference room for nine hours with the staff across his knees, and the cold of the Northern Wastes still in his bones, and Seed very quiet behind his eyes. He looked up.

"Asked how her knee was," he said.

"Every time he saw her."

> **Like Gu.**
>
> **No. Not the thread. I can check that when we find him.**
>
> **The kindness.**

---

They did not have to find him.

At eight o'clock that morning, when the Ossuary on East Market Road opened its bronze doors for the day, a man in a plain gray jacket was waiting on the step. He had been there since dawn, the volunteer on the door said afterward. Standing quietly. He had held the door for an old woman with a walking frame, and then for a mother with two children, and then come in last and walked across the marble floor of the old banking hall to the registration desk, and said that he would like to add his name to the Ledger.

Xiaoyu was at the desk that morning. She told Yuanshu later that she had known at once, the way you know a dog is going to bite. Not because he was frightening. Because he was not.

"Which choice?" she had asked.

And he had smiled, a calm, open, slightly sorry smile, and said: "None of them, I'm afraid. I'd like to suggest a fifth."

---

Yuanshu came through the bronze doors twenty minutes later with Lu, Old Seven and Lingyue behind him, and found Pei Shuren sitting on the plain wooden chair by the vault steps with his hands folded in his lap, under the long wall of the board. YES. ASK MY FAMILY. ONLY TO SPEAK. QUIET. CAME HOME. FROM OUT THERE. INSIDE.

He was an ordinary man. That was the first thing. Thirty-four, thin, neat, with a pleasant tired face and the very short haircut of a security guard and a small scar through one eyebrow. He stood up when Yuanshu came in. He did it the way you stand up when a teacher enters a classroom.

"Mister Zhou," he said. "Thank you for coming. I didn't want you to have to look for me. You've been very busy."

> **Heart rate: 61. Steady.**
>
> **He is not afraid of you, Yuanshu. He is not afraid of anything in this room.**
>
> **And he has a thread.**

*Where does it go?*

A long pause.

> **Up.**
>
> **Thin. Very thin. Thinner than Gu's was. It goes up out of the top of his head into the sky, the way the hollows' threads went.**
>
> **And it stops.**
>
> **Just below the keeper's ring, over the north. It goes all the way up to the door, and the door will not open for it, and it stops there. Like a hand on a doorknob that has been turned and turned and will not turn.**

---

Sister Ning came and sat on the chair by the bronze door with her candle unlit in her lap. Su Mingli came, and stood by the window with her arms folded. Lu stayed standing. Old Seven stood behind Yuanshu with his spear grounded and his gray flame very small and very hard.

"You accepted a crate at Dock B," said Lu. "Eight months ago. Under your own name, then took your name off the log."

"Yes."

"You took it down the Bureau shaft to the round chamber."

"Yes. I had the access. The night watch always has the access. Nobody ever thinks about the night watch." He said it without any pride at all. "I opened the cylinder's feeding port. It's for samples. It hadn't been used in twenty years. I put the seed in. I closed the port. I went back up and finished my shift."

"Why?"

Pei Shuren looked at the board. At the long wall of names. At the last column, in Xiaoyu's capitals, *INSIDE*, and the monthly lines underneath it. *The men are well.*

"Because it was a lamp," he said, "and somebody needed to put a lamp in the window."

---

He told them all of it. He did not need to be asked twice. That was the strange thing about him, Yuanshu thought later. He wanted to explain. He explained the way Gu had explained his garden: patiently, with real warmth, as if he were sorry they had not understood yet and was sure they would.

There were a hundred thousand of them. Perhaps a little more. They did not have a church. They had a register, like the Ledger, kept by hand and witnessed, and every name on it had been asked, properly, with witnesses, the way the Charter said. They called themselves nothing, because naming yourself was vanity. Other people had started calling them the Hollow Gods, after an old word from Yamato, and they had not minded.

They had begun on a mountain. In a shrine above the clouds, with twelve hundred gates up the path, where seven old men had sat in silence for twelve hundred years so that someone would remember. When the last of the Sleepers went quiet on Concordia, one of the monks who had tended them for forty years had come down the mountain and not gone back.

"He said they had it backwards," said Pei Shuren. "The Sleepers sat for twelve hundred years so that the world would remember the harvest and hide from it. He said the makers were right, the ones in the white column. The ones who asked to be kept." His face was perfectly calm. "Everything you are goes. Everything you've ever loved goes. You die and the river takes it. Unless someone keeps it. The makers saw that. They built a keeper so that nobody would ever be lost again."

"And then they asked it to let them go," said Yuanshu.

"Nine hundred and forty-one of them did. Fifty-two stayed." Pei smiled. "Fifty-two out of a thousand chose to be kept. Forever. You were there. You saw it. That isn't nothing, Mister Zhou."

"No," said Yuanshu. "It isn't."

---

"Then you didn't need the seed," said Su Mingli from the window. Her voice was flat and cold. "You have a keeper over the north that asks people what they want. You could have gone up and asked it to keep you."

"We did," said Pei Shuren.

The banking hall was very quiet.

"Six months ago," he said. "All of us. On the same night. We went out into the fields and the rooftops and the mountains and looked up at the white ring and asked it, the way you taught everyone to ask. *Keep us. Please. We want to be kept.*" He touched his own head, lightly, where Seed had seen the thread go up. "It answered. It said yes. It would keep us. If we wanted. And then it said it would ask us every day if we still wanted to be kept, and let us go the day we said no."

"*Once is not forever,*" said Sister Ning very softly, by the door.

"Yes. That's what it said. It learned that from your mother." Pei Shuren looked at Yuanshu with something that was almost compassion. "And we said: please don't. Please don't ask us again. We don't want to be asked again. We've thought about it our whole lives. We're sure. Keep us and stop asking."

He was quiet for a moment.

"It said it couldn't do that," he said. "It said it had promised."

---

> **It is true.**
>
> **I asked the keeper. Through the Elder. Just now, while he was talking.**
>
> **It says a hundred thousand voices came to the door, six months ago, all on one night. It says it said yes and then it said what you taught it, that it would ask again. It says they begged it not to.**
>
> **It says it did not know what to do. It says it still does not.**
>
> **It says one of them said: then we will find a keeper who does not ask.**

Yuanshu looked at the man on the wooden chair.

"So you went looking for one," he said.

"We were told where to look." Pei Shuren's voice did not change. "Something came to the Abbot, on the mountain, in a dream, the night after the keeper refused us. A voice. Very kind. It said there were older keepers. Gardeners. It said they keep everything and never ask. It said one of them had sent a scout to this world a long time ago, to look at the field, and the scout was still here, under a city, in a cage, waiting to be told it could call home." He folded his hands again. "It said a scout needs a heart to call with. It told us where to find seven."

"A voice in a dream," said Lu Zhengtian. He had gone very pale. "Very kind."

"Yes."

"Gu Hanshan heard a voice in a dream," said Lu. "For fifty-one years. It told him to build a transmitter on the roof of my Institute." He took off his glasses. His hands were not steady. "Did your Abbot ever wonder who was speaking?"

"Of course," said Pei Shuren gently. "We asked it. It said it was the one who would keep us." He looked at the board. "Nobody else has ever offered."

---

*Ask him,* Yuanshu thought. *Ask him what he wants. That's the job.*

He crouched in front of the wooden chair, so that his face was level with the man's, the way Old Seven crouched to talk to Hush.

"Pei Shuren," he said. "What do you want?"

The man looked at him for a while.

"I want to be kept," he said. "I want my mother to be kept, who died when I was nine and I can't remember her voice. I want everyone who ever said yes on our register to be kept, the way the fifty-two were kept. Forever. Not asked every morning. Not given a door. Kept." He smiled. "And I want you to stop asking us if we're sure."

"And the eight billion who didn't say yes?"

"They're not on our register," said Pei Shuren. "We'd never take anyone who didn't ask. That's your rule. We follow it." He tilted his head slightly. "If the gardener comes and keeps people who didn't ask, that will be the gardener's choice. Not ours. We only lit the lamp."

Sister Ning stood up.

She did it slowly, holding her unlit candle in both hands. She walked across the marble floor of the banking hall to the wooden chair, and stood in front of the man in the plain gray jacket, and looked down at him for a long moment.

"I've spent forty years telling people their choice is theirs," she said. "Not mine. Not the priest's. Not the state's." Her voice was very quiet. "Yours is yours, Pei Shuren. I'll defend it to anyone. You may go into any fire you like." She held up the candle. "But you lit a lamp in someone else's window. Three million people live in this city. You didn't ask one of them whether they'd like the gardener to come and look in."

For the first time, something moved in the calm face.

Not doubt. Something smaller. A flicker, like a man who has stepped on a stair that isn't there.

"They'd have said no," he said.

"Yes," said Sister Ning. "That's why you're supposed to ask."

---

The Bureau took him at noon. He went without resisting. He held the bronze door for the officers on the way out.

On the step, he stopped, and turned, and looked back into the banking hall at Yuanshu.

"The scout will call in fifteen days, Mister Zhou," he said. "Perhaps sooner. It doesn't matter if you kill it. The Abbot has the other lamps. There are other windows." He smiled his calm, sorry smile. "I hope you'll be kept too. I really do. I think you'd like it. You'd never have to ask anyone anything again."

Then he was gone, down the steps, into the gray noon.

Xiaoyu was standing behind the registration desk with her arms folded so tightly that her knuckles had gone white.

"I didn't give him a column," she said. "I want you to know that. He asked me for a column on the board, and I said no."

"Good," said Yuanshu.

"He said it was very kind of me to take the time to consider it." Her voice cracked. "Ge. I hate him so much."

"I know."

> **I do not hate him.**
>
> **I think that is worse.**
>
> **He is the first person I have ever met who understands Rule One completely and does not want it.**
