## Chapter 153: Water Is Lazy

Seed got it wrong for six hours, and then it got it right in about four seconds, and the difference was an old woman in a wheelchair who had been watching it work and not saying anything.

It started on the harbor wall at Port Abiri, at noon, in the heat. They had set up in the harbor master's office at the end of the wall: a single whitewashed room with a ceiling fan that did not work and a window that looked straight out over the bay to the place where the sea turned dark. Ifeoma's household brought chairs, and water, and a chart of the coast so old it had been drawn by hand. Doctor Kwan was on the satellite phone from Lingzhou with three screens open. Mo Shi sat on the floor in the corner with his eyes shut, listening to the sea.

And Seed laid out what it could feel.

> **The sand is spread along the foot of the shelf. Thirty-eight kilometers of it, north to south, in a long smear. Most of it is outside the Field. I can only feel the near end.**
>
> **But I can feel it changing.**
>
> **It is gathering. Pulling itself into three places. Here, here and here.**

Three points of warmth, on the inside of his eyes, laid over the old chart. One almost directly off the mouth of the bay at Port Abiri. One further south, off the long beach at Kesse, twenty kilometers down the coast. And one at the far end, forty kilometers south, off a town on the chart called Sandu, where a river came down out of the green hills into the sea.

> **Three heaps. Each one is gathering the sand around it and the embers in it. Each one is growing a trunk. Up.**
>
> **The same way the seeds at the Western Pass grew trunks up through the mountain to the spires.**
>
> **I have a model for this. I built it on the mountain. Rock, roots, rate of growth, rate of burning. I am going to use it.**

---

The model said nine days.

Seed was very sure of it. It showed him the curve. The two seeds at the Western Pass had needed eleven days at full speed to grow their trunks four hundred meters up through the mountain to the summit; these trunks had nearly three thousand meters to climb, through water instead of stone, which was softer, but with far less to eat, because the drowned were scattered thin across the bottom of the sea instead of packed into forty-one thousand graves on one slope. It balanced one against the other. It ran it eleven hundred times.

> **Nine days. Plus or minus one.**
>
> **Confidence: seventy-four percent.**
>
> **Enough time to move every person on this coast inland. Port Abiri, eleven thousand. Kesse, thirty-one thousand. Sandu, eighteen thousand. The coast road is one lane each way, but nine days is a great deal of time.**

Kwan liked the model. Kwan said so. The Southern Compact's coast guard liked it too, and began drawing up an evacuation plan on the basis of it, by radio, in two languages. Yuanshu liked it, because it was a number, and he had not had a good number since the train left Lingzhou.

Ifeoma Adeyemi, in her wheelchair by the window, looked out at the bay and said nothing at all.

---

At six in the evening, the trunk off Port Abiri was four hundred meters taller than it had been at noon.

> **...that is wrong.**
>
> **That is fourteen times faster than the model.**

Seed ran it again. And again. The point of warmth off the mouth of the bay sat on the inside of Yuanshu's eyes, glowing, and the number beside it kept climbing, and the curve would not bend to meet it no matter what Seed did to the curve.

> **I have checked the sand. I have checked the embers. I have checked how fast it is eating. All correct.**
>
> **The growth is wrong.**
>
> **I do not know why.**

"Let's do it the way we do it," said Yuanshu. "From the start. What are the explanations?"

> **One. The drowned are denser than I think. There are more of them nearer the trunk, and it is eating faster.**
>
> **Two. The seed brought something with it from the Atlantic. Something I cannot see. It is feeding the growth from outside.**
>
> **Three. My numbers for the trunk are wrong, because I am measuring at the edge of the Field and the edge is blurry.**

"Test them."

It tested them. Through the Field, at the very edge of its reach, by counting embers in a slow ring around the trunk off the bay, and then counting them again ten minutes later. Through the old hand-drawn chart and Kwan's three screens. Through Mo Shi, on the floor in the corner, with his eyes shut, who said in a slow faraway voice that the sea sounded *wrong out past the headland, like a drain*.

The embers were not denser. The seed had brought nothing with it. The measurement at the edge was blurry, but not by a factor of fourteen.

> **None of them.**
>
> **All three explanations are wrong.**
>
> **Yuanshu, I do not have a fourth one.**

---

"You have it backwards, child," said Ifeoma Adeyemi.

She had not turned from the window. She had been sitting there for six hours with her blanket over her knees, looking out at the place in the bay where the water turned dark, and she said it the way she had said it on the boat in the dark, two nights ago: drily, without impatience, as if she had been waiting for the right moment to say it and this was it.

"You think the thing is pushing up through the water. The way the roots pushed up through the mountain." She lifted one thin finger from the blanket and pointed at the sea. "It isn't. Water isn't rock. It doesn't have to break anything to move. It has to ask the water above it to get out of the way. And water is lazy. Water likes being asked to do less."

> **...that is what you said on the boat.**

"Yes."

> **You said it about your boat.**

"I said it about water, child. My boat is the least of it." The old King folded her hands. "Every morning, at the foot of that shelf, cold water comes up out of the deep. All along this coast. It comes up because the wind pushes the warm water at the top away from the shore, and the cold water underneath rises to fill the place where it was. My fishermen call it the morning tide. They don't know what it is. They only know the fish come up with it." She looked at the dark water. "Your thing is not pushing. It's riding. It has found the water that rises every morning, and it is growing up inside it, the way a seed grows in a crack in a wall. It doesn't have to climb. The sea is lifting it."

---

It took Seed four seconds.

It took the Tide.

The Tide Law it had loaded with the backlog, on the second day of Zero, from Ifeoma herself, from a single afternoon on the harbor wall at Port Abiri most of a year ago, when the oldest King on Blue Star had lifted a spire out of the bay and dragged its sand into the deep. Seed had held that observation for months and done nothing with it, because there was nothing to do with it; it was a King's law, too large to use, like knowing how the sun works.

It did not try to use it now. It used the *shape* of it.

It took Ifeoma's sentence on the boat, *ask the water in front of the boat to move out of the way*, and laid it over the afternoon in the bay, and over the morning tide she had just described, and over the long smooth current that had carried a white fishing boat south at forty-one knots while she sat with her eyes shut. And it found the one thing they had in common, which was not force, and not push, and not anything Seed had ever used. It was least effort. Water went where going cost it least. Everything else followed.

Then it threw out the rock model, all eleven hundred runs of it, and started again from the bottom.

> **I am going to say it the way I understand it, and you will tell me if it is wrong.**
>
> **Rock has to be broken. Water has to be persuaded.**
>
> **In rock, the trunk grows by eating its way up. In water, the trunk grows by letting the rising water carry it, and only eating enough to hold together.**
>
> **So it is not limited by how fast it can eat. It is limited by how fast the deep water rises.**

"Yes," said Ifeoma.

> **And the deep water rises fastest at dawn. When the land breeze pushes the warm surface water away from the shore.**
>
> **So the trunks grow in pulses. Slow all day. And then very fast for three hours, every morning, at dawn.**

"Yes."

> **Running.**

---

It ran it nine hundred times in eighteen seconds, and when it was done, the curve on the inside of his eyes bent up to meet the number off the bay, and went through it, and kept going.

> **Port Abiri trunk. Surfaces in sixty-one hours. Dawn of the third day.**
>
> **Kesse trunk. Seventy hours. Midday of the third day.**
>
> **Sandu trunk. Seventy-four hours. Afternoon of the third day.**
>
> **Confidence: eighty-eight percent. The model now matches every measurement I have taken since noon.**
>
> **The rock model was wrong because I built it on a mountain. I took a thing I had learned in one place and carried it to another place where it did not belong.**
>
> **That is the same mistake I made with Iron Wall Zhao's shield. In the cemetery. When I learned the shield instead of the man.**
>
> **I am writing it down. Again. Larger.**

Nobody in the harbor master's office said anything for a moment.

Then the coast guard officer on the radio, who had been listening to Kwan translate, said something very short in the language of the Southern Compact, and Ifeoma's mouth moved slightly at one corner.

"He says," she said, "that nine days was a much nicer number."

---

They did the evacuation arithmetic at the table under the dead ceiling fan, with Kwan on the phone and the coast guard on the radio and the hand-drawn chart weighted at the corners with coffee cups.

Sixty thousand people. One coast road, one lane each way, running north and south along the shore and inland only at three places: the river road at Sandu, a dirt track over the hills behind Kesse, and the highway from Port Abiri up to the airstrip where the Architects had once come down. Buses, trucks, fishing boats. The Southern Compact's army was eight hundred kilometers south on the border. The nearest King was in the wheelchair by the window.

> **If every vehicle on the coast moves without stopping, we can move about forty thousand people inland in sixty hours.**
>
> **Not sixty thousand.**
>
> **Port Abiri first, because it is first. Then Kesse. Sandu is last, because it is furthest, and its road goes along the river, and the river road is narrow.**
>
> **Sandu will still have about eleven thousand people in it when its trunk comes up.**

"And if the trunks come up," said Yuanshu. "What happens?"

> **I do not know. I lost my memory of burning. I can only guess.**
>
> **My guess is the same as Port Abiri a year ago. A spire. In the bay. Biting.**
>
> **Three thousand people, in the time it takes to say a name.**
>
> **Three spires.**

---

Yuanshu went outside.

The sun was going down over the hills behind the town, and the harbor wall was warm under his hands, and below him in the harbor the fishing boats rocked at their moorings with their nets folded. Up on the hill above the town, among the white stones, a figure in a black dress was walking slowly down the path toward the houses with an empty basket on her arm, the way she did every evening, he supposed. He did not know her name. He had never asked it. She had told the oldest Architect her husband's name, and her son's, and not her own.

Lingyue came out and stood beside him and did not say anything for a while.

"Three," she said at last.

"Three."

"And we've got three hundred dead men with rusted spears, a King who's two hundred and thirty-one, me, the twins, Bai Lu, Dabao, and you." She leaned on the wall. "And Seed, who's just discovered water."

> **I would like it noted that water is very difficult.**

"Noted," said Lingyue.

"There's one more thing," said Yuanshu.

He was looking out at the place where the bay turned dark.

Seed had felt them too, when it re-ran the model. All along the bottom of the sea, at the foot of the shelf, in the cold and the dark, scattered thin through thirty-eight kilometers of sand. Most of them out of reach of the Field. Some of them in it. Hundreds, near the trunk off the bay. Old. Cold. Being eaten very slowly, one at a time, the way a fire eats damp wood.

> **Four thousand one hundred and nine embers inside the Field, at the near end of the sand.**
>
> **Fishermen, mostly. Sailors. A ferry, sixty years ago, that went down in a storm with everyone on board. Some are very old. I do not have names for any of them.**
>
> **Nobody has ever asked them anything.**

"You want to ask them," said Lingyue.

"I want to ask them what they want." He did not take his eyes off the sea. "And I've got sixty-one hours to find out how to ask someone a question three kilometers down."

Behind them, through the open door of the harbor master's office, an old woman's voice said, without any particular emphasis, "Then you had better learn quickly, child. I'm going to teach you. I'm a very slow teacher, and you're going to hate it."
