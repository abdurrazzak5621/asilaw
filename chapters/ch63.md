## Chapter 63: Third Battalion

The sergeant's name was Huo Changsheng, and it took him an hour to remember it.

Yuanshu sat on the crushed glass at the edge of the road with his hand held out, and waited, and held the quiet, while the gray river of the dead went past in front of him, scrape, scrape, scrape, and in the middle of it two dead soldiers stood facing each other like two stones in a stream.

Old Seven did not move. He stood in front of his sergeant with his spear grounded and his gray flame burning low and steady, the way he had stood beside Yuanshu on the first night in the cemetery, the way he stood at the foot of every bed in every ward. Waiting. Not pushing. Just there.

And slowly, the way frost comes up on a window, the thing at the bottom of Sergeant Huo came up to meet him.

> **It is very faint.**
>
> **Words, almost. Pieces of words. I am putting them together.**
>
> **I will tell you what I can.**

---

> **"...captain."**
>
> **"...captain. You're... here. They said. You fell. Eastern wall."**
>
> **"...we looked. After. We looked for you. Then the... the man. The Necromancer. He made us... stand. Everyone. Told us. Go after it."**
>
> **"So we went."**

The sergeant's three-fingered hand was still raised, halfway to a salute, trembling. It had been trembling for an hour.

> **"...how long. Captain. How long have we been walking."**

Old Seven did not have a voice. He had never had one, not since the first night. But his gray flame changed. It brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed, slowly, like breathing, and Yuanshu understood, and so did Seed, and so, from the way his trembling hand went still, did Sergeant Huo.

> **"...thirty-one years."**
>
> **"Oh."**
>
> **"Oh, captain."**

---

> **"Nobody told us we could stop."**

The words came up so quietly that Yuanshu almost missed them under the endless scrape of bone on glass.

> **"He said, go after it. And then he stopped saying anything. And we kept going. We kept going because nobody told us we could stop, and nobody came, and the thing in the sky went away and we couldn't find it, and we kept going anyway, north, north, because that was the order, and you don't..."**
>
> **"...you don't break ranks. Captain. You taught us. You don't break ranks."**
>
> **"I'm so tired."**

Old Seven's flame went out.

Not out. Down. Down to a single tiny blue-gray bead in the cage of his ribs, the way it had gone on the night Yuanshu read the notebook aloud. *Qi is missing. He was holding the eastern end of the wall.*

And then the old spear captain did something Yuanshu had never seen him do in six months. He let go of his spear.

It fell into the dust of the road with a soft clatter. The dead flowed around it.

Old Seven reached out with both bony hands, and took his sergeant's trembling, three-fingered hand in them, and held it.

---

Yuanshu stood up.

He walked out onto the road. The dead went past him, scrape, scrape, close enough to brush his sleeves, cold as the inside of a refrigerator, and did not see him, and did not slow. He walked through them to where the two soldiers stood, and stopped beside them.

"Sergeant Huo," he said aloud. "I'm not your captain. I'm not the man who raised you. I can't give you an order." He swallowed. "But I can ask you something. If you want me to."

The skull turned toward him. Slowly. Very slowly.

> **"...ask."**

"Do you want to stop?"

A long pause. The river of the dead went past.

> **"...yes."**
>
> **"No."**
>
> **"I don't know. I don't know anymore what stopping is."**

"Then here's what I can do," said Yuanshu. "If you want to rest. Lie down. Stop walking. Go quiet. I can do that. Gently. You'd just... stop. Like falling asleep after a long shift." He paused. "And if you want to keep walking, but with someone, instead of after something, you can walk with us. With your captain. North. We're going where the thing in the sky went. To find someone it took." He held the quiet. "Or neither. Or something else. It's your choice. Nobody else's. Not mine. Not his."

The skull looked at him for a long time.

Then it looked at Old Seven.

> **"...captain. Where are you going?"**

Old Seven's flame came back up. Slowly. Gray, and then brighter. And he bent down, and picked up his spear out of the dust, and turned, and pointed it north.

> **"Then I'll walk with you."**
>
> **"I always did."**

---

It went down the road like a ripple.

Sergeant Huo turned around in the middle of the gray river and walked against it. South. Back down the endless crowd. Old Seven walked beside him. And as they passed, here and there, one dead soldier in a hundred, one in a thousand, would stop. Mid-stride. Foot raised.

A skeleton in a gray greatcoat with a faded corporal's stripe. A tall one with no jaw. A short one with a rusted bayonet still clipped to what was left of a belt. A woman, bones wrapped in the rags of a field medic's armband.

They stopped, and stood there in the river with the dead flowing around them, and turned their skulls toward the two soldiers walking past. Toward the gray flame. Toward a spear captain they had last seen holding the eastern end of a wall, alone, thirty-one years ago.

And they remembered.

Not much. A little. Enough.

> **Third Battalion.**
>
> **They are finding each other.**
>
> **I am counting. Thirty. Fifty. Ninety.**
>
> **They were all walking near each other. Thirty-one years. They kept together without knowing why.**
>
> **Because nobody broke ranks.**

---

By sunset, there were a hundred and forty-one of them.

They stood on the slope of the valley below the ridge where the expedition had camped, in rows, in the order of a battalion that had not stood in order for thirty-one years. Gray bones and rags of gray greatcoats, red with the low red light. Some of them were missing arms. Some were missing skulls. One was only a pelvis and a pair of legs, and stood in its place in the third row anyway.

In front of them, Old Seven. Beside him, Sergeant Huo.

And in front of all of them, Yuanshu, with his hand on the black leather notebook inside his coat, asking.

One at a time. A hundred and forty-one times. While the sun went down and the cold came up off the glass and the rest of the gray river went on past in the valley below, scrape, scrape, scrape, north, endlessly, not seeing any of it.

Sister Ning was not there. So Sergeant Vera Sokolova of the Northern Union sat on a rock beside him with a notebook of her own on her knee, and a pencil, and wrote down every answer in careful Huaxian. She had asked to. She said Marshal Orlov would want it done properly.

> **YES: 88. Walk with the captain.**
>
> **QUIET: 53.**
>
> **ONLY TO SPEAK: 0. They do not have much left to say.**
>
> **ASK MY FAMILY: 0.**
>
> **Their families are not anywhere anyone could ask anymore.**

---

The fifty-three lay down at dusk.

He did it the way he had done it for the people in WARDEN's core: gently, one at a time, opening the Death Law under each of them like a cool dark door, and letting the order go. *Walk* went out of them like a breath. And one by one, in the last red light, fifty-three old soldiers of the Third Battalion folded down onto the crushed glass of the valley slope, slowly, without a sound, and lay still.

Some of them lay down on their backs, looking at the sky.

Some of them curled on their sides, like men asleep in a barracks.

One of them, the woman with the medic's armband, lay down beside the one that was only a pelvis and legs, and put the bones of her hand on its knee.

Old Seven walked down the line of them with his spear, and stopped at each one, and stood for a moment, and moved on. A captain inspecting his men.

When he reached the end, he turned to face the eighty-eight who were still standing. He raised his spear.

They raised what they had. Arms. Bayonets. Hands with missing fingers. A pelvis and a pair of legs that had no arms at all stood a little straighter.

> **Third Battalion.**
>
> **Reporting.**

> **Command: three Law Cores and eighty-eight of the Third Battalion. Soul-power load: thirty-one percent.**
>
> **Death Law: 22.4% → 23.1%. A hundred and forty-one echoes, every one of them asked. That is more Death than I have read since the cemetery.**
>
> **Small steps. As promised.**

---

Lingyue found him later, at the edge of the firelight on the ridge, looking down at the valley.

The river of the dead was still going past below. Two hundred thousand, minus a hundred and forty-one. Scrape, scrape, scrape. In the dark you couldn't see them, only hear them, a long dry endless sound like the sea on a shingle beach.

She sat down beside him with her sword across her knees.

"My light burns undead," she said, after a long time. "I've been thinking about it all day. If we have to fight something out here, and they're in the way..." She stopped. "I'd have to bend it around two hundred thousand people who never said yes to any of this."

"I know."

"I don't know if I can bend it that far."

"I know."

She was quiet for a while.

"You can't ask all of them," she said. "Can you. One by one. It took you three hours for a hundred and forty. They're two hundred thousand."

"No," said Yuanshu. "Not one by one." He put his hand on the notebook inside his coat. "But Lu Xuanming raised them. All at once. With one thing." He looked north. "*My staff is with me. Take it. It remembers more than I do.*"

Lingyue looked at him in the firelight.

"Whoever holds the staff," she said slowly, "can talk to all of them."

"Maybe."

"Or order them." Her voice was very quiet. "Two hundred thousand soldiers that the thing in the sky can't see."

"Yes."

"And Mercer," said Lingyue, "has had a forty-day head start."

---

At two in the morning, Sergeant Sokolova woke the camp.

She did not shout. She knelt beside each of them in turn, in the dark, with one hand on their shoulder and the other holding a small battered radio receiver to her ear, its tiny green light glowing. When she got to Yuanshu, he saw her face in the green glow, and it was white.

"The listening station at Grid Nine," she whispered. "It is automatic. Solar. Nobody has been there for a year. It still records. It still transmits, once a day, on a frequency only the Northern Union uses." She held out the receiver. "I have been listening every night. In case."

He put it to his ear.

Static. Long, soft, crackling static, from somewhere a very long way to the north.

And under the static, very faint, rising and falling, so low that he felt it in his teeth before he heard it: a sound.

It was not a voice. It was not an animal. It was not a machine. It was a long, slow, wavering note, like wind across the mouth of a bottle, like a hundred thousand people trying to scream with their mouths sewn shut. It rose, and fell, and rose.

It was getting louder.

"Eleven seconds," whispered Vera Sokolova. "That is what they played the Marshal. From the night Grid Nine died. Eleven seconds." She took the receiver back. Her hand was shaking. "That one has been going for forty minutes."

> **The Wailing Monarch.**
>
> **It is not at Grid Nine.**
>
> **Grid Nine only heard it.**
>
> **It is moving, Yuanshu. South.**
>
> **Toward the march.**
