## Chapter 5: Ten Thousand Letters

The letters started arriving on the second day.

At first they came the ordinary way, in the gray canvas sacks of the Lingzhou postal service, dropped off at the old observatory gate by a nervous mail carrier who would not get out of his van. Then they came by courier. Then people simply walked up Mount Qingyun and pushed them through the bars of the gate, and by the end of the week the pine needles under the gate were buried in envelopes, white and brown and blue, drifts of them, like snow that had fallen in the wrong season.

Tianshu's staff carried them down in plastic crates.

By the fifth day there were ten thousand.

---

Xiaoyu had claimed a long table on the twelfth floor and turned it into a sorting office.

She had rubber bands around both wrists and a pen behind each ear, and the table was covered in piles, each one marked with a sticky note in her loud, slanting handwriting. *FIND THEM.* *DON'T YOU DARE.* *MARRIAGE PROPOSALS (??).* *THREATS (gave to General Han).* *CHILDREN'S DRAWINGS.* *I DON'T KNOW WHAT THIS IS.*

The *FIND THEM* pile was the tallest. It had fallen over twice.

Yuanshu sat on the end of the table and read.

*My father was on the Eastern Line. They told my mother "unidentified." She died waiting. If he is one of the ones who stood up that night, please. I only want to know which stone.*

*My brother was nineteen. He had a scar through his left eyebrow from falling off a bicycle. If you see him, can you tell him the bicycle was my fault.*

*I don't want anything raised. I just want a name. Thirty-one years of a number. Please.*

He read them one at a time and put each one face down when he finished, gently, as if it might wake up.

> **This pile contains 6,114 letters.**
>
> **4,890 of them describe a specific person: age, unit, injury, a distinguishing mark.**
>
> **I could cross-reference these descriptions with the echoes in the eastern field. Many of them would match.**

*Not yet,* Yuanshu thought.

> **Why not?**

*Because they asked for a name. The dead haven't been asked anything.*

Seed was quiet for a moment.

> **Noted.**

---

The second pile was shorter. It was also heavier to read.

*Leave him where he is. He has done enough.*

*My husband was buried in the eastern field. If you lift him out of the ground I will never forgive you. Not for one day of my life.*

*You think you asked them. How would you know what a yes from the dead sounds like? How would any of us?*

That one was signed. Not with a name. With a small ink drawing of a candle.

Yuanshu held it up. "Do you know what this is?"

Xiaoyu glanced at it and made a face. "The Quiet," she said. "They're like a church. Kind of. They don't have a building. They just light candles. Grandma Fang down the street was one." She tapped a pen against her teeth. "They believe when you die, you go quiet. That's the whole point. You're done. You've earned it. So waking up the dead is like... shaking someone who just fell asleep after a forty-year shift." She pointed the pen toward the window. "They're at the cemetery. Since yesterday. Go look."

---

He went at dusk.

He did not take a car. He walked the last kilometer up East Market Road in a gray hooded jacket, past the noodle shop with the TV still showing the footage, past a red dumpling cart with steam pouring off it where Dabao's mother was shouting prices at the evening crowd.

He heard the cemetery before he saw it.

Not voices. The absence of them. East Market Road was loud every evening of the year, with motorbikes and vendors and music from open windows, and in the last hundred meters before the cemetery gate the noise just stopped, as if the street had stepped into a church.

There were people on both sides of the iron gate.

Hundreds. Sitting on the pavement, on folding stools, on flattened cardboard. Old women in cardigans. Young men in work clothes. A father with a small sleeping boy across his knees. Every one of them holding a candle in a paper cup, and every candle lit. Nobody had a sign. Nobody was chanting. They just sat there, side by side, in the warm summer dark, with their small lights, facing the stones.

Six hundred tiny flames, all leaning the same way in the breeze.

Yuanshu stopped at the edge of the light.

At the gate itself, on a low folding stool, sat a small elderly woman in a plain gray tunic. Her hair was cropped close to her skull, white as salt. She was not holding a candle. She had a cloth bag at her feet, and she was taking new candles out of it one by one, lighting them from her own, and passing them to whoever came up the road.

She looked up.

He hadn't taken his hood down. It didn't matter. She looked straight at his face in the candlelight and held out an unlit candle.

"Sit down," she said. "If you're going to stand there, you'll make people nervous."

He sat on the pavement beside her stool.

She lit the candle from her own and put it in his hands. The paper cup was warm.

"I'm Ning," she said. "Some people call me Sister Ning. I don't mind either way."

"Zhou Yuanshu."

"I know." She went back to her bag. Took out another candle. "You came to see if we hate you."

"Do you?"

"No." She lit the next candle, passed it to a teenage girl who had come up the road alone, and patted the girl's hand. "We're afraid of you. That's different. Fear can be talked to. Hate just shouts."

They sat for a while.

Through the bars of the gate, the eastern field lay dark and still. Somewhere in there was stone No. 7, with a hollow in the earth in front of it. Old Seven was not in the grave. He was three paces behind Yuanshu, in the shadow of the wall, where nobody but Yuanshu could see him, standing guard with his spear grounded.

"How do you know they said yes?" asked Sister Ning.

She wasn't accusing him. She asked the way a doctor asks where it hurts.

"I felt it."

"You felt *something.*" She turned the candle in her fingers. "When my husband was dying, at the end, he said yes to everything. Yes to water. Yes to the window being open. Yes, yes. He'd have said yes to a horse." She smiled a little. "It wasn't him anymore saying it. It was the part of him that was tired and wanted to be agreeable. How do you know the dead aren't like that? Just echoes, being polite?"

Yuanshu looked down at his candle.

> **She is asking a real question.**
>
> **I do not have a certain answer.**
>
> **I want to be honest about that.**

"I don't know," he said. "Not for certain."

Sister Ning nodded slowly, as if that were the first correct thing anyone had said to her all week.

"Then here's what I'd ask," she said. "Don't promise me you're right. Promise me you'll keep asking that question. Every time." She looked at him. "And let someone who isn't you watch, when you do it."

He thought about it.

"All right," he said.

"All right?"

"Next time I raise anyone who has a family, the family can be there. And so can you."

She studied him for a long moment in the candlelight. Then she reached into her bag and took out one more unlit candle, and put it in his jacket pocket.

"For next time," she said.

---

The woman in the gray suit was waiting for him at the bottom of Mount Qingyun when he came back.

She was leaning against a dark car under the one streetlight, with a folder under her arm, and she straightened up when she saw him, the way people do when they've been told exactly what you look like.

"Mister Zhou." She held out a hand. "Catherine Moore. Atlantic Federation, Office of Strategic Talent. Five minutes. I'll be quick."

Old Seven stepped out of the dark behind Yuanshu's shoulder.

Catherine Moore looked at the skeleton for a long second. Then, impressively, she did not step back.

"The Federation is offering you citizenship," she said, "a guaranteed seat at the Avalon Institute, protection for you and your sister, unlimited research funding, and access to the Federation's Law Relic archive. Forty-one relics. Huaxia has nine." She held out the folder. "That's the first page."

He did not take it. "And the second page?"

She was quiet for a moment.

Then she smiled, a little tiredly. "They told me you'd ask that." She opened the folder and showed him. "Risk Containment Protocol. If the Federation ever assesses you as an existential threat, you agree to voluntary sedation and relocation to a secure facility. It's standard for high-rank talents in our system. It's never been used."

"Has it ever been signed by someone the Stele couldn't rate?"

"No. You'd be the first."

He shook his head, gently. "Thank you for showing me. I don't sign things that end with me asleep."

She closed the folder. She didn't argue.

At the door of her car, she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning around, "I'd have said no too. And Mister Zhou: the Office of Strategic Talent isn't the only office in the Federation that's interested in you. Some of the others don't send people with folders."

The car pulled away into the dark.

---

His phone buzzed on the walk up the mountain.

An unknown number. One line.

*The Quiet asked my grandfather to sign a petition against you. He tore it up. I'd have torn it up first.*

He looked at it for a long time under the pine trees.

Then he saved the number under *Shen Lingyue,* and put the phone away, and took out the candle Sister Ning had given him, and turned it over in his fingers all the way back to the gate.
