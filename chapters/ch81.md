## Chapter 81: The Letter

The train from the Northern Union came into Lingzhou Central at 6:14 in the morning, eleven hours late, with snow still packed on its roof and icicles hanging from the doors like teeth.

Yuanshu was on the platform. So was half the Ossuary.

They had not been asked to come. Sister Ning had told no one. But somehow, by five in the morning, the long cold platform under the station's iron arches was lined with people. Quiet. Holding candles. Unlit. Volunteers from the Listening Pavilion. Nurses from the Institute ward. Fat Tan in his enormous padded coat. Ma Jun, in his delivery uniform, with his cap in both hands, standing at the very front and not looking at anyone.

And in the middle of all of them, in a wheelchair with a blanket over his knees and a hospital band still on his wrist, a thin nineteen-year-old with a narrow face, holding a school notebook with a rocket ship drawn on the cover.

Andrei Belov had been awake for forty-one days.

He still could not stand for long. His legs had forgotten how. He still woke at night sometimes, Ma Jun said, and turned his head on the pillow toward the window, three degrees, and had to be talked back. He still did not like silver things, or rain, or anyone humming.

But he had asked to come to the station. And he had asked Sergeant Vera Sokolova to push the chair.

She stood behind it now in her white winter fatigues, very straight, with her ice-gray eyes on the train.

---

The doors opened.

People got off. Northern travelers in fur hats with bundles and suitcases. A soldier. Two students. An old man with a dog in his coat. They stepped down onto the platform and saw the crowd with its candles and stopped, bewildered, and edged past it.

Then nobody else got off.

The platform was quiet. Steam hissed under the carriages. A pigeon flew up into the iron arches.

Belov's hands had gone white on the notebook.

And then, at the very last door of the very last carriage, a thin woman in a gray headscarf and a long gray coat stepped down onto the platform. Slowly. Carefully. Holding the rail with one hand, the way people do who have never been on a train before and are not sure the ground will stay still.

She had a cat basket in her other hand.

She looked up the long platform. At the crowd. At the candles. At the wheelchair.

She did not run. She could not have run. She walked. Very slowly. Down the platform, past a hundred silent people, with the cat basket bumping against her knee and her face perfectly still, the way faces go when they are holding something so large that they cannot let any of it out yet.

She stopped in front of the wheelchair.

Galina Belova looked down at her son.

He looked up at her.

Neither of them said anything for a long, long moment.

Then she set down the cat basket on the platform, very carefully, and knelt on the cold concrete in her long gray coat, and took his face in both her hands, the way Zhou Wanqing had taken Yuanshu's face in an endless field above the sky.

"*Andryusha,*" she said.

That was all. Just his name. But she said it the way you'd say it if you had been saying it to an empty room every night for a year.

---

Yuanshu looked away.

So did everyone. All down the platform. A hundred people turning their faces up to the iron arches, or down to their candles, or to each other, so that a mother and her son could have this one minute without anyone watching.

> **Nobody told them to look away.**
>
> **They just did.**
>
> **I think that is the Quiet, Yuanshu. Not the candles. Not the silence. That.**

When he looked back, Galina Belova was still kneeling on the platform, holding her son's hands, and Belov was holding out the notebook.

"*I didn't finish the letter,*" he said, in Northern. His voice was still thin and cracked. Seed translated it quietly. "*At Grid Nine. I was writing it when... I didn't finish.*" He swallowed. "*I want to finish it. Out loud. Now. To you.*"

His mother looked at him. Then she nodded, once.

He opened the notebook to the last page.

---

He read it slowly. In Northern. The whole thing, from the beginning, in his thin cracked voice, while his mother knelt on the concrete in front of him and the steam hissed under the train and a hundred people stood on the platform with their unlit candles, listening to words they couldn't understand.

*Mama. It's very cold here and the food is terrible. Don't worry. I have the scarf.*

*There's something in the sky.*

*Some of the men are acting strange.*

*I had a dream last night that someone was asking me questions.*

He got to the end. To the line that stopped. *Mama, if anything happens, I want you to know.*

And he stopped. And looked up from the page at her.

And finished it.

Not reading. Talking. In a voice that was still thin and still cracked but steady now, steadier than it had been in forty-one days, he told her what he'd wanted her to know. Yuanshu didn't ask Seed to translate. Some things are not yours to hear.

But at the very end, Belov turned in the wheelchair, and looked over his shoulder at Sergeant Sokolova, standing behind him. And she stepped forward, and took something out of her coat, folded small, and gave it to him.

A page. Torn carefully from the back of a black leather notebook. Written in pencil, in Yuanshu's handwriting, from a field above the sky.

Belov read it. His mother watched his face as he read.

*Tell Andryusha it's Pasha. Tell him the sergeant was wrong. It wasn't an atmospheric effect.*

*Tell him I said it was aliens, and I was wrong too.*

*Tell him he was right. It was beautiful.*

*It was like a ring someone dropped.*

Andrei Belov put his face down on the page and wept, and his mother held his head against her shoulder on the cold platform, and the cat in the basket at her feet complained loudly about all of it.

---

Sister Ning lit her candle.

From hers, Fat Tan lit his. From his, a nurse. From hers, Ma Jun. Down the platform, one by one, a hundred small gold flames, in the gray light under the iron arches, while the train steamed and the pigeons wheeled and a woman from a farm in the Northern Union knelt on the concrete holding her son.

That was all.

That was the whole thing.

Yuanshu stood at the back of the crowd with Old Seven behind him and the white staff in his hand, and thought that he would remember it longer than anything that had happened on the ice.

---

The hospital room was on the fourth floor of the Institute, in the new ward Doctor Kwan had set up, with a window that looked out over the river toward the sea.

It was the only room in the ward with a lemon tree on the windowsill.

Gu had sent it. From the Bureau's holding cells, through his lawyer, with a handwritten note that said only: *For Wanqing. It needs water on Thursdays. Lu does it wrong.*

Zhou Wanqing was sitting up in bed when Yuanshu came in, with her hair down for once, gray at the temples now in a way it had not been seven years ago. She was thin. Much too thin. Her hands lay on the white blanket like two small birds. But her eyes were exactly the same. Quick and dark and sharp, missing nothing.

Xiaoyu was asleep in the chair beside the bed, curled up in his old hoodie, with her head on the blanket next to their mother's hand. She had slept in that chair for forty-one nights. Nobody had been able to make her leave.

"She snores," said Wanqing quietly. "She didn't used to snore."

"She does now," said Yuanshu. "Since she was twelve."

His mother looked down at the sleeping girl for a long moment. At the too-big hoodie. At the green flask, mended with white tape, standing on the bedside table.

"I missed that," she said. "The snoring starting." Her voice did not change. "I missed a lot of things."

He sat down on the edge of the bed. He didn't know what to say. He hadn't known for forty-one days. He came every day, and sat here, and mostly didn't say anything, and she didn't seem to mind.

---

"The station," said Wanqing. "Belov's mother."

"How did you know?"

"I heard." She tapped her temple, lightly, with one thin finger. "Not like before. Not like the field. Not everything." She paused. "But some things. Sometimes. Loud things. Things that matter to people a long way off." She looked out of the window at the river. "Something came back down with me. A little piece of the field. I don't know what it is yet." Her mouth moved, very slightly. "Kwan's running tests. She's very excited. She's not supposed to be excited about patients."

> **I can feel it too.**
>
> **When I am close to her. A thread. Not like the hollows. Not going up.**
>
> **Going north. To the small white ring.**
>
> **It is very, very faint. And it is not listening to her.**
>
> **I think it is listening for her. Like a phone left off the hook, in case she calls.**

"It's the keeper," said Yuanshu.

"I know." Wanqing looked at him. "I think it's lonely, Yuanshu. I think it's very, very lonely. It kept everyone for six hundred and forty million years, and now most of them have gone quiet, and it's sitting up there in the north in an empty field with fifty-two old friends and a door left open." She was quiet for a moment. "And I think it's frightened."

"Of the four."

"Of the four." His mother looked at him with her quick dark eyes. "I saw them. In the field. At the end. Before I went quiet. The keeper showed me. Not on purpose. It was so frightened, it couldn't help it. It was thinking about them very loudly." She took a breath. "They're not like it. They weren't born from a yes. They weren't born from anything. They were *built.* For one thing."

"What thing?"

Zhou Wanqing looked out of the window. North. At the gray sky over the river.

"Fuel," she said.

---

Xiaoyu stirred in the chair, and mumbled something, and woke up, and lifted her head off the blanket with a crease on her cheek from the sheet. She looked at her mother. Then at her brother, sitting on the edge of the bed. Then back at her mother.

"You're talking about the scary thing again," she said. "I can tell. You both get the same face."

"We do not," said Wanqing.

"You do. It's like this." Xiaoyu made a face. It was, Yuanshu had to admit, exactly right.

Their mother looked at her for a long moment.

Then Zhou Wanqing, who had stood alone in an endless field above the sky and gone loud to save her son, and been squeezed by the oldest thing in the universe for seventy-one days without breaking, put her thin hand over her eyes and laughed until she cried.

---

That night, at the bottom of the Ossuary vault, Seed counted.

Not the pulse. There was no pulse anymore.

Something else.

> **The four.**
>
> **I can see them. Faintly. Every night now. When the sky is clear.**
>
> **They are very far away. So far I cannot measure it.**
>
> **But I have been watching them for forty-one nights. And I have been drawing lines.**
>
> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **One of them is closer than the others.**
>
> **And it is not drifting.**
>
> **It is coming straight here.**
