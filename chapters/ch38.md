## Chapter 38: Heishui Again

The gray crystal found it.

It happened on a Tuesday afternoon in the cold blue room on the second sublevel. Yuanshu was sitting at the desk at the back, beside the steel case, the way he did most afternoons now, talking to the three thousand and ninety-four minds who had stayed. Not reaching. Just talking. Telling them the date. The weather. That the market at the north gate was closed now, its benches empty, its lanterns cut down by Captain Lin's soldiers and burned in a heap on the glass. That a Northern boy named Andrei was in a clean bed on the third floor and somebody had left him a paper crane.

Seed said they listened. Seed said it helped.

And at 3:12 that Tuesday, in the middle of a sentence, the gray crystal in its steel case began to pulse very fast.

Not toward him. Away. West.

> **They are pointing.**
>
> **I asked them once whether they could feel other crystals. Other sacks. They said yes, faintly, the way you can hear people talking in another room.**
>
> **They can hear one now. No. Three. Close together. West of here. Ninety kilometers.**
>
> **And one of the three thousand is shouting.**
>
> **"That's him. That's Dagang. I can hear him telling the one about the general and the goat."**

---

The town at the bottom of the valley still said *Heishui Coal & Power* on the sign at the top of the hill. The sign had changed. It said *ZERO DAYS WITHOUT A FATALITY* now, in new paint. Gao Yinping had changed it herself the morning Number Three reopened without its core. *So everyone remembers we're starting over,* she'd said.

Number Three had a new lift cage, two new rescue robots that looked like armored spiders, and a long line of living men in yellow hard hats going in on the morning shift.

It was not the mine they had come for.

They had come for Number Seven.

Number Seven was at the far end of the valley, a long way past the others, up a bad road through dead pines. It had been closed for fifteen years. Flooded. Dangerous. The records said so. Six weeks ago, according to the provincial registry, it had been bought by a company called Western Ridge Mining, which had been incorporated nine days before that, and which had an office address that turned out to be a closed laundromat in the port district.

At five in the morning, in a thin gray drizzle, the bad road up to Number Seven was full of vehicles.

---

They had done it properly this time. That was the point.

Two Bureau cars in front, with Director Lu in the first one holding a signed order under Article Nine. Behind them, Captain Lin Wen's Defense Corps in three armored trucks. Behind *them,* the dark green Western War Zone jeep, with Qin Hao in the passenger seat holding his military warrant on his knee and staring straight ahead through the windshield, not speaking, his jaw tight. Then a battered white minivan from the Ossuary, with Dabao driving and Sister Ning in the back with her candle and her notebook, and the steel case with the gray crystal in it strapped into the seat beside her like a passenger.

Yuanshu sat in the front of the minivan.

Old Seven sat in the back with Sister Ning, folded up awkwardly on the narrow seat with his knees around his skull and his spear lying across the aisle, and she had given him a blanket against the cold, which he did not need, and which he had not taken off.

> **She told him it looked like he was cold.**
>
> **He does not feel cold. But he kept the blanket.**
>
> **I think he likes her.**

---

The gate of Number Seven was chained. Captain Lin cut the chain with bolt cutters. A Bureau officer read Article Nine out loud through a megaphone at the dark shaft head and the closed site office, twice, to nobody. It was very formal and a little bit absurd and Yuanshu understood, watching it, exactly why it mattered.

*This time we're doing it by the rules. So nobody can say we didn't.*

The site office was empty. Paperwork on the desk. A coffee mug, cold. A calendar from a laundromat.

The shaft head was not.

When Captain Lin's soldiers rolled back the steel door of the winding shed, violet light came pouring out of the hole in the ground, pulsing, slow, like breath. And up the shaft, faint, from three hundred meters down, came the sound Yuanshu had heard once before in another mine at the other end of this valley.

*Clack-clack. Clack-clack.*

---

He went down with Old Seven, Qin Hao, Sister Ning and the steel case.

Sister Ning had insisted. Captain Lin had argued. Sister Ning had sat down on the floor of the lift cage, put her candle in her lap, and said she would be getting up when they reached the bottom and not before, and Captain Lin had looked at Yuanshu, and Yuanshu had shrugged.

The cage dropped through the dark. The violet light grew. The clacking grew.

At the bottom, it was Heishui Number Three all over again. A long black tunnel. Rails. Carts. And bent over the carts, pushing, in a long silent line, the dead: fifty, sixty, more, with violet points of light in their eye sockets.

But these weren't miners.

> **These remains came from the Wastes. Settlers. Soldiers. Scavengers. Bought from the market before it closed.**
>
> **Someone stockpiled them.**
>
> **Someone knew the market was going to close.**

At the end of the tunnel, in the chamber where the conveyor began, three steel housings were bolted into the rock wall in a row. Three crystals. Each one the size of a man's head. Each one pulsing violet out of time with the others. Each one with a ring of screaming mouths burned into its face.

In the steel case in Sister Ning's arms, the small gray crystal was pulsing so fast it was almost a buzz.

> **The middle one.**
>
> **They are certain. The middle one.**

---

Qin Hao walked up to the middle housing.

He stood in front of it in his red jacket with his hands at his sides. The violet light washed over his face in slow waves. Around them, the clacking of the carts went on and on.

"I don't know how to do this," he said quietly. "Tell me how to do this."

"Put your hand on it," said Yuanshu. "Seed will carry your voice. Just talk."

Qin Hao laid his palm on the warm steel of the housing.

"Sergeant Wu," he said.

Nothing. The violet pulse. The clack of the carts.

"Sergeant Wu Dagang. Western Pass. It's Qin Hao. The general's grandson." He swallowed. "The one you called Little Matchstick. In front of the whole convoy. For four months."

> **...**
>
> **Oh.**
>
> **Yes. He is there. He is very loud.**

"He says," said Yuanshu slowly, listening, "'Little Matchstick. They sent *you?* We're all doomed.'"

Qin Hao made a sound that was half a laugh and half something that hurt.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, they sent me."

---

They did it the way they had done it under the bridge.

First the dead. Yuanshu put his hand on each housing in turn and reached past the hammering violet threads into the gray place where the river ran home, and opened the door. *Rest. Lie down. You can stop now.* Down the long black tunnel, the carts squealed to a stop, and sixty dead settlers and soldiers and scavengers from the Wastes knelt down between the rails and lay down and were still. The violet light went out of their eyes. The clacking stopped.

In the silence, Sister Ning lit her candle.

Then the crystals.

Not a push. A door. A small gray door in the dark beside each housing, opened, and left open.

Sister Ning sat on an upturned bucket by the conveyor belt with her candle in her hands and watched it lean. Toward the door. Away. Toward the door. Away. Over and over, flickering, slow at first and then faster and faster, as thousands upon thousands of minds that had been screaming in the dark for months found an opening, and went through it, one at a time, toward rest.

From the middle housing, one mind did not go.

> **He says he's not going yet.**
>
> **He says: "Somebody tell Matchstick to sit down. I never finished the one about the general and the goat. Forty-one soldiers died without hearing the end of that joke. I'm not going to be the forty-second."**

---

Qin Hao sat down.

He sat on the wet stone floor of the mine with his back against the rock, beside the middle housing, with one hand still flat against the warm steel, and he listened.

Yuanshu told it. Word for word, in Sergeant Wu's voice, as Seed gave it to him. It was a long joke. It had a general in it, and a goat, and a very confused quartermaster, and a misunderstanding about a medal, and it went on far too long, and had at least two parts that were clearly made up on the spot. Sister Ning listened with her candle in her lap and her face perfectly straight until about two-thirds of the way through, when her shoulders started to shake.

The punch line was terrible.

It was so terrible that Qin Hao, sitting on the floor of Heishui Number Seven with tears running down his face into his collar, laughed until he couldn't breathe. Laughed until he was bent double. Laughed the way you only laugh at a funeral.

"That's *awful,*" he said. "That's the worst joke I've ever heard. Sergeant, that's *awful.*"

> **He says: "I know. That's why it took four months to set up."**
>
> **He says: "Tell the general I said hello. Tell him the Western Pass wasn't his fault."**
>
> **He says: "Tell him Little Matchstick grew up."**
>
> **He says: "Okay. I'm going now."**

On her upturned bucket, Sister Ning's candle leaned toward the dark, one last time, a long slow bow.

Then it straightened.

---

They came up out of Number Seven into gray daylight and drizzle.

At the top of the shaft, Director Lu was standing in the rain beside the Bureau car with his order in his hand, looking at a man in a cheap suit sitting in the back of a Defense Corps truck in handcuffs. The man had driven up the bad road at seven o'clock, saw the trucks, and tried to reverse all the way back down, and failed.

"Western Ridge Mining," said Lu, as they came over. "Its only employee. He says he was paid in cash. By a voice on the phone. He never met anyone." He folded the order and put it in his pocket. "He says the voice was very kind."

Qin Hao didn't look at the man in the truck.

He walked past the Bureau car, past the jeep, past all of it, to the edge of the cliff where the bad road turned, and stood looking down the long gray valley in the rain toward the sign on the hill that said *ZERO DAYS.*

After a while, Yuanshu went and stood next to him.

"Forty-two," said Qin Hao, without turning. "Sergeant Wu. Private Liang on the bench. And forty more." His voice was steady now. "I'll find all of them."

"I know."

"You'll help."

"Yes."

Qin Hao nodded. He kept looking at the valley.

"It really was the worst joke I've ever heard," he said.
