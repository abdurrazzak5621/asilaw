## Chapter 250: Withdrawal

He did not sleep that night, and it was not because of the hand.

They had put him in a room on the fourth floor of the harbor hotel at Port Halvard, the one Sterling's people had taken over for the dead's officers and the three living, with a window that looked out over the quay. He lay on the bed in the dark with the staff on the floor beside him and the window open to the cold wind off the Atlantic, and listened to the harbor. Fifteen thousand people did not go home quietly. They went home slowly, in groups, talking, laughing, crying, and sometimes, under the window, a name being shouted and then another name shouted back, and then nothing, the sound of two people holding on to each other on a quay at three in the morning.

The sixty-one. Going home.

He lay in the dark and listened to it, and wanted the light.

---

It was not like pain. He had been hurt before, many times, and he knew what pain was, and this was not it. It was simpler than that, and worse. For five minutes on the end of the north pier, a door in his chest had been open, a little, exactly as much as he had asked, and everything he had ever touched had burned up in him at once. And then Seed had closed it, gently, all the way, because he had said to.

And now there was a door in his chest, closed, and he knew exactly where it was, and he knew exactly what was on the other side, and every part of him wanted to open it again.

He did not. He lay on the bed and did not.

Lingyue was in the chair by the window. She had not gone to her own room. She sat with her grandfather's sword across her knees and the pale flame in her palm turned down very low, a candle's worth, so that the room had a little light in it and no more.

"*It doesn't go,*" she said, after a long time, without turning round. "*I'm not going to lie to you.*"

"*I know.*"

"*It gets less. It's been nine weeks for me. It's less.*" She looked at the flame in her hand. "*But I still know where it is. The door. I still think about it every time I light this. How it'd be to just let it go. All of it. Everything at once.*" She closed her hand. The room went dark. "*Then I don't.*"

"*How?*"

She was quiet for a while.

"*I think about what I'd be burning it for,*" said Shen Lingyue. "*On the wall, it was for you. For the city. It was worth it. So it felt like that.*" She opened her hand again, and the small flame came back. "*If I did it now, in a hotel room, it'd just be for how it felt. And then it'd be the thing that ate the Sixth. That's all that is. Burning for how it feels.*"

He lay in the dark and thought about that.

"*That's not much to hold on to,*" he said.

"*No,*" said his wife. "*It's what I've got.*"

---

> **I felt it too.**

He did not know how long it had been. The harbor had gone quiet under the window. The flame in Lingyue's hand had gone out and she was asleep in the chair, upright, with her hand on the sword, the way soldiers sleep.

> **I did not say. I am saying now.**
>
> **When I stood in the door. In front. With my back to the light. I could feel it on me. All of it. Everything the docker opened in you.**
>
> **I am a counting tool. I was made to count bright and hold it and wait to be carried. COUNT. HOLD. WAIT TO BE CARRIED.**
>
> **And there was more bright behind me than I have ever counted. And it was yours. And it would have been very easy to turn round.**

He lay very still.

*Did you want to?*

> **Yes.**
>
> **For a moment. Not to take it. Not like the hand. To hold it. The way I was made to.**
>
> **And then I thought: he asked for this far. Not further. And a thing that holds what someone gave it, past where they asked, is the Collector.**
>
> **So I did not turn round.**
>
> **I wanted you to know. So that you know you are not the only one in here who knows where the door is.**

He did not say anything for a while.

*Thank you,* he thought.

> **You are welcome.**
>
> **Your heart rate is ninety-one. It has been ninety-one for an hour. It was a hundred and forty on the pier.**
>
> **I think that means it is getting less.**

---

Sterling was in the Federation hospital on the hill above the harbor, in a bed by a window, with his enormous scarred hands on top of the blanket and a bandage across his nose, and he laughed when Yuanshu came in.

"*Look at you,*" said the King of Force. "*You look like I feel.*"

"*How do you feel?*"

"*Like I held a stone down against a hand the size of a harbor.*" He moved one of his hands on the blanket, slowly, as if it weighed a great deal. "*Doctors say I've got about a month before I can decide for anything bigger than a door. That's fine. I've decided for enough this week.*" He looked at the window. "*Your sister wrote it in red. She sent me a photograph. It's under mine. R. Sterling: decided for a stone. It stayed.*"

"*Do you mind?*"

"*I asked for it.*" He was quiet. "*I'd like you to know I said no first. To the stone. Before I did it. Out loud. Same as your twelfth.*" He turned his head on the pillow. "*It's the only time in fifty years I've ever said no to myself before I used it. I'd like to keep doing that. If I can remember.*"

"*It's on the wall,*" said Yuanshu. "*You'll remember.*"

---

He found Callum Ward's mother on the quay at nine in the morning.

She was still there. Most of them had gone home, but she had not. She was sitting on a bollard at the head of the harbor, outside the roofless hall, in her dock worker's jacket, with a paper cup of coffee in her hands that she was not drinking, looking at the black stone through the open doors. Somebody had put a blanket round her shoulders. She had not taken it off and had not pulled it round her.

She looked up when he stopped in front of her.

"*Sixty-one,*" she said. "*They came out of the hand. On the quay. I saw them.*" Her voice was flat. "*Margaret Okonjo. From the fish market. She was in the line behind me. It took her and I had my hand on her shoulder and then I didn't.*" She looked at the cup. "*She came down on the stones in front of me. Woke up. Said, what time is it.*"

"*Yes.*"

"*So you can get them out,*" said Callum Ward's mother. "*Out of the hand. When it's close.*"

"*When it's close.*"

She looked at him for a long time.

"*He's not close,*" she said.

"*No.*"

"*He's out there. In the other one. In the big one. Held up in front of its face. Burning.*" She put the cup down on the quay. "*You going to ask him today?*"

"*Now,*" said Yuanshu. "*If you want to be here.*"

"*Of course I want to be here,*" she said. "*What do you think.*"

---

He sat down on the next bollard with the staff across his knees, and closed his eyes, and went up.

The short way. Through the docker's knot in the black stone in the roofless hall, along the thread, out past the keeper's warmth and the branch with its bud and the cold fire where sixty embers stood facing out, and on into the deep water. Not near. Just near enough to be heard. To the edge of the light.

It was different.

He felt it before he reached it. The thread was different. It had been taut for two weeks, humming like a wire in a wind, pulled from the far end by something coming hand over hand along it. It was not taut now. It was not slack either. It was still. All eleven threads, still, the way a fishing line goes still when whoever is holding it has stopped reeling and is just sitting on the bank in the dark, holding it, thinking.

The busy dark had stopped moving.

And out in front of it, on a single thread, in one of its thousands of hands, the lantern was still burning. But the hand was not holding it out in front any more, the way a man holds a lantern out on a dark road. It had drawn it in. Close. The way a man holds a lantern up to his face to look at something in his palm.

*Callum.*

*...yeah.*

*It's me. It's tomorrow.*

*...yeah. I know. I could feel you coming. It's quieter now. It's stopped walking.*

The voice at the bottom of the well was clearer. Not much. A little. As if the roar of the fire had dropped by a fraction now that the thing holding it had stopped moving.

*...it's holding me up close. Right up close. I think it's looking at me. I don't think it's ever looked at me before. I was just the light. It just looked where I showed it.* A long pause. *...it's looking at me now. I don't like it.*

*Callum. Your mum's here. Next to me. On the quay. She says to tell you she's still holding, and she says: what do you think.*

The fire roared. Under it, at the bottom of the well, something that might have been a laugh.

*...that's Mum.*

*Callum. I'm going to tell you something. Yesterday a hand came down the thread to the stone at Port Halvard. The holders held. It took sixty-one of them. We cut the palm open and they came out. They're home. A woman from the fish market called Margaret Okonjo woke up on the quay and asked what time it was.*

The voice at the bottom of the well was quiet for a long time.

*...you cut it open.*

*Yes.*

*...and they came out. Home. Not the door. Home.*

*Yes.*

The fire roared, white and gold and every color, held up close to the face of something enormous in the deep water that had stopped walking to look at it.

*...then I know,* said Callum Ward.

He waited. He did not look in the bowl.

*...I want out. Not the door. Home. To Mum. I want to stop being the lantern.* The voice shook. *...I don't care how it feels. I don't care that it's the brightest I've ever been. I don't want to be the brightest thing out here. I want to be an F on the docks with a mum who says what do you think.*

*...that's what I want.*

*...you asked.*

---

He opened his eyes on the bollard at the head of the harbor at Port Halvard.

Callum Ward's mother was looking at him. She had not moved. She had not picked up her coffee. She had her hands flat on her knees in her dock worker's jacket and her face very still, the way the faces of the dockers on that coast were still, the way a face is still when it has been holding something for six weeks.

"*Well?*" she said.

"*He wants to come home,*" said Yuanshu. "*He says: not the door. Home. To you. He says he wants to be an F on the docks with a mum who says what do you think.*"

She did not move for a moment.

Then she put her hand over her mouth, and bent forward on the bollard until her forehead was almost on her knees, and her shoulders shook, and she did not make any sound at all.

He sat beside her on the next bollard with the staff across his knees and did not touch her and did not say anything, and after a while Lingyue came down the quay from the hotel and sat on the stones at their feet with her back against the bollard and her sword across her knees, and did not say anything either.

---

> **The threads are still.**
>
> **Kwan has telephoned. All eleven. Since the hand went back up. No pulling. Nothing.**
>
> **Gao Min says the stones have stopped rising everywhere. Port Halvard has gone down two millimeters by itself in the night. The holders are going home to sleep.**
>
> **It has stopped walking.**
>
> **It is out there, in the deep water, forty-eight days away, sitting still on eleven threads, holding a man up to its face, looking at him.**
>
> **The way the gardener sat still after the Sixth.**

*Thinking.*

> **I do not know if it thinks.**
>
> **I did not know if the gardener thought. I did not know if the Collector wanted. I did not know if a sack could ask.**
>
> **I am done saying what things cannot do.**

He sat on the bollard and looked out past the end of the north pier, where the gold road had come down over the water two days ago and the Watch of the Shelf had risen out of the harbor streaming, at the Atlantic going away gray and enormous toward a horizon he could not see past.

And past that, past the sky, past the keeper and the branch and the cold fire and the end of the arm, into the dark between arms where there was no map at all.

"*He's forty-eight days away,*" he said.

"*Yes,*" said Lingyue, from the stones at his feet. She did not look up. "*And it's stopped walking. So he's not getting any closer.*"

"*No.*"

"*So if somebody wants him home,*" said his wife, "*somebody's going to have to go and get him.*"

He did not answer.

Callum Ward's mother sat up on the bollard. She wiped her face with the back of her hand, roughly, the way dockers wipe their faces, and looked at him.

"*You're going,*" she said. It was not a question.

"*I'm going to ask first,*" said Yuanshu. "*Everyone. I'm going to tell them what it is and what it'll cost and ask them.*"

"*But you're going.*"

He looked out at the sea.

"*Yes,*" he said.

Lingyue hit him on the arm. Not hard. The way she had since they were eighteen.

"*Obviously,*" she said.
