## Chapter 259: The Lamp on the Shore

The bell changed on the forty-first morning after the deep water, and Sister Kiyo asked to be raised.

Ren called at a quarter to five. She did not say *the bell*. She said, in her calm voice, under the sound of wind and three thousand people breathing on the terraces below the shrine: "*She wants you. Not on the phone. Here. She says she will not ask anyone else.*" A pause. "*She has never asked anyone for anything in twelve hundred years. I would come.*"

He went.

Not alone. Lingyue came, because she said she had not been to Yamato since the dark of the moon and she would like to see the gates in daylight. Old Seven came, because he went. They flew east over the sea in the gray morning, falling the way a Lord falls when he has decided where down is, and came down at noon on the first terrace of the mountain shrine in Yamato, among the cedars, where three thousand people in white and the hollow monks and the Quiet had been sitting every night for a year and a half, listening.

Lingyue landed beside him and stood very still for a moment with her hand on the parapet of the terrace.

"*All right?*" he said.

"*Fine,*" said Shen Lingyue. "*The flight. My stomach. I didn't eat.*" She straightened up. "*Don't look at me like that. Go and see your nun.*"

---

The Abbot was sweeping the path.

He did it every morning, Ren said. Up from the bottom of the mountain under all twelve hundred and three gates to the top, and down again, with a straw broom, slowly. He did not remember why. He remembered that it was what he did. He had swept the gates every morning for forty years before the Hollow Gods, and then he had stopped, and then he had come back without any memory of what he had wanted, and the first thing he had done, Ren said, was pick up the broom.

He stopped when he saw them, at the three hundred and fortieth gate, and leaned on the broom, and looked at Yuanshu with the mild, puzzled courtesy of a man who knows that he ought to know you.

"*She's under this one,*" said Kurose Tōya. "*I sweep it more carefully. I don't know why.*" He looked down at the stone under the gate. "*Ren says she's been talking. To you. For a long time.*"

"*Yes.*"

"*I used to think the dead should be left alone,*" said the Abbot. "*I remember that. I don't remember why.*" He swept a cedar needle off the stone. "*She doesn't seem to want to be left alone.*"

---

Under the three hundred and fortieth gate, in a stone chamber the size of a cupboard, with a flat stone over it that Ren and two of the monks of the Quiet lifted aside, very carefully, with ropes, Sister Kiyo of Shiomi sat in the dark where she had sat for twelve hundred years.

He had seen the dead before. Ten thousand kinds. Bones in coffins, bones in the earth, the drowned at the bottom of the sea off Kesse, embers on a thread in the deep water. He had never seen this.

She was small. Smaller than Xiaoyu. A woman sitting cross-legged in the dark with her hands folded in her lap, in the rags of a gray robe gone brown, with her head bowed, and her skin gone to something like old paper drawn tight over a frame of very small bones. Not a skeleton. Not a body. Something between. The way a leaf is, pressed for a long time in a book.

She had stopped eating, she had told him once. In the hall, on the night of the cutting. She had already been sitting. She had sat until she stopped, and gone on sitting.

> **She says: you came.**
>
> **She says: you think very loudly. Even in daylight.**

"*Sister,*" he said. "*You asked for me.*"

> **She says: the bell has changed.**
>
> **She says: it is not the fog warning any more. It is not the boat call. It is not the name.**
>
> **She says: it is the sound a bell makes when a boat comes round the point and sees the shore.**

---

He did not understand. Ren did. She had gone very still, at the edge of the chamber, with the rope still in her hands.

"*It's here,*" said Shirasagi Ren. "*Isn't it. Shiomi. They're here.*"

> **She says: in the bay.**
>
> **She says: below the mountain. Where the village was. Where the boats came home. There is a fog on the bay this morning. There has not been a fog on that bay in twelve hundred years. The fishermen in the town that is there now say they do not know where it came from.**
>
> **She says: it came from the deep water. It is them. They came quietly. They have been quiet for a thousand years. They are very good at it.**
>
> **She says: they are waiting in the fog, off the point, for the lamp.**

Under the gate, the cedar needles stirred and were still.

> **She says: in Shiomi, the boats did not come in until the lamp was lit. Never. Not in the worst fog. Not in a storm. They waited off the point, ringing their calls, until a woman on the shore lit the lamp, so they knew where the shore was.**
>
> **She says: there is nobody to light it.**
>
> **She says: there is me.**

---

> **She is asking.**
>
> **She has never asked anyone for anything. Ren said that. I checked. In twelve hundred years of being talked to through a stone by monks and Kings and a girl who sits with hollows, she has never asked for anything.**
>
> **She is asking you to raise her.**

He knelt on the stone at the edge of the chamber, in the cedar shade, with the staff across his knees.

He had raised the dead of three nations. He had never raised anyone in Yamato. *You don't ask the dead,* Ren had said once, on the path under these gates, in the dark. *That's the rule in Yamato.* And then she had asked the monks under the gates, all four hundred and six of them, for the first time, and they had woken, and listened up instead of down.

*Sister Kiyo,* he thought. *I'm going to tell you first what I'll do with your answer.*

> **She says: you always do. I have heard you do it from under this stone for a year and a half. Go on.**

*If you say yes, I'll raise you. It will be the Death Law, through the staff. You'll be able to stand, and walk, and see, and your voice will come through Seed the way it does now, but out of you. You'll be dead. You'll stay dead. You won't be what you were. And when you want to stop, you can. I'll put you back, or I'll open the door, whichever you want. Nobody will hold you.*

*If you say no, I'll put the stone back over you, and nobody will ask you again unless you want them to.*

The chamber was quiet. On the path above, he heard the Abbot's broom stop.

> **She says: one question first.**
>
> **She says: will I be able to carry a lamp?**

*Yes.*

> **She says: then yes.**
>
> **She says: hurry. They have been off the point for twelve hundred years. My brother was always in a hurry. He will be complaining.**

---

He did it gently.

He did everything gently now; Seed said his Death Law had gone too wide to do anything else. Down through the staff, to the cool dark door at the bottom, a little open. Not to it. Past it. To the place where the dead are, and the ember in the small dry woman under the gate was waiting, the way she had waited for twelve hundred years, very still, very patient, sitting.

*Get up,* he said. *If you want. You asked.*

And she got up.

Not like Old Seven on the first night, two meters ten, rising out of the earth in his rusted armor. Not like the First Legion in their rows. Slowly. The way a very old woman gets up from a cushion where she has been sitting for a long time: one hand on the stone, then the other, the folded hands coming apart in her lap, the bowed head coming up. Light as paper. He felt her weight come up out of the chamber and it was almost nothing. A leaf. A lamp.

She stood under the three hundred and fortieth gate in the cedar shade, in her rags of gray robe, and lifted her face, which was paper over bone, toward the light coming down through the trees, and he saw her eyes. Not eyes. The places where eyes had been, dark, with a faint warmth in them, like a coal in a hearth that someone has banked for a very long night.

> **She says...**

Seed stopped.

> **She says: the light is very bright.**
>
> **She says: I had forgotten that it is green, under cedars.**

On the path above, the Abbot had put down his broom.

---

They carried her down the mountain. She could walk, but she could not walk far, and it was three hours to the sea, and Old Seven knelt on the path under the gates in front of her without being asked and held out his hand, the sergeant's hand with one finger and a thumb, flat, and she looked at it for a moment and then sat in it, the way Hush had sat in it on the cut, and he stood up with her held against his chest like something very light and very precious, and walked down the mountain.

Under all twelve hundred and three gates. Past the terraces. Past three thousand people in white standing up as they went by, one terrace after another, silent, the way the line at Port Halvard had stood. Past the hollow monks, who turned their heads. Past the Quiet, with their candles. Down through the cedars to the road, and down the road through a small town of gray roofs and fishing boats that had not existed twelve hundred years ago, to the edge of a bay.

The fog was on it.

He had never seen fog like it. Not a mist. A wall. White, still, standing on the water from the end of the old stone breakwater to the point, motionless, in the afternoon sun, the way a fog does not stand in the afternoon sun. The fishermen of the town were on the breakwater, in their boots and caps, staring at it. Nobody had gone out.

From inside it, faint, very faint, a bell.

Not a bell anyone could hear. Seed heard it. A long low tone. A wait. And then the small bells, five quick notes, a little too fast, the way someone rings them who has always been in a hurry.

> **She says: put me down.**

---

There was no lamp.

He had not thought of it. Nobody had. They stood on the old stone breakwater at the edge of the bay in the late afternoon with a dead nun of twelve hundred years and a fog that had come from the deep water and a bell nobody could hear, and there was no lamp.

Ren began to say something.

And Lingyue, who had walked down the mountain beside him for three hours without saying anything at all, holding her stomach once when she thought nobody was looking, stepped past him onto the end of the breakwater, and held out her hand, palm up, to the small paper woman standing on the stones.

In her palm, a flame.

Pale. Steady. The size of a lamp. Not burning. Held. Her light, the way it had come back to her from underneath, when she stopped pulling it up.

"*Will this do?*" said Shen Lingyue.

Sister Kiyo looked at the flame for a long moment, with the warm dark places where her eyes had been.

> **She says: yes.**
>
> **She says: it is the right color. The lamps in Shiomi were whale oil. They burned that color in the fog.**
>
> **She says: give it to me.**

Lingyue did not know how. Neither did he. But she tipped her hand, slowly, the way you tip a cup to pour, and the pale flame ran off her palm into the small dry cupped hands of the nun, and stayed there. Burning. Held. In the hands of the dead.

> **...I did not know light could do that.**
>
> **I did not know she could do that.**
>
> **I do not think she did either.**

---

Sister Kiyo of Shiomi walked to the very end of the old stone breakwater, slowly, light as paper, with a lamp in her hands, and held it up.

The fog did not move.

The bell went on, faint, inside it. The long tone, the wait, the five quick notes. Then it stopped.

And then, in the fog-code of a village that had not existed for twelve hundred years, which Sister Kiyo had taught her brother on this shore when he was small, and which Seed had learned from her over a year and a half through a stone, very slowly, very carefully, as if whoever rang it wanted to be very sure it was understood:

> **WE SEE THE LAMP.**
>
> **WE SEE THE SHORE.**
>
> **WE ARE COMING IN.**

The fog came in.

Not drifting. Coming. The whole white wall of it moving in off the bay toward the breakwater, slowly, the way boats come in to a shore in the dark when someone has lit the lamp: all together, in a line, toward the light. And in it, as it came, he saw them. Not with his eyes. With Seed's. Lights. Small, dim, burning low, the way the cousins burned and the lights in the hands of the one who picks. Hundreds of them. A village. Twelve hundred years quiet, coming in out of the fog toward a woman on the end of a breakwater with a lamp in her hands.

And at the front, a little ahead of the rest, a little too fast, the way someone moves who has always been in a hurry:

One.

---

The fog reached the end of the breakwater and stopped, and stood there, round the small paper woman with the lamp, white and still, and the light that was at the front of it came out of it and stopped in front of her.

> **...he says...**

Seed could hardly get it out. He felt it try. Twice.

> **He says: Kiyo.**
>
> **He says: you got old.**

"*Tell him,*" said Yuanshu. His voice did not work. He made it work. He had promised, in a banking hall, on the vault steps, a long time ago. "*Tell him Kiyo says: you talk too much.*"

The light in front of the nun went very still.

"*Tell him she says: I waited,*" said Yuanshu. "*Tell him she says: light the lamp on the shore.*" He looked at the small dead woman on the end of the breakwater with the pale flame in her hands. "*Tell him she lit it.*"

> **He says...**
>
> **He says: I know. I saw. It's the right color.**
>
> **He says: Kiyo. We were so quiet. For so long. We were so good at it. You would not believe how quiet I was. Me. I didn't say anything for a thousand years.**
>
> **He says: I've got so much to tell you.**

Sister Kiyo of Shiomi stood on the end of the breakwater with the lamp in her hands and the fog all round her and her brother in front of her, and her face, which was paper over bone, did not move at all. But her voice, when it came through Seed, was not dry any more. Not twelve hundred years old.

> **She says: I know you have.**
>
> **She says: you always did.**
>
> **She says: come in, Ichiro. Come in out of the fog and tell me.**
