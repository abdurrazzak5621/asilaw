## Chapter 141: The Mountain of Graves

There was no frost on the mountain.

That was the first thing. Yuanshu saw it from the jeep on the last bend of the road, the way he had seen the mountain the first time, all at once, the way you see the sea. Iron-gray slope. Forty-one thousand three hundred and six small gray stones in long straight rows, climbing from the gate to the cloud on the summit like lines of writing on a page. It was the middle of winter in the high west, and the air coming through the jeep's cracked window was cold enough to hurt his teeth, and there was no frost on the stones.

They were dark. Wet. Steaming very faintly in the morning light, the way a road steams after summer rain.

Qin Hao stopped the jeep at the gate and did not take the keys out.

"It started eleven days ago," he said. "A few rows near the top. Then the middle. Now all of it." He looked straight ahead through the windshield. "The snow melts when it touches them. The grass between the rows is green. In January." His hands were tight on the wheel. "Grandfather says it's like the whole mountain has a fever."

---

General Qin Zhenyue was sitting on the grave in row two hundred and twelve.

Not beside it. On it. On the flat top of a small gray stone with his one hand resting on his knee and his greatcoat open to the cold, in the middle of a mountain of steaming graves, the way a man sits on the edge of a sickbed. He had been there, Qin Hao said, for three days. They brought him food. He ate some of it. He would not come down.

He watched them climb the terraces toward him without moving. Yuanshu, with the staff. Old Seven. Lingyue, with two swords on her back, one of them a King's. Adjutant Xu, small and stooped, with his cane. Behind them, on the road, three hundred skeletons of the Spear Corps climbing out of four dark green trucks.

The general looked at the Spear Corps for a while. Then at Yuanshu.

"You're late," he said.

"I know."

"A year late." The old voice was like gravel in a bucket. "You stood on this mountain and asked forty-one thousand of my soldiers what they wanted, and thirty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two of them said yes to you, and then you went to the end of the world and up into the sky and out into the dark, and you never came back to tell them what for." He looked down at the stone he was sitting on. "They waited. That's what soldiers do. They wait to be told."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." General Qin put his hand flat on the stone beside him, the way he had a year ago. "Feel that."

Yuanshu crouched and put his palm on the gray stone of row two hundred and twelve.

It was hot. Not warm. Hot, like a kettle just off the stove, so that he took his hand away without meaning to.

"My son," said the general. "Colonel Qin Zhaolin. He held the inner gate for four hours against a rift that opened inside the pass. He said *only to speak,* when you asked. He said his piece, about a man in a gray cardigan who brought him tea, and he went back down to rest." The old man's jaw tightened. "He's not resting. He's burning. I can feel it through the stone."

---

> **He is right.**
>
> **Yuanshu, I am going to say this carefully, because I am not certain of the numbers yet.**
>
> **Every ember on this mountain is being pulled. Not toward the surface. Down. Toward two points under the summit, about four hundred meters into the rock.**
>
> **The seeds.**
>
> **They have put down roots. Through the rock. Up through the graves. Every grave has a root in it now. Thin. Gray. Like a vein.**
>
> **And the roots are drinking.**

*All of them?*

> **All of them. The YES. The ONLY TO SPEAK. The QUIET.**
>
> **Yuanshu, the Quiet are burning too.**

He stood up slowly.

Eight thousand two hundred and fifteen. He remembered the number. He remembered the moment: standing on this terrace a year ago in the cold, with the staff not yet in his hands, asking a whole mountain at once, and eight thousand two hundred and fifteen of the dead saying *quiet*, and going back down into the ground, and Seed marking every one of them, gently, so that no one would ever ask them again.

*Quiet means quiet.* Article Three. Lu's stone on the hill above the cemetery in Lingzhou, with the word cut into it in Xiaoyu's capitals.

They had asked to be left alone. And something had come into their graves and was burning them.

---

"How long?" he said.

> **I am guessing.**
>
> **The two seeds were planted before the round chamber. Perhaps two months ago. They grew slowly at first. Since the scout's ring, they have been growing fast.**
>
> **At this rate, every ember on the mountain is gone in nine to twelve days.**
>
> **And the seeds are growing up toward the surface. Toward the summit. I think they want to be high.**

*The Abbot said: somewhere high, and give it a voice.*

> **Yes.**
>
> **The Western Pass is the highest ground in Huaxia with this many dead in it.**
>
> **Forty-one thousand embers is a great deal of fire, Yuanshu. Enough to grow a spire much taller than the one in Port Abiri. Much taller than the one in Port Halvard.**
>
> **Tall enough to shout.**

He looked up the mountain, at the rows of steaming stones climbing into the cloud on the summit, and thought about Specimen Zero in the round chamber with its head forced down to the floor, ringing sideways to its sisters for one point three seconds because it could not ring up.

This time, it would not need a head.

---

They went up to the summit at noon.

General Qin came. He would not be carried. He walked up the terraces between his soldiers' graves with his one hand on Qin Hao's shoulder, slowly, stopping every fifty rows to breathe, and nobody tried to make him go faster. Behind him, his chief of staff, Major Zhuo Wenli, a thin, quiet man of about fifty with gray at his temples and a tablet in one hand, who had served the general for twenty years and who kept stopping to put his free hand on a stone, briefly, as he passed, the way a man touches a doorframe on his way out of a house he loves.

"His family's up here," said Qin Hao quietly, when Yuanshu looked. "Not buried. The memorial wall at the summit. His wife and his two daughters. Eight years ago. The silver rain, the same week as my father." He did not say anything else.

The summit was flat and bare, a long saddle of iron-gray rock above the last row of stones, with the pass falling away on the far side into cloud. At the near end, a low stone wall with names cut into it: the soldiers' families. The ones who had been in the pass when the rain came, and had turned their heads three degrees north, and gone hollow, and never come back.

The rock of the saddle was hot through his boots.

Yuanshu knelt and put the staff to the stone.

> **There.**
>
> **Two of them. Four hundred meters down. A hundred meters apart.**
>
> **They are big, Yuanshu. Much bigger than the one in the round chamber. They have been eating for two months.**
>
> **And they are not sleeping.**
>
> **They know you are here.**

The ground under the summit shifted. Very slightly. A shiver in the rock, like a sleeper turning over. Somewhere below them, far down, the pull on the mountain's embers changed. Gathered. Pointed.

At the staff.

"*Oh,*" said Jiang Lan, from the white skull, very dryly. "*Ninety million names. The brightest thing on the mountain. I did wonder how long it would take them to notice.*"

---

They went back down to the gate at dusk, and the general sat down on his son's grave again, and would not come inside.

Yuanshu stood with Lingyue at the bottom of the terraces, in the last of the light, looking up at forty-one thousand steaming stones.

"You can't ask them," said Lingyue. "Can you. They're burning. They can't hear."

"I tried. On the way down. In row eighty." He had knelt at a grave at random and reached through the staff, gently, as he always did. And met a fire. Not screaming. Too far gone for screaming. A soldier's ember, slowly coming apart in the dark, the way a log comes apart in a grate. "It's like the minds in the Hunger. Before the Sixth. You can't ask someone something while they're on fire."

"So what do you do?"

"In Port Abiri, I told the keeper: you don't ask someone in a fire if they want to be carried out. You carry them out. Then you ask." He looked up the mountain. "That's the yes ones. And the ones who only wanted to speak. I can do that. Carry them out and ask."

"And the Quiet?"

He did not answer.

Behind them, at the gate, Major Zhuo Wenli stood with his tablet tucked under his arm, looking up at the summit, at the low stone wall with his family's names cut into it, the way a man looks at a light in a window across a dark field.
