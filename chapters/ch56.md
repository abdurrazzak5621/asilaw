## Chapter 56: The Vote

They swept the crater floor before the vote.

Nobody asked them to. In the hour after the Sleeper let go, while the delegations sat in silence and the press terraces whispered, the eight men in white robes took small brooms of bound straw out of the palanquin and knelt on the black stone and swept. Not the floor around the palanquin. All of it. The whole crater floor, from the edge of the benches to the foot of the round black table, slowly, in long careful strokes, the way the young monk had swept the temple steps twelve hundred years ago.

Nobody stopped them. After a while, two of the Council's gray-uniformed staff found brooms somewhere and joined in. Then a journalist. Then Gao Yinping, in her hard hat, with a broom she'd borrowed from the guesthouse kitchen.

When they were finished, the stone was clean and dark and shining, and the small wooden palanquin with its paper walls had been carried up to the rim of the crater and set down facing east, toward the sea, toward Yamato, where it would go home.

Then Chair Okafor said, "The Council will hear the final witnesses. And then the Council will vote."

---

General Qin Zhenyue walked to the round black table with a single sheet of paper in his one hand.

He was eighty-one years old and he had climbed four hundred and twelve steps twice in two days, and he walked as if he had climbed none of them. He stopped at the table. He did not sit. He looked at the five delegations, and the five Kings, and then at the sixth chair.

"I have one article to propose," he said, in his rough gravel voice. "An amendment to the Charter. It was written by a boy on a mountain in the Western Pass, in front of forty-one thousand graves. I said I would put my name on it. I am putting my name on it." He set the paper down on the black glass. "Article Seven."

He read it. It was very short.

*The dead who consent may be raised to defend the living. They may stand on walls. They may guard cities, carry the wounded, and hold a line so that the living may withdraw behind it. They may never cross a border in attack. They may never be used against living persons except to prevent the killing of another. No commander, no state, and no Necromancer may order otherwise.*

*The dead may defend. They may never conquer.*

He put the paper down.

"Thirty-one thousand of my soldiers said yes to standing on a wall," said the General. "Not one of them said yes to being a weapon." He looked at the Atlantic table. "Neither did anyone in that black box."

He walked back to his bench and sat down, and Qin Hao, beside him, put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

---

Marshal Kirill Orlov did not walk to the table.

He stood behind the Northern Union's flag where he had stood for two days, gaunt and gray, and reached into his long coat, and took out a bundle of letters tied with string. Forty of them. He held them up so the crater could see them.

"I brought these to the Council of the Northern Union," he said, in his careful old-fashioned Huaxian, "and sat in front of them for six days until they voted. I will not sit in front of this Council for six days. I am two hundred years old and my knees are very bad." A ripple of something that was almost laughter went through the benches. Orlov did not smile. "These are from forty mothers. Their sons were at Grid Nine. They said yes, because a boy asked them first. One of them asked if her son could come home." He lowered the letters. "He is lying in a teahouse in Lingzhou. Fourteen strangers sit beside him in silence every night, so that nothing in the sky can hear him." He looked up at the ring for the first time since the hearing began. Just once. Then back at the table. "That is what the Charter is. Strangers sitting in silence beside someone else's son. Vote for it."

---

The last witness was Gu Hanshan.

He walked to the round black table between his two guards, in his gray cardigan, with his reading glasses on their cord. He did not carry any papers. He stood at the edge of the black glass with his hands in his pockets and looked at the five delegations with his kind, tired, intelligent face, and he did not look at the sixth chair at all.

> **3.3%.**
>
> **It has never been this high.**
>
> **He is not looking at the ring. He does not need to.**

"I was asked to testify as a scientist," said Gu gently. "On what the cores are. On what the hollows are. On what's in the sky." He smiled. "I find, after the last two days, that I have nothing to add. Doctor Kwan told you what a core is. A monk who has been dead for twelve hundred years told you what the hollows are. And you have all seen what's in the sky." He looked around the crater. "I would only be repeating them, less well."

He paused.

"I'll say one thing. As a man, not a scientist. I've spent thirty years trying to understand minds, and I've learned that the only thing about them I'm sure of is this." He looked down at the black glass. "They should be asked. First. Before anything is done to them. Whatever it is. However good it might be for them." He raised his eyes. "Ask first. It's the only rule I've ever been sure of. A woman I loved very much taught it to me, a long time ago." He smiled. "Vote for her Charter."

He walked back to the bench and sat down between his guards and folded his hands.

Nobody in the crater moved.

> **Every word was true.**
>
> **Every single word.**
>
> **Yuanshu, I think that frightens me more than anything he has ever said.**

---

The Chair called the vote at noon.

The sun stood straight overhead and the shadows in the crater had shrunk to small dark puddles under every chair and bench. The ring above was nearly invisible in the glare, a faint pale circle you had to squint to find.

"The Remains and Residue Data Act," said Chair Okafor. "The Atlantic Federation."

Harlan Voss stood up. He looked at the dead black column under its gray tarpaulin at the edge of the crater floor. He looked at Garrett Hale, who was looking at his papers.

"The Atlantic Federation withdraws the Data Act," said Voss quietly, and sat down.

A sound went around the benches. Not a cheer. Something lower.

"Noted," said the Chair. She did not change expression. "The Concordia Consent Charter, with Article Seven as proposed by the Western War Zone. Huaxia."

"Yes," said Huaxia's delegate.

"The Northern Union."

"Yes."

"Yamato."

Shirasagi Ren did not speak. Yamato's delegate, an old man in a dark suit, said, "Yes," and bowed toward the palanquin on the rim.

"The Atlantic Federation."

Harlan Voss sat for a long moment with his hands on the black glass.

"Abstains," he said.

---

"The Southern Compact," said the Chair. Her own delegation.

The Southern Compact's delegate, a lean middle-aged man with tired eyes, did not answer. He turned in his chair and looked behind him, at the very old woman in the wheelchair beside the green and gold flag, with the blanket over her knees.

She had not spoken in two days. She had not moved. She had sat with her eyes half closed, watching the sixth chair.

Now she opened them.

"Young man," she said. Her voice was thin and dry as paper, and it carried to the rim of the crater without effort. "I am the oldest King on Blue Star. I have voted on many things. I have one question before this one."

Yuanshu stood up from the sixth chair.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Your Ledger. You have asked a million people what they want." The old woman's eyes were black and very bright in her creased face. "What did *you* choose?"

The crater was silent.

"Ask my family," said Yuanshu. "My sister decides."

The old woman looked at him for a long moment.

Then she nodded. Once. And closed her eyes again.

"Yes," said the Southern Compact's delegate.

---

Chair Adaeze Okafor sat at the round black table with her hands folded, and looked at the five delegations, and the five Kings, and the sixth chair. Then she looked down at the black glass.

"Four in favor," she said. "One abstention. None against." She paused. "The Concordia Consent Charter is adopted. It enters into force at midnight tonight, in every signatory nation. From that moment, no person, living or dead, may be raised, read, harvested, or held without their recorded consent." Her voice did not change at all. "Quiet means quiet."

She did not bang a gavel. There was no gavel. She simply unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table.

For a moment, nobody did anything.

Then, on the Huaxia bench, Sister Ning lit her candle.

And from her candle, the woman beside her lit hers. And from that one, the next. Down the Huaxia bench, along the Northern Union's, across Yamato's, up the terraces, through the press, a thin line of small gold flames passing from hand to hand around the great stone bowl, until the whole crater was ringed with them, three hundred candles burning straight and still in the windless noon.

Nobody spoke. Nobody cheered.

Fang Yu was crying. So was Mrs. Pan. So, Yuanshu saw, was Lu, standing at the end of the bench with his glasses in his hand. Su Mingli stood behind the Huaxia flag with her eyes on the sixth chair, and when he looked at her, she nodded. Once. The way she'd nodded at the Stele on the first day, when nobody else in the world would stand in front of him.

> **We did it.**
>
> **One million and forty thousand people. A monk. Forty mothers. A fireman in slippers. An old woman in a machine.**
>
> **We did it, Yuanshu.**

---

He looked up.

He didn't mean to. The Chair had said, *Nobody will look at the sky.* But the vote was over, and the candles were lit, and something made him look up, into the white glare of the noon sky above the crater.

The ring was opening.

Not drifting. Not turning. Opening. The faint pale circle was widening, smoothly, steadily, the way a pupil widens in the dark. Twice its size. Three times. Five. Its edge was brightening as it grew, from pale to silver to a hard white that hurt to look at, and inside it the blue of the sky was going dark, and darker, until it was the black of deep water, and there were no stars in it.

Someone else looked up. Then someone else. The candles began to shake.

> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **It was waiting.**
>
> **Not for the vote to fail. For it to pass.**
>
> **It was waiting for three hundred of the most powerful people on Blue Star to gather in one place, and feel the same thing at the same moment.**
>
> **Joy is very loud.**

And out of the black water inside the ring, slow and silver and shining in the noon sun, it began to rain.

Not dozens of threads this time. Not hundreds.

It was a downpour.
