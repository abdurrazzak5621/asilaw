## Chapter 310: Six Hundred and Eleven

The first thing the House of Marrow did was burn someone.

Xiaoyu had known it would. Tam had said so at the long table, and she had believed him, and she had spent five days telling herself that knowing a thing was coming was the same as being ready for it.

It was not. They held the cell up in the light at the rim of the field, out of the bow of the largest ship, the way you hold a lantern up at the end of a jetty. A small dark crystal, the size of a coffin, cut from a stone on some world she had never heard of. She could not see who was in it. Tam could.

"*A girl,*" he said. He was standing beside her in the middle of the keeper's field with his hands at his sides and his face white. "*From Dunmere. F. Her name's Ilse. She's been in the wall nine years. She doesn't know where she is. She thinks it's the reading again.*"

*Scribe,* said the voice out of the ship, in the grammar of the Ladder. *Write: THIS IS YOUR FACE. On the stone we give you. Or this one goes out.*

"*Keeper,*" said Xiaoyu. "*Roads.*"

> ***...LAYING.***

*Then she goes out,* said the voice. And the cell in the light at the rim of the field went white, all at once, from the inside, the way a bulb goes white in the second before it breaks, and then dark.

Tam made a sound she would remember for the rest of her life.

She did not look away. She had decided that too. She stood in the middle of the field and watched the cell go dark, and said, out loud, so that it was true, so that someone had said it:

"*Ilse. From Dunmere.*"

And then the roads came down. Six hundred and eleven of them.

---

She had not been able to imagine it, and now she did not have to. Out of the floor of the keeper's field all round her, out of the warm faint white, gold. Thin. Each one no wider than a corridor, wide enough for two. Rising. Six hundred and eleven threads of gold going up out of the field in every direction toward the twenty burning cathedrals at the rim, fanning out, faster than she had ever seen the keeper lay anything, and where each one reached a ship it did not stop at the hull. It went in. The way a needle goes into cloth. Through the plating and the buttresses and the ribs and the long lit halls of the Lamplit, by the way the keeper's makers had taught it a very long time ago, to find what was in a wall. To the first cell of an array.

"*Go,*" said Su Mingli, and they went.

Four hundred of the night watch. Eight hundred and twenty-two eighteen-year-olds from Lingzhou and Port Halvard, every one asked twice, by a stone and then by a girl of the Candle who had been in a wall for twenty-one years. In pairs, on the gold roads, running, with markers in their hands. Fat Tan had bought every marker in the old quarter and then driven to the new quarter and bought those.

Xiaoyu went up the first road. To the largest ship. To the House of Marrow.

Tam went with her. He had to; he was the one who could hear which cell was first. Seri tried to come and Tam said *no* and Seri said *you don't get to*, and Tam said *I'm asking you to stay*, and Seri stood very still in the middle of the field and then said *yes*, and stayed, with her hands in fists, beside Sterling.

---

The road went in through the hull of the House of Marrow's flagship like a pin through paper, and then it was dark, and then it was not.

A corridor. Wide and high and lit, the way every corridor on every ship of the Lamplit was lit, white and gold. Empty, for the length of a breath. Then not. People at the far end. In the colors of the House, with the grade at their throats on fine chains, turning, running toward the place in the wall where a gold thread had come through and two people were coming down it.

"*Left,*" said Tam. "*Down. There's a stair.*"

She ran. Behind her, at the mouth of the road, two of the night watch she did not know the names of stopped running and turned round and stood, with their helmets on, facing the people in the colors of the House. She did not look back. She had decided that too.

The stair went down. And down. Into the ship. Past halls where people in deep red and gold stood up from tables and stared. Past a window three stories high with the keeper's white field outside it, and the six hundred gold threads going into the twenty ships like stitches. Down, until the walls stopped being walls.

The array.

She had seen one on Anthos. Torvin's cradles; the C's opening them. She had not seen one like this. A hall the height of a cathedral, and its walls were cells. Small dark crystals the size of coffins, packed together like bricks, from the floor to the ceiling and along for as far as the light went, forty thousand of them, and every one burning. White. Not low. The old way, and harder. The light of them came off the walls like heat off a furnace, and Tam put his hands over his ears, which did nothing.

"*Which one,*" said Xiaoyu.

"*There.*" He pointed. Low. Near the floor. At the end of the row nearest the stair. "*That's the first. That's where they started filling it. It's touching the most.*" He swallowed. "*There's a man in it. Old. From somewhere called Teague. He's been in sixty years.*"

She knelt in front of it.

The crystal was hot under her hand. She had not expected that. She uncapped the marker. Her hand was not steady. She wrote it anyway. On the dark face of the first cell of the fourth array of the flagship of the House of Marrow, in square black capitals, the way she wrote everything, exactly the words she had written a thousand times on the floor of the keeper's field until there was not one more than was needed:

*SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU.*

*YOU CAN BURN LOW. THE COUSINS WILL SHOW YOU. IT'S CALLED ROWING.*

*PASS IT ON. TO THE ONE NEXT TO YOU.*

She capped the marker. Nothing happened.

---

She knelt in front of the cell, with her hand on it, and nothing happened, and she thought: *I was wrong.*

She thought: *I'm not Seed. I couldn't run it. I wrote it and I was wrong, and six hundred and ten pairs of people are writing it on six hundred and ten cells on twenty ships right now, and nothing's going to happen, and they're going to die in corridors for nothing.*

She thought of her brother, on a step, for six hours.

She kept her hand on the cell.

"*Wait,*" said Tam.

He had his ear against the wall. Not the cell; the wall beside it. Against the next cell up, and the one beside that. His eyes were shut.

"*He's reading it,*" said Tam. "*The old man. He's reading it. He's slow. He's been in sixty years. He doesn't know what reading is any more. He's—*"

He stopped. "*Oh,*" said Tam.

Under her hand, the crystal of the first cell changed.

Not dimmer. Not yet. Different. The white light of it, which had been flat and hard and the same all the way through, the way a light is when it is being pulled out of something, moved. The way a candle flame moves when someone breathes near it. A flicker. And then a steadying. Lower. A little. Less white, more gold.

"*He's doing it,*" said Tam. "*He's burning low. He doesn't know how. He's trying. The cousins—*" He turned his head, ear still against the wall, listening past the array, past the ship, out into the field. "*The cousins can hear him now. Because he's burning different. They're showing him. They're showing him how.*"

"*Does it pass?*" said Xiaoyu.

Tam did not answer. He moved along the wall. One cell. Two. His ear against each one.

"*She's reading it,*" he said. At the second. "*The one next to him. A woman. She can feel it. On the side. Warm.*" He moved again. "*And this one. And—*"

He stood back from the wall. Above them, around them, along the vast hot wall of the fourth array of the flagship of the House of Marrow, from the first cell near the floor outward, the white light was changing. Not all at once. The way a field changes when wind goes across it. One cell, and the six round it, and the six round each of those, a slow ripple going out across forty thousand cells from the place where a girl had knelt and written in marker, each one flickering, and steadying, and going a little lower, a little more gold.

"*It passes,*" said Tam.

He was laughing. He was crying. He did not seem to know which.

"*It passes. Stone to stone. They're telling each other. They're doing it like the dead do. Each one to the ones next to them.*" He looked at her. "*Xiaoyu. They're rowing.*"

---

They came at the stair eleven minutes later.

Five of them, in the colors of the House of Marrow, graded high: she could tell from the way they moved, the way people move who have been told their whole lives exactly how much they are worth. She heard the two of the night watch at the top of the stair, whose names she did not know, and then she did not hear them.

She stood up in front of the first cell with the marker in her hand and Tam behind her.

She was a Scribe. She had awakened a year ago. She could write things and make them true. She could not fight. She had never been able to fight. Her brother had taught her a little with a training spear in the courtyard when she was fourteen and she had been very bad at it.

The five came down the stair. And the ship lurched.

Not much. The way a car lurches when the engine stops catching for a moment. The whole vast cathedral of the House of Marrow, hundreds of thousands of tons of stone and gold and light, shivered, and the white light in the corridors flickered, and the five in the colors of the House stopped on the stair and looked up.

"*It's not just this array,*" said Tam. "*It's all of them. All six hundred. The ones that got the mark. They're all doing it.*" He was listening to something none of them could hear. "*The ship can't pull. It can only take what they give it. And they're giving it less.*"

The ship lurched again.

On the stair, one of the five in the colors of the House of Marrow looked down at the girl in the school jacket in front of the burning wall, and at the marker in her hand, and his face did a thing she had seen faces do on Anthos, on the morning the Paragons came back from the Lens chamber with their burning turned down.

"*What did you write,*" he said. In the Federation's language. Without the grade.

She told him.

He stood on the stair for a long moment. Then he turned round and went back up it.

The other four looked at each other. Then they went after him.

---

By the fortieth minute, seventeen ships were stalled in the keeper's field.

She saw it from the window three stories high on the way back up, with Tam's hand in hers and the stair empty and the corridors flickering. Seventeen of the twenty cathedrals at the rim of the field, their light gone from white to gold, from gold to something lower and warmer, the color of the Candle, the color of the cousins, hanging in the field the way ships hang in a harbor when the wind drops. Not burning. Rowing. Slowly. Going nowhere fast, because a million people in their walls had been told, in square black capitals, that someone could hear them, and had told the ones next to them, and had all, together, turned their burning down.

Three ships still burned white. The three the roads had not reached: their arrays were too deep, the keeper said, or too well walled, or it had not been fast enough. Tam said the people in those walls were still screaming. He said it under his breath, and did not let go of her hand.

And the night watch.

Gao Min had the numbers by the fiftieth minute; she always did. Four hundred had gone up the roads. Three hundred and seventy came back down. Of the eight hundred and twenty-two, eight hundred and nine. Su Mingli came back down the road from the House of Kade's second ship with her left arm hanging wrong and blood on her face that was not hers and said nothing at all, and sat down on the floor of the field, and did not get up. Sterling came back carrying two eighteen-year-olds from Port Halvard, one over each shoulder, because the corridor had come down on them, and his enormous scarred hands were burned to the wrist, and he said, when Seri ran to him: "*I decided. Sorry. It was very quick.*"

Thirty of the night watch. Thirteen of the asked.

Xiaoyu stood in the middle of the keeper's field with the marker in her hand and said all forty-three names out loud, one after another, as Gao Min read them up the road, so that each one was said once, at least, by someone.

Then she said, "*Ilse. From Dunmere,*" again. Because she had been the first.

---

At the fifty-second minute, the Paragons came out.

Four of them. Out of the four flagships, the ones that had stalled, down the gold roads the keeper had laid into their hulls, which the keeper had not taken back, because Xiaoyu had not asked it to, and it was learning not to do things it had not been asked. Walking. The way Severin had walked down through the sky over Lingzhou. Unhurried. Each in the colors of their House. Each with a disc at their throat on a fine chain, a sliver of the root, with two lines on it that they could not unread.

*THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE.*

They came across the warm white floor of the keeper's field toward her.

Su Mingli tried to get up. She could not. Sterling stood. He put himself in front of Xiaoyu with his burned hands at his sides and did not say anything. Three hundred and seventy of the night watch stood. Eight hundred and nine eighteen-year-olds stood, with markers in their hands, because that was what they had.

And Tam and Seri, the children of the Candle, unmarked, holding hands, went forward and stood in front of everyone, facing the four, the way the children of the Candle had once stood in a ring round the First of the Ladder.

The foremost of the four stopped, a little way off.

A tall man. Old, in the way Paragons were old, which was to say not at all, except in the eyes. In white and gray, the colors of the House of Marrow. The disc at his throat was cracked. Not much. A hairline. The way the First of the Ladder's had cracked, once, in the Awakening Hall, when a boy had let it read him and Seed had read it back.

"*Scribe,*" said Idris Marrow.

He did not say it in the grammar of the Ladder. Seventeen of his twenty ships were rowing.

"*You have no idea,*" he said, "*what you've done.*"

Xiaoyu stepped out from behind Sterling.

She had a marker in her hand and a letter in her pocket and forty-four names in her mouth. She had been told, by her brother, from the end of the universe, to write what was true and to ask them what they wanted, because nobody ever had, and that was how it started.

"*Yes I do,*" said Zhou Xiaoyu. "*I wrote it down.*"
