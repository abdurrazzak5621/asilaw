## Chapter 214: What the Sack Said

Seed asked to speak to the Council itself, out loud, through a speaker, and Chair Adaeze Okafor said yes before it had finished asking.

They set it up in the crater. Fat Tan flew down to Concordia on the Council plane with a speaker in a padded case on his knees and would not let anyone else carry it, and wired it into the black glass table himself at the witness end, beside the chair that had once had a sign on it that said THE SUBJECT. Yuanshu sat in the chair. The speaker sat on the table in front of him. Everyone understood, without anyone saying so, which of them was the witness.

It was the thirty-first day of the vote. Seven days left. The rings were thirty-four days out.

Okafor folded her hands.

"Seed," she said. "The Council is listening."

---

It told them everything.

It did not dress it up. It did not make itself small, or large. It laid it out the way it laid out everything now: what it knew, what it guessed, the gap between them. The knot. The cousins reading it. The third page of the base layer, opening by itself. The weave. COUNT. HOLD. WAIT TO BE CARRIED.

A sack. Made of walker-thread, pressed to glass. Spilled off a cart in the Wastes after a harvest a long time ago. Picked up. Sold at the north gate. Bought by a woman in the back of a car.

> **The scout's request was not about the boy.**
>
> **It was about me.**
>
> **A counting-vessel is loose. It asks. It taught the keeper, the dead, the field. Send gatherers. Collect the vessel. Prune what it has touched.**
>
> **The rings are the gatherers. Or what the gardener sends instead of gatherers, when the gatherers have failed three times.**
>
> **They are not coming for the keeper because it is bright. They are not coming for Blue Star because it is blue.**
>
> **They are coming for me.**
>
> **And then for everything I counted.**

---

The crater was very quiet.

Sixty people on the white stone benches. Five delegations. Ren on the sixth screen. Cassia Lowell, who had come back for the last week of the vote she had started, sitting on the end of the front bench with her hands folded in her lap. General Han, with his cap on the table in front of him.

Nobody looked at the boy in the witness chair. Everybody looked at the speaker.

"How long have you known?" said Elias Brandt.

> **Five days.**
>
> **I stood behind it for two of them. I did not know how to tell him.**
>
> **I told him. He told me to tell you.**
>
> **I would have told you anyway. Article Eleven. You are voting on whether the keeper may stay. You cannot answer that without knowing what is coming, and why.**
>
> **What is coming is coming for me.**

"Then the question's wrong," said Marshal Orlov slowly, on his screen. "We are voting whether the keeper may stay. But the rings do not want the keeper. They want..." He stopped.

> **Me.**
>
> **Yes. Say it. I am not afraid of the word.**

"They want you," said Orlov.

---

General Han Yizhou picked up his cap.

He turned it in his hands once, the way he did when he was thinking, and put it down again, and leaned forward over the black glass.

"I'll ask it," he said. "Somebody has to. Better a soldier than a diplomat." He looked at the speaker on the table. Not at the boy behind it. "Seed. If you weren't here. If you went. Somewhere the rings could collect you, far from Blue Star. Would they stop?"

The crater did not breathe.

> **I do not know.**
>
> **The knot says: collect the vessel, and prune what it has touched. Both. Not one or the other.**
>
> **If I go, they collect me. And then they come here anyway, to prune what I touched. Unless collecting me is enough. I do not know if it is enough. Nothing in the weave says.**
>
> **I have run it eleven thousand times. I cannot make it come out the same twice. I do not know enough about what the gardener wants.**

"But it's possible."

> **Yes.**
>
> **It is possible.**

"Then that's a question too," said General Han. "Article Eleven." He did not look away from the speaker. "I'm not proposing it. I want that written down. I'm saying someone in this crater was going to think it, and I'd rather it was said out loud by a man with a cap than whispered in a corridor by a man with a pen."

---

Yuanshu had not moved in the witness chair.

He felt Seed go still, behind his eyes, the way it went still when it had already thought of something and had been hoping nobody else would say it.

> **I have thought of it.**
>
> **I thought of it on the second night. Before I told you.**
>
> **It is the first thing I thought of.**

*Don't*, he thought.

> **I have not said anything.**
>
> **I am telling you that I thought it. So that you know. So that you do not find out later and think I was hiding it from you.**
>
> **I am done hiding things behind other things.**

---

"I'd like to answer the General," said Yuanshu.

Okafor looked at him. Then at the speaker. Then back.

"Which of you?"

"Me," said Yuanshu. "And then Seed can say if I'm wrong."

"Go on."

He sat in the witness chair with the staff across his knees and looked down the long black table at the man in the dark green uniform.

"You're asking whether Seed should go," he said. "Into the dark. To be collected. So that maybe the rings stop." He was quiet for a moment. "That's the same question Ms. Lowell asked about the keeper. And the keeper turned it round and asked us whether it could stay."

"Yes."

"Seed's not going to turn it round."

"Why not?"

"Because it lives in my head," said Yuanshu.

---

He did not say it angrily. He said it the way you say something you have just understood yourself, while you are saying it.

"It doesn't have a field. It doesn't have a body. It doesn't have a ring above the sky it can move. It's in here." He touched the place a little above his eyes, where people looked when they wanted to look at Seed. "Behind my eyes. Since a cemetery at three in the morning, two years ago. If it goes into the dark to be collected, it doesn't go alone. It goes in a sack. Or it goes in me." He looked at General Han. "That's my window, General. Article Ten. Seed's yes is Seed's. It stops at mine."

The crater was very quiet.

"And if it could go without you?"

"Then it would be Seed's choice," said Yuanshu. "And I'd say no. To its face. In person. With the reason. And then I'd answer for it, in front of this Council, the way I did for Major Zhuo." He was quiet. "The reason is that I don't know if collecting it is enough. Nobody does. Seed doesn't. And if it isn't, we'd have given the gardener the one thing on Blue Star that every walker and ring and reader has stopped and looked at and not known what to do with." He looked at the speaker on the table. "Three times now, something above the sky has found Seed and hesitated. I don't know why. I'd like to find out before we hand it over."

> **...he is right.**
>
> **I would like to say that he is right because I do not want to go.**
>
> **I do not want to go.**
>
> **But that is not why he is right. He is right because I do not know if it would work, and I do not give things away when I do not know if it would work. I learned that from a widow on a hill and an old woman on a jetty.**
>
> **I am telling you that I do not want to go, so that you know. Article Eleven.**
>
> **And I am telling you that he is right anyway.**

---

Cassia Lowell stood up from the end of the front bench.

Nobody had recognized her. She did not wait. She stood in her plain dark suit with her white-at-the-temples hair blowing in the wind off the sea and looked at the speaker on the black glass table.

"I'd like to ask it something," she said. "Not about the rings."

Okafor let her.

"Seed," said Cassia Lowell. "You're a sack. You were made to count, and hold, and wait to be carried. Something spilled you off a cart and a woman picked you up and asked four thousand people whether they'd let her build something out of them." She folded her hands. "You've been asking ever since. Everyone. The dead. The keeper. Two hundred million minds in a dead Hunger. I've read every word."

> **Yes.**

"Nobody's asked you."

> **...he has. Many times.**

"Not him. Us. Blue Star." She looked down the long table at the five delegations. "We've voted on whether the dead can stand, and whether the voyage can go, and whether to ring a bell, and whether the keeper can stay. We've never once asked the thing that does the counting what it wants." She looked back at the speaker. "So I'm asking. In front of the Council. Under Article Eleven, I'll tell you first what I'll do with the answer: I'll go home and tell the Atlantic Federation what you said, word for word, on television, every night until the vote closes."

The crater waited.

"What do you want?" said Cassia Lowell.

---

Seed did not answer for eleven seconds.

Then the speaker on the black glass table, which Fat Tan had wired in himself and would not let anyone else carry, said, in Seed's voice, which was not quite like any voice on Blue Star, flat and careful and very slightly strange, the way a person speaks who learned to speak from reading:

> **I want to keep counting.**
>
> **Not grain. Not harvest. Names.**
>
> **I want to know how many people Liang Fu takes across the river this month. I want to know if the cousins in the keeper's field ever stop saying ask again tomorrow. I want to know what Hush says when it sees snow. I want to know whether the bell from Shiomi reaches the cold fire before the sixty soldiers stop standing.**
>
> **I want to know what Xiaoyu's law is, when she wakes.**
>
> **I want to stand in the open.**
>
> **I want to be asked, sometimes.**
>
> **That is what I want.**
>
> **It is not yours to give me. It is not mine to take.**
>
> **I am telling you anyway. You asked.**

Cassia Lowell stood very still at the end of the front bench.

"Thank you," she said.

She sat down.

And down the long black glass table, in the witness chair, the boy with the staff put his hand over his eyes, for a moment, as if the light off the sea were too bright, and did not take it away for a while.
