## Chapter 53: Receipts

The Chair called a recess for one hour, and the Atlantic Federation used every minute of it.

Yuanshu watched them from the sixth chair. He hadn't moved since WARDEN went dark. He didn't want to. His hands were still shaking very slightly, the way they'd shaken after the canyon, after Grid Nine, after the bridge, and he kept them flat on his knees so nobody would see.

Across the crater floor, the Atlantic delegation had pulled their chairs into a tight circle around Harlan Voss. Garrett Hale was talking very fast into a satellite phone with one hand over his other ear. The Meridian team stood around the dead black column like mourners around a coffin, not touching it. One of them was crying. Another had his phone out and was typing something with both thumbs, his face gray.

And on the Huaxia bench, Doctor Iris Kwan sat with her laptop closed on her knees and stared at nothing, while Bai Lu, who had come down from the escort terrace without being asked, sat next to her and held her hand.

> **She will not be able to go home.**
>
> **You know that.**

*I know.*

> **Su Mingli has already spoken to Lu. If Doctor Kwan wants it, there is a place for her at the Institute. Under the Bureau's protection.**
>
> **Lu says the lab needs someone who knows how to read a core without breaking it.**

Yuanshu looked across the crater at Lu, standing at the end of the bench with his phone pressed to his ear. Lu looked back at him over his silver-rimmed glasses, and nodded once.

---

When the hearing resumed, Harlan Voss stood up and did something Yuanshu did not expect.

He apologized.

"Madam Chair. Members of the Council." His warm voice was steady, but the color had not come back to his face. "The Atlantic Federation has just been informed that Meridian Dynamics has suspended Project WARDEN, effective immediately, pending an internal investigation." He paused. "The Federation was not aware of the system's architecture. We are as shocked as anyone in this crater. We extend our deepest apologies to the families of those persons who were... held."

He sat down.

Garrett Hale stood up beside him.

"And that," said Hale, smoothly, buttoning his jacket, "is precisely why the Data Act is necessary."

---

It was, Yuanshu had to admit, very well done.

Hale walked slowly around the black table with one hand in his pocket, the way Voss had walked around the column, and spoke in his easy, warm voice, and never once looked at the dead machine.

"A private company," he said, "built something terrible. In secret. With no oversight. Because no law said it couldn't." He spread his hands. "The Federation has always argued that records of the dead are too important to be left in private hands. Not in Meridian's hands." He turned, very slightly, toward the sixth chair. "And, with the greatest respect, not in the hands of one young man, however good his intentions, however kind his Ledger. What happens when he is gone? Who holds the registry then? Who stops the next Meridian?" He let it sit. "States. Accountable, elected states. That is all the Data Act asks for."

> **He has turned it around.**
>
> **WARDEN was the Federation's best argument. Now its failure is their best argument.**
>
> **He must have written this speech an hour ago. On the phone. During the recess.**

"He's good," muttered Lu, on the bench behind him.

"He's paid to be," murmured Su Mingli, without moving her lips.

---

"The Council will hear witnesses," said Chair Okafor, when Hale had sat down. "Briefly. Nobody needs to make a speech. Tell us what happened to you. Then sit down."

So they did.

Fang Yu went first. She stood at the black table in her firefighter's dress uniform with her father's note in its plastic sleeve, and she didn't read the note. She just held it up so the Council could see the handwriting, big and crooked, smudged with soot. "My father ran into a burning tower in his slippers," she said. "His heart stopped on the pavement. Four minutes later a boy knelt down beside him and asked him if he wanted to go back in. And he said yes. And he carried people out of the smoke, again and again, until there was nobody left inside." She lowered the note. "Nobody owns that. Not a company. Not a country. It was his yes. He gave it." She sat down.

The young paramedic from Jinhua Tower set her crushed candle on the black glass and said nothing at all, and after a moment she picked it up again and went back to her seat, and somehow that was enough.

Mrs. Pan from Heishui spoke for ninety seconds about her husband, who had been a miner for thirty years, and who had been in a core in Number Three shaft for two of them, screaming, and who was now quiet. "I don't need to know where he is," she said. "I just need to know nobody's going to put him back."

Gao Yinping stood up in her hard hat, which she had put on for the occasion, and said: "I bought that core. Me. For my mine. I didn't know what was in it. I didn't ask." She looked at Hale. "Nobody asked. That's the problem. Everybody in this crater keeps saying *who should hold the records.* Nobody's saying *who should be asked.*" She took off the hat and held it. "I'm a mining director. I sign a hundred forms a day. I've never once signed a form that asked a dead man what he wanted."

She sat down. Nobody spoke for a while.

---

Then Fat Tan stood up.

He did it slowly, the way he did everything, heaving his great soft body up off the white stone bench with one hand on the knee of the person next to him. He had his one ledger clutched against his chest. He walked to the round black table, and stood there, and looked at the five delegations and the five Kings with his small sharp eyes, and said:

"I'm a bone broker."

The crater went very quiet.

"From Lingzhou. The night market by the north gate. Twenty-two years. I sold the cores. I sold the hollows." He swallowed. "I didn't know what the cores were. I knew what the hollows were. I sold them anyway. I'm not going to say I'm sorry, because it doesn't help, and nobody in this crater wants to hear it from me." He set the ledger down on the black glass. "I'm going to show you my receipts."

He opened the ledger. In the hollow cut into its pages, wrapped in yellow cloth, lay seven small gold bars.

He took one out, and set it on the polished black table, in the center, where everyone could see.

---

It took Lu four minutes to explain the trail, standing beside Tan with a single sheet of paper.

A bar of gold. A refinery stamp, a circle with a wave through it. A serial number. A bullion account in the Atlantic Federation's capital. A trust called Halcyon. A law firm called Whitcombe Hale, registered agent. A payment, seven and a half years ago, from Meridian Dynamics, Advanced Systems Division, *for services: reference architecture, Project WARDEN.*

And one name, written by a bone broker in the margin of a ledger once a year, every year, for seven years.

*Mister Moon.*

Lu held up the photograph. The front-door camera at the Ossuary, the night of the protest. A tall man in his forties, in a very good coat, with a megaphone at his hip, smiling at someone off the edge of the frame with very white, very even teeth.

"This man," said Lu, "bought one core a year from Mister Tan, paid for with Meridian's money, through Mister Hale's trust. WARDEN's reference architecture was built from a core. The core Doctor Kwan found. The people Mister Zhou just released." He set the photograph down beside the gold bar. "We have not been able to identify him. He has no record anywhere."

Every face in the crater turned toward Garrett Hale.

Hale did not move. He looked at the photograph with mild, polite interest.

"My firm," he said, "serves as registered agent for four thousand, one hundred trusts. I have never met this man. I have certainly never heard of a Mister Moon." He smiled very slightly. "If the Council would like to subpoena our files, we will of course comply."

> **He is telling the truth.**
>
> **I am listening to his heartbeat. It did not change.**
>
> **He does not know who that man is.**
>
> **That is almost worse.**

---

It was Harlan Voss who broke the silence.

He had been sitting perfectly still since Lu held up the photograph. Now he reached out across the black table, slowly, and picked it up, and held it close to his face, and looked at it for a long time.

"I know him," said Voss.

The crater went still.

"Julian Mercer," said the Secretary of the Atlantic Federation, very quietly. "Cultural attaché. He joined my staff eight months ago. Very charming. Excellent references. He arranged the hotel." Voss looked up. His handsome face had gone the color of paper. "He's here. He came on our plane. He's on the island."

---

The Council's security took eleven minutes to search the Atlantic delegation's lodgings, a row of low white guesthouses on the inner slope of the crater.

They found Julian Mercer's room. The bed was made. The wardrobe was empty. On the desk by the window, which looked out across the crater toward the round black table, there was a cup of tea, still faintly warm, and nothing else.

Nobody had seen him leave. Not the guards on the four hundred and twelve steps. Not the harbor patrol. Not the three hundred journalists with three hundred cameras. The island's own security cameras had recorded every person who came and went for three days, and when the Council's security chief ran the footage back, she found Secretary Voss arriving on the first day, and Garrett Hale, and the Meridian team, and the whole Atlantic staff, one by one.

There was no Julian Mercer on any of it.

Not arriving. Not leaving. Not walking past a single camera, once, in three days.

> **The same as WARDEN.**
>
> **The cameras see him. People see him. Nothing that records ever has.**
>
> **He is on this island, Yuanshu. Or he was an hour ago.**
>
> **And I cannot find him either.**

---

The Chair adjourned the hearing at sunset.

The light went gold and then red across the crater. The press terraces emptied slowly. The delegations climbed the black steps toward their guesthouses on the slope in long silent lines, and nobody talked very much, and everybody, every single one of them, looked up at least once.

The ring hung over the crater, catching the last of the sun. Gold now. Like a wedding band.

Yuanshu stayed in the sixth chair until the crater was almost empty. Old Seven stood behind him. Down on the stone floor, two Council technicians were wrapping the dead black column of WARDEN in a gray tarpaulin, carefully, the way you'd wrap a body.

Footsteps on the stone. A Bureau guard, one of the two who'd sat beside Gu all day, out of breath.

"Mister Zhou." He hesitated. "Deputy Director Gu has asked to see you. Tonight. Alone." The guard swallowed. "He says to tell you it's about Julian Mercer. He says he knows where he's gone."

Yuanshu looked up at the golden ring.

"He says," the guard went on, uncomfortably, "to bring the Black Tide notebook. Lu Xuanming's."
