## Chapter 241: What Do You Want to Become

Morning came to Lingzhou the color of strong tea, and by seven there was nowhere left to stand on East Ring Road.

He had thought it would be like his own Awakening Day. It was not. On his own Awakening Day there had been four thousand people inside the hall and the ordinary traffic outside. Today the four thousand were inside by half past eight, every seat, every aisle, and outside there were more: on the steps, on the grass under the trees, along the pavements, on the roofs of the shops across the road, up the lampposts. Quiet. Not a crowd waiting for a match. A crowd waiting outside a hospital, the way the parents had waited the first night. Half of Lingzhou, standing in the summer heat, with nothing to see but the long curved roof of a building and a screen somebody had rigged over the doors.

And above it, low in the north, faint even in daylight if you knew where to look: a small gold point. A bud on the end of a branch nobody could see.

> **Wen Jia.**

He had told it at six, sitting on the edge of the bed with his feet on the cold boards.

*Wen Jia. Seventeen. Lingzhou. She went first.*

> **...thank you.**
>
> **She is in the stone. I can feel the stone now. It is warm. It has someone in it.**
>
> **I do not remember her. I know her name.**
>
> **That will do.**

---

At 9:52, the Number Nine bus stopped outside the Awakening Hall.

Not at a stop. There was no stop there. It pulled in to the curb through a crowd that parted for it without being asked, and the handbrake went on, and the doors opened, front and back, with a hiss.

Wen Hao counted to ten.

Then he turned the engine off, and got out of the driver's seat, and came down the step onto the pavement in his blue Transit Authority jacket with the shiny elbows, carrying, folded over one arm, a girl's jacket in a different blue. Brighter. Never worn. Forty years in a box on top of a wardrobe.

The crowd let him through. Nobody had told them who he was. They let him through anyway.

---

Inside, the hall was the bowl of silence his own had been a bowl of noise.

Four thousand people on the curved tiers. Camera drones hanging overhead, not moving, like silver fish asleep. The private boxes on the back wall empty; Gao Min had locked them, and said that anyone who wanted to sit above the children could sit on the roof. In the front row, at the foot of the platform, where nobody had ever been allowed to sit because it was too close, a woman in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees. Beside her, a bus driver with a blue jacket on his lap. Beside him, a big man from the coal yard with his hands between his knees.

At the official's table at the side, where on his own Awakening Day a man with a tablet had read out names and grades, Gao Min sat with a gray cloth ledger open in front of her. New. Ruled in pencil. She had drawn the columns herself the night before. *Name. Date. What they said.*

There was no column for Talent.

At the foot of the platform steps, facing out, Old Seven. Beside him, in his gray raincoat with his scarred hands in his pockets, Roman Sterling. Sister Ning on her folding stool with her candle lit. And at the corner of the platform, with her arms folded, exactly where she had been standing two years ago when a boy put his hand on the stone and it went black: Su Mingli.

He stood beside her.

"*She made me promise,*" said Su Mingli, without looking at him. "*Your mother. A long time ago. That if this day ever came, I'd be standing right here. I thought she meant you.*" Her arms did not unfold. "*She meant both of you.*"

---

"*Zhou Xiaoyu,*" said Gao Min.

She did not read it off a tablet. She said it from memory, in her flat careful voice, and four thousand people heard it, and half of Lingzhou outside on the screen, and on the open channel, ten other halls, and every radio on Blue Star that was on.

His sister came out of the line.

Seventeen. School jacket, the plain dark one, not the training uniform; she had refused the training uniform. Her hair tied back badly, the way she always tied it. A black marker in the pocket of her jacket, capped, because she had said she was not going anywhere important without one. She walked to the foot of the platform steps and stopped, and looked at her mother in the front row, and her mother looked back at her, and neither of them did anything at all.

Then Lingyue came out of the line behind her.

Not a candidate. Nobody had put her name down. She walked out of the line of eighteen-year-olds in the gray sweater with her grandfather's sword on her back, and the hall saw her, and something went through four thousand people like wind through grass: *the SSS, the Dawnbreaker, the one who crossed out her card*. She stopped one step behind Xiaoyu.

"*I said I'd stand behind you,*" she said.

"*I know,*" said Xiaoyu. "*Obviously.*"

They went up the three steps together.

---

Up close, the Stele was warm.

He could see that from the foot of the steps: the faint shimmer of heat off the black stone in the light of the ceiling lamps. The crack ran down its face from the top to the bottom. The runes were not flat and dull. They had not been since midnight. Every one of them, all over the black surface, was turned and pointing down, into the stone, at the point where it went into the floor.

At someone.

Xiaoyu stood in front of it.

She did not put her hand on it at once. She stood there for a long moment, in front of four thousand people and every camera on Blue Star, with her hands at her sides, looking at it. The way you look at a door before you knock.

Then she said, quite clearly, in her ordinary voice, to the stone:

"*Hello. I'm Xiaoyu. I'm going to put my hand on you now. I'm telling you first.*"

And she did.

---

Nothing happened.

One second. Two. Somewhere high in the tiers, nobody coughed. The drones did not whine. Four thousand people held their breath at once, and he could hear it, the absence of four thousand breaths, like a held note.

Three.

Then the runes moved.

Not toward her hand. Not to point at her the way they had pointed at him, like a crowd turning in a street. They rose. Slowly. All over the stone, every rune lifting off the black surface by a finger's width, the way the hair on your arm lifts when someone you love comes into a room. And light came out of the Stele.

Not white-gold, the way it had burst out for Lingyue. Not black. Soft. Low. The color of a lamp in a window at night, seen from the street, when someone is still awake inside.

Letters formed in the air above the stone.

Not **[Class:]**. Not **[Talent:]**. Slowly, the way they had formed for him two years ago, as if the Stele were searching for words it had never needed. But not cracking. Not breaking through question marks. Forming, one at a time, carefully, in a hand that was not the Stele's.

**[What do you want to become?]**

---

The hall did not make a sound.

He would remember that. He would remember it longer than anything else about the day. Four thousand people, and half a city outside, and ten other halls, and every radio on Blue Star that was on, and nobody made a sound. They read it. All of them. A question, in the air, over a black stone that had told every eighteen-year-old on Blue Star for forty-two years what they were.

In the front row, the bus driver had put his hand flat on the blue jacket in his lap.

Xiaoyu did not take her hand off the stone.

She looked up at the letters for a long time. And then, because she had never in her life been able to say anything important without saying it to someone's face, she looked down, at the stone, at the place where the runes were pointing, at the point in the floor where a girl of seventeen in a blue jacket she had never worn was sitting in a knot.

"*I was scared,*" said Zhou Xiaoyu. "*I was scared you'd say Scribe. Talent D. And everybody would go: oh. And that would be what I was. Because you said so.*"

The letters hung in the air.

"*I've been writing names on a wall for two years,*" said his sister. "*Everybody who comes home. Everybody who doesn't. Everybody who gives something up so that somebody else can stay. My brother's thing forgets them, because it has to. Somebody has to not forget.*" Her voice was shaking. She let it. "*I write them down and I don't hold on to them. They're not mine. I just write them so they're written. So nobody can say afterwards they weren't there.*"

She took a breath.

"*That's what I want to become,*" she said. "*That. Whatever that is. I don't know what it's called.*"

---

The letters above the stone went out.

All at once. The hall breathed in.

And then, slowly, carefully, in the same hand that was not the Stele's, new letters formed. Not words of grading. A question again. Smaller.

**[What should we call it?]**

Xiaoyu stared at it.

Then she laughed. Out loud, in front of four thousand people and every camera on Blue Star, with her hand flat on the black stone and tears running down her face, the way she laughed at the long table when Shi Lei said something stupid. A real laugh.

"*Scribe,*" she said. "*Call it Scribe.*" She wiped her face with the back of her other hand. "*That's what I was scared of. So I want it. I want it to be mine. Not because you said. Because I did.*"

The light in the stone went up.

Not bright. Warm. The lamp in the window, turned up a little, because someone was coming home late.

**[Class: Scribe]**

**[Chosen.]**

**[Talent: — not marked —]**

And then, below it, very small, so small that only the people in the front row could read it, and the cameras, and Gao Min at her table with her pencil frozen over her new ledger, one more line, in the hand that was not the Stele's:

**[Only if it's something good. It is.]**

---

Xiaoyu took her hand off the stone.

The letters stayed.

He saw it happen and did not understand it at first. On his own Awakening Day, when he took his hand away, the black had drained out of the Stele like water out of a basin and the letters had gone with it. Today his sister took her hand off the warm stone and stepped back, and the letters hung in the air above the platform, and did not fade. They stayed there. Written. As if someone had written them down so that they were written, so that nobody could say afterwards they had not been there.

> **...that is her Law.**
>
> **I can see it. Very small. Very new. It does not have a name in any of the Bureau's files. It is not on the tree in the stone. The tree did not have a knot for it.**
>
> **Wen Jia did not open a knot on the tree.**
>
> **She opened what Xiaoyu said.**

At the foot of the platform, Su Mingli's arms had come unfolded.

---

The hall did not cheer.

Not at first. Nobody knew what you were supposed to do. Four thousand people sat in the silence with three lines of soft light hanging in the air over a black stone, and a girl of seventeen standing in front of it with her hands at her sides and her face wet, and the SSS Dawnbreaker one step behind her with a hand, now, on her shoulder.

Then, in the front row, the woman in the wheelchair put both her thin hands on the arms of the chair.

And pushed.

He saw Fat Tan start forward and stop. He saw Wen Hao turn his head. He saw his mother, Zhou Wanqing, who had not stood up out of that chair in a year, get her feet under her on the polished stone floor of the Awakening Hall, and push, with her arms shaking, and her face set the way her daughter's set, and stand.

Not for long. Three seconds. Four. Long enough. Standing in the front row, in front of four thousand people, on her own feet, looking up at her daughter on the platform.

"*I said I would,*" said his mother. To nobody. To everybody. "*I said if it looked at you wrong I'd get out of this chair. It didn't look at you wrong.*" Her knees were going. "*I'm getting out anyway.*"

Fat Tan caught her as she sat back down.

And then the living cheered.

---

"*Shi Lei,*" said Gao Min, when it was quiet enough. Her voice was not quite as flat as usual.

He came up the steps with his wooden training spear, and he did not put it down, and he did not tell the stone he was going to touch it first, because he had not thought of that, and then halfway through putting his hand out he stopped, and went red, and said: "*Sorry. Hello. I'm Shi Lei. I'm going to touch you now,*" and four thousand people laughed, gently, all at once.

**[What do you want to become?]**

"*Someone who stands in front of the coal yard when a rift opens,*" said Shi Lei. He did not look at the stone. He looked at the front row, at a big man with his hands between his knees. "*So my dad doesn't have to. That's it. That's all I wanted. The S was just for the Academy. The Academy was just for that.*"

**[Class: Spear Guardian]**

**[Chosen.]**

**[Talent: — not marked —]**

His father did not stand up. He put his face in his hands.

---

They came all morning. All afternoon.

Not all of them touched it. Some walked to the foot of the steps and looked up at the warm stone and turned round and walked back, and Gao Min wrote in her new column, *chose not to, this year*, and on the open channel a faint voice from the eleventh stone, the one who had been a C, Precise Count, and had asked to count the ones who said no, read out the number every hour. Some went up and put their hands on it and said *I don't know*, and the stone said, in the hand that was not its own, **[Then come back when you do. I will be here.]**, and they came down again, crying or laughing or both, with nothing opened in them, and nobody in the hall thought less of them for it.

And some said things that nobody had ever said to a Stele. *A healer, because my grandmother.* *A Guardian, but a small one, for the school.* *Something that makes things grow.* *I want to be able to hear the dead, like him. Not raise them. Just hear them.* *I want to fly. Just fly. Nothing else. I just want to fly.*

And the stone opened what they said.

On the radio, from Novaya Zarya, Kesse, Yamato, Port Halvard, Port Abiri, all eleven halls, the same. Hour after hour. Children putting their hands on warm black stones on every continent of Blue Star, and being asked, for the first time in forty-two years, what they wanted, and answering.

---

At ten past six, when the last of the line had gone, and the hall was emptying, and the drones had landed, the bus driver came up the steps.

Gao Min did not call him. He was fifty-one and had awakened thirty-three years ago and there was no line for him. But nobody stopped him. He came up the three steps of the platform slowly, in his blue Transit Authority jacket with the shiny elbows, with the other jacket over his arm, the brighter blue, and stopped in front of the Stele.

He did not put his hand on it.

He unfolded the jacket. And laid it over the black stone, carefully, the way you lay a coat over someone asleep in a chair, across its shoulder, where the crack began. The bright blue against the black. Forty years in a box on top of a wardrobe.

"*There,*" said Wen Hao. "*You never got to wear it to the hall.*"

The light in the stone came up. Warm. The lamp in the window.

He stood there for a while. Then he put his hand flat on the stone beside the jacket. Steady. Talent D. Twenty-six years.

**[What do you want to become?]**

The driver of the Number Nine bus looked up at the letters for a long time.

"*Nothing,*" he said. "*I'm fine. I've got a bus. I just wanted to hold your hand.*"

The letters went out. And came back. Not in the air, this time. Small. On the surface of the stone, under his palm, so that only he could see them, and the boy at the foot of the steps with a harvest tool behind his eyes that could see anything written anywhere.

**[Hao-Hao. You're still annoying.]**

**[Come past twelve times a day.]**

**[Count to ten.]**
