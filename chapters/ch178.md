## Chapter 178: Weight With an Opinion

On the first day, Lingyue could not hold a pebble.

Su Mingli put it on a flat stone in the middle of the Academy training ground, a gray river pebble the size of a thumbnail, and stood back with her arms folded, and told Lingyue to put her light on top of it and hold it there.

"Not on it," she said. "Above it. Pressing down. So that if I try to lift it, I can't."

Lingyue lit her sword. Inside the Field, it went pale: silver-gray, the color it went now whenever Yuanshu was within ten kilometers, which was most of the time. She let a drop of the pale fire gather at the point of the blade, heavy, the way she had at the mouth of the harbor at Port Abiri. She let it fall onto the pebble.

It fell through the pebble and into the stone underneath and went out.

"Again," said Su Mingli.

It went through again.

"Light runs downhill," said Su Mingli. "You taught it to fall. Good. Now you have to teach it to stop." She bent and picked the pebble up off the stone, easily, and turned it over in her fingers. "Falling is easy. Water falls. Stones fall. Everything falls if you let go of it. Holding is the hard part." She put the pebble back. "Again."

---

Yuanshu watched from the steps of the main hall with Qin Hao, who was writing everything down.

"She's going to hate this," said Qin Hao, not looking up. "She's never been bad at anything. Not once. Not since we were twelve."

"She was bad at cooking."

"Nobody's ever eaten her cooking. That doesn't count." Qin Hao turned a page and looked up at the training ground, where Lingyue had just watched a fourth drop of pale fire go through the pebble and into the ground, and had said something to the King of Gravity that Principal Yue, in his window, pretended not to hear. "She's going to be bad at this for at least two days. I'm going to enjoy every minute."

On the second day, she held the pebble for one second.

He felt it happen through the Field. A drop of pale fire, falling off the point of her blade onto the gray stone, and stopping. Not going through. Sitting on top of the pebble like a bead of mercury on a table, trembling, silver-gray. Su Mingli bent and put two fingers on the pebble and lifted, and for one second the pebble did not move.

Then the drop went through it and into the stone, and the pebble came up in Su Mingli's fingers.

"One second," said Su Mingli.

"One second," said Lingyue. She was breathing hard. Her face was gray with sweat. "One."

"The scout's blind gap was zero point six two." Su Mingli put the pebble back down. "One second is nearly two of those. In the round chamber, that would have been enough to put a spear in." She stepped back. "Again."

By dusk, she could hold it for four seconds. By midnight, eleven.

At midnight, with the training ground lit by floodlights and the cicadas gone quiet and Qin Hao asleep on the steps with the notebook on his chest, she let a drop fall onto the pebble and it sat there, silver-gray, heavy, and Su Mingli bent and put her whole hand around the pebble and pulled, and the pebble did not move, and the King of Gravity straightened up and looked at Shen Lingyue across the stone.

"Good," she said.

It was the first time Yuanshu had ever heard her say it to anyone.

---

On the third day, Su Mingli took away the pebble and put a training dummy on the stone.

Straw and sacking and a wooden post, two meters tall, with a painted face. She stood behind it and put her hand on the back of its head.

"That's the lamp," she said. "It's going to try to turn. I'm going to turn it. Hold it down."

Lingyue held it down for two seconds before the dummy's head came up under Su Mingli's hand and the pale fire slid off it like water off a roof.

"It's not a pebble," said Su Mingli. "A pebble doesn't want anything. A lamp wants to point. You're not holding weight down, you're holding a want down." She turned the dummy's head back. "You've done that before. You told me about the mountain. The father with the paper."

Lingyue stood very still.

"You told him his yes stopped at his children," said Su Mingli. "To his face. He was a head taller than you. You held it." She took her hand off the dummy. "That's the same thing. That's what this is. Again."

By dusk, she could hold the dummy's face down for thirty seconds against everything Su Mingli could do to lift it. By midnight, two minutes.

Her sword cracked at eleven.

He heard it from the steps. A small sharp sound, like ice going on a pond. Lingyue's own sword, the one she had carried since the Academy, the one she had lit with pale fire against herons over the canal road and drowned spires at Port Abiri, split along its length from the guard to a hand-width from the point. The pale fire on it flickered and went out.

She looked at it for a long moment.

Then she put it down on the stone very carefully, the way you lay down something you have carried a long way, and drew her grandfather's sword.

"Told you," said Su Mingli.

On the fourth day, the tribulation came.

Nobody had expected it. Lingyue least of all. She was on the training ground at noon with her grandfather's sword, a hundred kilos of King's blade, holding the training dummy's face down against Su Mingli's hand for the eleventh time that morning, when the sky over the Academy went white.

Not the black lid of a Lord's tribulation at sea. Not the iron wheel of an Elite's over the battlements. White. A clean hard white, like the inside of a shell, spreading out across the noon sky from directly overhead.

Su Mingli took her hand off the dummy and stepped back. All the way back. Off the training ground. To the steps.

"Ah," she said. "There it is."

Lingyue stood alone on the stone in the middle of the sand with her grandfather's sword in both hands and her face turned up to the white sky.

"I'm sixty," she said. Not to anyone. "I've been sixty for a month. I didn't think..."

> **Elite peak. Level sixty. For thirty-one days.**
>
> **She has been holding the breakthrough down. Not on purpose. Because she was learning to hold things down, and she was very good at it, and she held that down too.**
>
> **Yesterday she learned to hold a want down. Today she let go of one.**
>
> **It is a realm tribulation. Light Law. Ordinary.**
>
> **It is not ordinary for her.**

---

He went down the steps.

He did not ask anyone. He walked across the sand to the stone in the middle of the training ground, under the white sky, and stood beside her. Not in front. Not behind. Beside. Close. His shoulder against her arm.

She did not look at him.

"I'm supposed to do this alone," she said. "Everybody does it alone. In a quiet room. With a master watching."

"I know."

"You were alone. On the battlement. You said."

"I wasn't alone at sea."

"That's different. You had fifty-two thousand dead."

"I had forty thousand," said Yuanshu. "And Seed. And my mother." He did not move. "I told you, on this training ground, four days ago, that I'd stand next to you the whole time. You said yes to that."

Lingyue was silent for a moment. The white sky pressed down. He could feel it now, through the Field: not on him. On her. The cosmos pressing on the glass from outside, testing whether it would hold.

"Don't help," she said.

"I won't."

"I mean it. Don't do anything. Don't hold anything. Don't let Seed run anything." Her hands tightened on her grandfather's sword. "I want to know I did it."

"Then I'll stand here," said Yuanshu. "That's all."

"Good," said Shen Lingyue, and closed her eyes, and pushed.

---

He did not see what happened inside the glass. He could not. It was hers.

He saw what happened outside. The white sky came down on her the way the sea had come down on the old jetty at Sandu, the whole weight of it, even and patient and enormous, pressing on a nineteen-year-old girl standing on a stone in the middle of a training ground with a King's sword in her hands. Her knees bent. The stone cracked under her feet. Su Mingli, on the steps, took one step forward and stopped herself.

And then Lingyue did the thing she had spent four days learning.

She did not push back. She did not shine. She let the light run down the blade of her grandfather's sword, pale and heavy and silver-gray, into the stone at her feet, and stop there. Hold. A bead of mercury on a table. And she stood on it.

On her own light. Holding it down. The way she had held down a pebble, and a dummy's face, and a father's paper on a mountain in Yamato.

The white sky pressed.

She held.

It pressed harder.

She held. She did not move. She did not shine. She stood on the weight of her own light in the middle of the training ground with her eyes shut and her split lip bleeding again and her grandfather's sword pointed down at the stone between her feet, and did not let go of anything at all.

At 12:41, the white sky cracked.

Not her. The sky. A thin line, like the crack in her sword, running across the hard white shell from one side of the Academy to the other. And then the white went out of it, all at once, like a breath, and it was blue again, and noon, and the cicadas started.

Lingyue opened her eyes.

She stood on the cracked stone with the sword still pointed down, breathing hard, and looked at him.

"You didn't help," she said.

"I didn't help."

"Good." She lowered the sword. Her knees went, all at once, and she sat down on the stone, hard, with the King's blade across her lap, and started to laugh. Out loud, on the training ground, with her lip bleeding. "I'm a Lord. I'm a *Lord*." She looked up at the steps. "Qin Hao. Write it down. Third. Not second. I don't care. Third."

On the steps, Qin Hao had his notebook open. He was not writing.

"I already did," he said. His voice was very rough. "This morning. Before it happened. I wrote: *today, probably.*"

Lingyue stared at him.

Then she lay back on the cracked stone in the middle of the training ground, in the noon sun, with her grandfather's sword across her chest, and laughed until she cried, and Yuanshu sat down on the sand beside her and did not say anything at all.
