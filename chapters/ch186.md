## Chapter 186: Forty Days

They asked a fishmonger in Kesse on the first morning.

Ngozi Eze did it herself. She stood in the fish market at the bottom of the town, in her plain dark suit, at six o'clock, with a clipboard she did not need and a radio crew she had not wanted, and asked the first person who looked at her. A broad old woman behind a slab of ice with a knife in her hand, gutting mackerel. Ngozi Eze told her about the bell, and the fishermen's code, and the four notes, and the thing that might be listening, and what would happen if Blue Star said yes, and what would happen if it said no. It took eleven minutes. The fishmonger did not stop gutting.

When it was over, she put the knife down.

"My grandfather went over the side off the point in the year of the long rains," she said. "They never found him." She wiped her hands on her apron. "If somebody out there's been ringing for twelve hundred years to see if anyone's still on shore, I'd want them to know." She picked the knife back up. "Yes. Write it down."

It was the first answer. Seed counted it.

> **One.**

---

After that, it went the way water goes through a crack once it starts.

Not quickly. Not neatly. Blue Star had never asked itself anything all at once before, and it did not know how, and so it did it the way people do things they do not know how to do: in a thousand different ways at the same time, badly, out loud, arguing.

In the Atlantic Federation, Elias Brandt went on television every night for forty nights and read out what the Council knew, in his careful voice, putting his glasses on and taking them off, and then said, *You don't have to decide tonight. You have forty days. Talk to someone.* In the Northern Union, Marshal Orlov had the question read aloud on every wall of every fortress at dawn, in his formal old-fashioned Huaxian translated into six languages, and the soldiers voted by putting a stone in one of two buckets at the foot of the wall, and the buckets were carried to Kirovsk by sled. In Yamato, the prefectures set up tables under the gates of every shrine in the islands, and people came up the paths with their children to stand under the gates and hear the bell played once on an old radio and then decide.

In Huaxia, the Ossuary opened its doors.

---

The Ledger had been built for asking. That was the whole of it. Rows of booths in the old banking hall where a person could sit down with a witness and say what they wanted done with their mind and remains when they died: YES, QUIET, ONLY TO SPEAK. A million names in the first forty-three days, nearly two years ago. Millions since. Fat Tan's people had built the booths, and Sister Ning's had sat in them, and Xiaoyu had written the names on the wall.

Now the queue went round the block.

Not for the Ledger. For the bell. People came in off East Market Road and stood under the long white wall of names and listened to eleven minutes of a twelve-hundred-year-old bell from a village that no longer existed, played through Fat Tan's speaker in the wall, and then sat down in a booth with a witness and were told everything the Council knew, and asked.

Most of them stayed afterward to read the wall.

General Han came on the eleventh day, on a Wednesday, and stood in the queue in plain clothes like everyone else, and listened to the bell, and sat in a booth across from Sister Ning, and said yes. Then he went and stood in front of the GRID NINE column for an hour.

Hendrik Maas drove a bus. He had been driving one since the morning at Port Abiri, he said, on the phone, when Ngozi Eze's people asked him; he had found he liked it. He drove the old and the sick from the villages up the coast to the polling tables in Kesse, forty days, eleven hours a day, and when they asked him what he had voted, he said that was his business, and that he would still like to be kept where his wife was kept, but not by anything that told you to stay quiet in the fog.

On the hill above Port Abiri, Akosua Mensah said yes on the first morning, sitting on the grass beside two stones, to a clerk from the capital who had climbed up the path with a clipboard. Then she said: "Tell them she'd have said it louder."

---

The dead were asked too.

That was in Article Eleven. General Han's sentence. *This Article binds every asker: persons, the dead, the powers above the sky, and the nations of this Council.* Nobody had thought about what it meant until the eighth day, when Sister Ning came down the vault steps of the Ossuary with the Ledger in her arms and said, quietly, that the Commission had fifty-two thousand dead on its register who had said yes to standing, and that they were part of *all*, and that someone should ask them.

So he asked them.

Through Adjutant Xu, down the Lattice, to every unit on the Commission's register. The First Legion on the north wall. The Western Legion on its mountain. The cemetery companies. The Watch of the Shelf at the bottom of the sea. The Spear Corps, two hundred and seventy-seven now, in their rows in the banking hall. And through Ren's hand on the stone, the three hundred and seventy-two under the gates in Yamato.

He told them what the living had been told. All of it. And asked.

It took four days for the answers to come back, because the Western Legion asked each other very carefully, nine by nine, the way Sergeant Ma Dashan said a miner checks every prop in a tunnel before he trusts it.

> **The Watch of the Shelf: yes. Nana Ampofo says a pilot always answers a bell in the fog.**
>
> **The monks under the gates: yes. They are the ones who asked. They say it would be strange to say no.**
>
> **The Western Legion: no. Sergeant Ma says the pass taught them what shouting brings down. He says four notes is not shouting, but he says a miner never makes the first sound in a dark tunnel. He says they are sorry. He says if Blue Star says yes, they will hold the pass anyway, and gladly.**
>
> **The First Legion: yes. Ma Lianhua says a city that has been afraid for thirty-one years would like to say something to someone who is not.**
>
> **The Spear Corps: they did not say anything. Old Seven says they are waiting to see what the living say. He says they are soldiers. Soldiers do not vote. He says they will do whatever is decided, and they would like it decided well.**

---

On the twenty-ninth night, Lingyue found him on the Sector Nine battlements, in his old place, with his back against the parapet and the staff across his knees, looking north.

She sat down beside him without asking. She had her grandfather's sword. She always had it now. She laid it across her knees beside his staff and did not say anything for a while.

Out over the end of the world, the small white ring hung low in the dark.

"Did you vote?" she said.

"Yes."

"What did you say?"

"You're supposed to not ask that."

"I'm not asking. I'm telling you I want to know." She did not look at him. "You taught me that. On the mountain. When I told you I wanted something and wasn't going to take it." She turned her grandfather's sword over on her knees. "So. I want to know. You don't have to tell me."

He looked at the ring for a while.

"Yes," he said. "I said yes."

"Why?"

"Because she's been under a gate for twelve hundred years. And he's been ringing for twelve hundred years. And neither of them knew the other one was still there." He was quiet. "And because I don't want to live in a world that's so afraid of being heard that it won't tell a fisherman he isn't alone on the water."

Lingyue did not say anything.

Then she leaned her head against his shoulder. Not quickly. Not the way she did things, which was all at once and hard. Slowly, the way you set something down that you have carried a long way, and left it there.

"I said yes too," she said.

"I know."

"You don't know. You can't know. That's the point of the booth."

"I know you," said Yuanshu.

She did not lift her head.

"Ask me something else," she said, after a while. "Not about the bell."

"What?"

"Anything. Ask me what I want. Properly. The way you do. With what you'll do with it."

He looked down at her. At the top of her head on his shoulder, and her hands on the King's sword across her knees, and the faint white scar on her lip from the training ground at the Academy, where the sky had pressed down on her and she had stood on her own light and held.

"Lingyue," he said. "I'm going to ask you what you want. Whatever you say, I'll keep it. Not like the Collector keeps things. Like a person. Badly. With bruises." He was quiet. "What do you want?"

She did not answer for a long time.

Then she said, into his shoulder, very quietly, so that only he heard it and not even the First Legion banked along the wall in the dark:

"This. The wall. You. Nobody looking at us. For a while." Her hands tightened on the sword. "And then the next thing. Whatever it is. Together. I don't want to be counted as a girl standing next to you. I want to be counted as the one who stands next to you." She lifted her head and looked at him. "Is that two things?"

"It's one thing," said Yuanshu.

"Good." She put her head back down. "Then you can have it."

On the fortieth day, at midnight, the asking closed.

Not neatly. Blue Star did not know how to do anything neatly. The counts came in by radio and phone and sled and fishing boat, in a hundred formats, from a thousand cities, and Seed took them all and laid them side by side, the way it had laid Xiaoyu's seconds side by side under the board, and counted.

It took nine hours.

At nine in the morning on the forty-first day, in the crater on Concordia, in front of the five delegations and the sixth screen and the whole of Blue Star watching on every channel, Chair Adaeze Okafor read the number.

"Asked: six billion, nine hundred and eleven million, four hundred and two thousand, one hundred and six, living. Fifty-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-three, dead." She did not look up from the paper. "Answered yes: fifty-eight point three percent. Answered no: thirty-three point one. Answered: I don't know, or nothing, or something we couldn't put in either bucket: eight point six."

She put the paper down.

"Blue Star says yes," she said. "Not loudly. Not all of it. A great many people said no, and they said it for good reasons, and I want it written down that they were heard." She looked at the sixth screen. "Shirasagi Ren. Tell Sister Kiyo."

On the screen, on the broken step of the hall in Yamato, Ren put her hand flat on the wood.

"*She knows,*" she said. "*She's been listening to the radio under her gate for forty days.*"

---

The four notes went out at dusk.

Not from Blue Star. From the keeper's eye, at the end of the world, the small white ring hanging low over the north in the evening. Seed built them: four notes in the fog-code of a fishing village that had not existed for a thousand years, the middle two changed. Sister Kiyo, under the three hundred and fortieth gate, through Ren, through the phone, through Seed, told it how fast to ring them. A little faster than it wanted. *He always rang a little too fast. If I ring it slow, he'll think it's a stranger.*

The keeper sent them out along its attention, very quietly, the way you hand something to someone in a dark room.

Four notes.

*We hear you.*

Then the keeper closed its eye, and the small white ring hung low in the dark over the end of the world, and on every radio on Blue Star that was tuned to the bell, nothing happened.

For four hundred and seven seconds.

And then the long low tone came, and the wait, and the small bells saying *Shiomi*, and the longer wait.

And under it, in the silence, very faint, very fast, too fast, the way a man rings a bell when he is in a hurry and his hands are shaking:

Four notes.

*We hear you.*

Under the three hundred and fortieth gate, on a mountain in Yamato, a woman who had been eighteen when she sat down beside her brothers twelve hundred years ago made a sound that came up through the stone and through a King's hand and through a phone and out of a speaker in the wall of an old bank in Lingzhou, and Seed did not translate it, because some things are not yours to hear.
