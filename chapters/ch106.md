## Chapter 106: Sixty Seconds

Zhou Xiaoyu had a clipboard, a stopwatch, and a whistle, and the whole of Lingzhou was afraid of her.

She stood on the battlements of Sector Nine at eleven o'clock at night with the stopwatch in one hand and the whistle in her teeth, looking out over the city. Forty kilometers of towers and rooftops and streets, packed inside the curve of the old wall. Lights in every window. Neon on the market streets. Headlights crawling along the river road. The great glowing dome of the Exhibition Hall by the water. The lit spires of the Institute.

Behind her, along the battlements, in a line that ran east and west out of sight, nineteen thousand of the dead stood their watch.

Beside her, in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees, her mother. With a radio. And a microphone.

"Ready?" said Zhou Wanqing.

Xiaoyu took the whistle out of her mouth.

"Ready," she said.

Her mother lifted the microphone, and pressed the button, and said, in a quiet, sharp, slightly hoarse voice that went out of every speaker in every street and every building and every phone in Lingzhou at once:

"*Lanterns.*"

Xiaoyu clicked the stopwatch.

---

The city went dark.

Not all at once. In a wave. From the center out. The way the Lantern sea had gone dark, her brother had told them on the thread, from beneath the raft outward, Lantern by Lantern. Here, it was window by window. Street by street. The Institute first, its spires going black. Then the Exhibition Hall. Then the old quarter, the narrow lanes around the teahouse on Teahouse Lane, where a man in a gray prison jacket sat beside a bed with a candle he did not light. Then the market streets. The neon flickering out sign by sign. Then the towers. Floor by floor. Then the headlights on the river road, stopping, going out. Then the harbor. Then the suburbs. Then the farms beyond.

Forty kilometers of city, going dark in nine seconds.

And then the second part.

---

You could not see the second part. You could only feel it.

Xiaoyu felt it the way you feel a room go quiet when someone important walks in. A hush. Spreading. Not in the ears. In the air. In the back of the neck. Three million people, in the dark, in their homes and their offices and their cars and their beds, closing their eyes. Not sleeping. Not praying. Not thinking.

Playing the game.

Sister Ning had taught it to four thousand people on the wall. Those four thousand had taught it to forty thousand. Those forty thousand had taught it to everyone. In schools. In factories. In hospital waiting rooms. On the radio, every morning at seven, in Sister Ning's quiet dry voice: *Think about rice. Think about the sound of the sea. Think about nothing. You've done it before. Every one of you. In the second before sleep.*

And on the battlements, along the whole length of the northern wall, nineteen thousand gray flames, which Hundred-Arrow Lin had spent twenty days teaching to bank, sank. Down. Small. Smaller. Coals under ash. Until the wall was dark too.

Xiaoyu stood in the dark with the stopwatch in her hand and her eyes closed and thought about nothing at all.

It was very hard. She was terrible at it. She thought about her brother the whole time.

---

At sixty seconds, she blew the whistle.

The city came back. In a wave. From the edges in, this time. Lights. Neon. Headlights. Windows. People opening their eyes in the dark and blinking and laughing a little, nervously, the way you laugh after you've been holding your breath. Somewhere down in the market, someone cheered. Somewhere else, a baby was crying.

Xiaoyu looked down at the stopwatch.

"Fifty-nine point eight," she said. "Everyone except the hospital district. They broke at fifty-one."

"Babies," said her mother quietly. "Again."

"Babies," said Xiaoyu. She wrote it on the clipboard. Underlined it twice. "Sister Ning says you can't teach a three-week-old to think about rice."

"No," said Zhou Wanqing. She looked out at the lit city for a long time. "No, you can't."

---

The half-raft had come home on the twentieth day after the split.

Xiaoyu had been on the wall when it came. Everybody had. It came down the gold thread out of the night sky over the north like a falling star that didn't fall, a pale disc of light, and settled on the battlements of Sector Nine as gently as a leaf on water. And three people stepped off it. A boy in Western War Zone green with a colonel's star over his heart. A pale tall woman in white winter fatigues with a ledger clutched to her chest. And a drift of ash and dark air with an iron arrowhead for a heart.

Not her brother.

She had known it wouldn't be. Her mother had told her, on the thread. But she had stood on the wall and watched the pale disc come down anyway, and when it was only three, she had turned around and walked down the stairs and gone home and sat in the kitchen for an hour with the green flask in her hands, and not cried, and then gone back up to the wall.

Hundred-Arrow Lin had walked straight past everyone to a captain of the Defense Corps with very short hair and a sharp face, standing at the top of the stairs. And stopped. And stood beside her.

Captain Lin Wen had not said anything. She had just put her hand out, very carefully, into the ash and dark air beside her. Where a hand would be.

And left it there.

---

Sergeant Sokolova had gone north. To the Northern Union. To a two-hundred-year-old Marshal who had asked her to come home. She had left the ledger with Xiaoyu.

"Your brother's," she had said. "Fat Tan's, first. It has the names of everyone he asked out there. The Lanterns. The Asking World. The ones in the first ring." She had pressed it into Xiaoyu's hands. "He wanted it to come here. To your board."

Xiaoyu had taken it to the Ossuary that night.

It had taken her six hours to copy the names onto the board. Every one. In a new section, under a new heading, in careful capitals, at the very end of the hall, past CAME HOME.

*FROM OUT THERE.*

---

Qin Hao went west, to the Western Pass. To stand beside an eighty-two-year-old general with one arm, in front of forty-one thousand graves.

But first he came to the hospital, to see Xiaoyu's mother. He sat by the bed with his cap in his hands and told her everything. The raft. The husk. Hush. The Lanterns. The cold place. Captain Zhao in the doorway.

Wanqing listened without interrupting, the way she always did.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"And my son," she said finally. "How is he?"

Qin Hao thought about it.

"He's still asking everyone," he said. "Every time. Even when there isn't time. Even when it's obvious." He turned his cap over in his hands. "He asked a Hunger if it wanted to stop."

"Did it?"

"Billions of people inside it said yes."

Zhou Wanqing closed her eyes.

"That's him," she said.

---

Every night now, the sky over the north had two new lights in it.

Not the small white ring of the keeper, which hung where it always hung, low over the end of the world, like a ring someone dropped. Further. Higher. Beyond where the stars began.

Two gray specks. Close together. Side by side. Growing.

The whole world could see them now. Not just Seed. Not just Kings. Everyone. You could stand on any street corner in Lingzhou at midnight and look up and see them. Two small gray rings. Turning. Coming.

The news had a name for them. *The Twins.* The Concordia Council had a number for them, on a big digital clock in the crater, counting down.

Fifty-one days.

---

On the forty-ninth night before they came, Xiaoyu sat on the battlements of Sector Nine with her clipboard on her knees and her mother's wheelchair beside her, and looked north.

"Mama," she said.

"Mm."

"The babies."

Zhou Wanqing did not answer for a while.

"I know," she said.

"Every drill. Every single one. The hospital district breaks. Babies cry. You can't stop them. You can't teach them." Xiaoyu looked down at the clipboard. At the number, underlined twice. "Three million people go quiet, and then one baby cries, and everyone in the whole ward hears it, and gets frightened, and..." She stopped. "Ge said the first ring heard one girl crying. On the Lantern world. One. And it came down."

"I know."

"So what do we do?"

Her mother looked out at the north for a long time. At the two gray specks. At the small white ring.

"I don't know yet," she said. "I've been thinking about it in the afternoons. Instead of sleeping." She was quiet. "I think the answer is something we haven't asked yet."

"Asked who?"

Zhou Wanqing did not answer.

But beside her, on the battlements, a drift of ash and dark air with an iron arrowhead for a heart, which had come to stand there every night beside Captain Lin Wen, turned. Very slowly. Toward the old quarter of the city. Toward a narrow lane by the river, and a teahouse with paper screens over the windows, where the dead had been taught to be quiet, and the living had been taught to sit beside them.

And the iron arrowhead in her chest flickered once.

---

> **Xiaoyu.**

She nearly dropped the clipboard.

It was not her brother's voice. It was not anyone's voice. It came out of the radio in her mother's lap, very faint, very far, crackling with distance, through a gold thread that ran north from the wall to a small white ring, and then out, out, across a dark so wide that no one on Blue Star could imagine it.

A quiet, careful, white voice.

> **Tell your mother we are coming.**
>
> **Faster than the currents.**
>
> **We are riding something very strange.**
>
> **And tell her I have an idea about the babies.**
