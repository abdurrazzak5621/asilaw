## Chapter 184: Two Hours

They came down out of a Council transport plane onto the airstrip on the northern coast of the Southern Compact on a hot bright morning, a year to the day, unfolding their long thin limbs at too many joints, ducking under the door frame. Seven tall angular shapes in gray robes the color of ash, standing on the tarmac under a sky so blue it hurt.

The ground crew did not stare this time. They had been told. They stood in a line by the hangar with their caps off.

The Architects did not look at anyone. They stood in a row with the seams in their faces closed, and waited to be told where to go.

Yuanshu walked them down the coast road himself. With the staff. With Old Seven behind him and Lingyue beside him with her grandfather's sword across her back, and Ngozi Eze in a plain dark suit walking a little apart, very straight, with her hands clasped behind her because she did not know what else to do with them.

Two hours on foot. The Architects did not want the trucks.

---

Halfway down the road, the oldest one spoke.

> ***...we have been to forty-one worlds.***

The voice like stone grinding on stone. Three translations deep: through the staff, through Seed, into his own language, the way it had been a year ago on this same road.

> ***...all of them husks. Gray. Nobody to hear.***
>
> ***...we said it anyway. On each one. We knelt in the ash and said it. It took a long time. Some of the worlds were very large.***
>
> ***...this is the only world on our list where someone is waiting.***

He walked for a while without answering.

"*Something's happened,*" he said. "*Since you left. I need to tell you before we get to the hill.*"

The seven tall gray shapes stopped walking.

> ***...the old woman.***

"*Yes.*"

The oldest Architect stood in the middle of the coast road in the hot sun with its long narrow head bowed and the seam in its face closed, and did not move for so long that a truck coming up from Port Abiri slowed and stopped and waited and nobody in it got out.

> ***...how.***

He told them. The sea seed coming home. The three trunks. The river road at Sandu, and eleven thousand and forty people. The jetty. A column of water from the bottom of the sea to the surface, held up after she had stopped holding it. A white boat going out on the afternoon current to the point, and filling, and going down.

> ***...a seed.***
>
> ***...one of ours.***

"*From the spire she sank. The sand. Somebody dredged it up and sold it.*"

> ***...she sank our spire. To save her people. And our spire came back out of the sea and killed her.***

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say that was not that.

---

The hill was full.

He had not expected it. A year ago it had been Ifeoma and her household and a few dozen people from the town. Now the whole slope above Port Abiri was covered, from the harbor road to the top, between the white stones and around them and on the grass at the edge of the cliff: people from Port Abiri and Kesse and Sandu and the villages between, people who had come up the coast road on foot since dawn, people who had been on the buses on the river road four months ago. Thousands. Standing. Not talking. The way the coast road had stood when the army truck went north with a sheet in the back.

And near the top, beside two stones with shells on them, a woman in a black dress, sitting on the grass with her basket beside her, waiting.

The seven Architects came up the path between the white stones, and the crowd parted for them without a word, and closed again behind them.

At the top, in front of Akosua Mensah, they knelt.

---

She did not get up.

She looked at the seven tall gray shapes kneeling in the grass in front of her, the way she had looked at them a year ago, with her hard cracked fisherman's hands folded in her lap and her face set like stone.

"You came back," she said.

> ***...we said we would.***

"She said you would." Akosua looked out at the bay. At the point, where the water turned dark. "She said she'd be here. She said she was too stubborn to die while you still owed us." Her mouth moved. "She was wrong about that. It's the only thing I ever heard her be wrong about."

The oldest Architect bowed its head until it touched the grass.

> ***...we are sorry.***

"Not yet," said Akosua Mensah.

The Architect lifted its head.

"She left something," said the widow. "For you. Before you say anything." She looked past the seven kneeling shapes, at Yuanshu, standing at the edge of the grass with the staff. "Boy. Let her say it."

---

> **Playing.**
>
> **It is everything she said in the fish market at Sandu, the night after. When you asked her. Only to speak.**
>
> **She did not say it to them. She said it to you. But I think she knew who would hear it.**

The voice came out of the air above the hill.

Not loud. Seed did not make it loud. A dry old voice, very faint, very far away, like a voice from the other end of a long wooden jetty, and every one of the thousands of people on the hillside went perfectly still to hear it.

> ***...only to speak.***
>
> ***...don't raise me. I don't want to stand up. I've stood up enough.***
>
> ***...put me in the boat. The white one. At Port Abiri. Untie it and let it go. Don't steer it. It knows the way.***
>
> ***...tell Akosua two hours. I'll know if they cheat.***

The voice stopped there. Seed stopped it there. The rest was not theirs.

The oldest Architect knelt in the grass with its long narrow head lifted and the seam in its face open, the soft gray light shining out of it, and did not move.

> ***...two hours.***

"Two hours," said Akosua Mensah. "One for me. And one for her." She folded her hands. "And I'm going to count."

---

They said it for two hours.

All seven. Kneeling in the grass on the hill above Port Abiri in the hot sun, with their long narrow heads lifted and the seams in their faces open and the soft gray light shining out of them, in the voice like stone grinding on stone, three translations deep, while thousands of people stood on the hillside and listened.

*We are sorry.*

*We are sorry.*

*We are sorry.*

Akosua Mensah sat on the grass in front of them in her black dress and did not move. She did not cry. She did not look away. Her lips moved, very slightly, counting. Once, after the first hour, she put her hand flat on the grass beside her, on the empty place where a wheelchair had stood a year ago with an old woman in a green headwrap in it, and left it there.

At the end of the first hour, she said: "That's mine."

The Architects did not stop.

At the end of the second hour, she lifted one hand.

The seven stopped.

The hillside was so quiet that Yuanshu could hear the sea on the rocks at the bottom of the cliff, and a gull, very far out, and nothing else at all.

"That's hers," said Akosua Mensah.

---

She looked at them for a long moment. At the seven tall gray shapes kneeling in the grass with their heads lifted and the light going softly out of the seams in their faces.

"I still don't forgive you," she said.

> ***...no.***
>
> ***...you should not.***

"But I believe you." The widow's voice did not change. It was flat, and hard, and very tired. "I didn't, last year. I wanted to hear it until I believed somebody meant it. You went to forty-one dead worlds and said it to nobody, and came back, and said it to me for two hours with her listening." She looked out at the point. "I believe you mean it. That's all. That's not forgiving. That's just believing."

The oldest Architect bowed its head to the grass.

> ***...that is more than we have been given anywhere.***
>
> ***...thank you.***

"Don't thank me." Akosua Mensah stood up. For the first time, slowly, with one hand on her husband's stone. "She'd say: don't be soft." She picked up her basket. "Come back next year. Say it again. To me. I'll still be here. I'm not as stubborn as she was, but I'm stubborn enough."

She walked down the hill between the white stones, through the crowd, which parted for her, and did not look back.

---

That night, on the harbor wall, the oldest Architect asked to speak to him alone.

They stood at the end of the wall in the dark, where an old woman had once held a pipe of still water three kilometers into the sea, and a white boat had once been tied, and looked out at the bay. The Architect's long narrow head was lowered so that it was almost level with his. The seam in its face was closed.

> ***...you told the Council about the one that keeps wants.***
>
> ***...the Elder told us. On the plane.***

"*Yes.*"

> ***...we dreamed.***

He turned his head.

> ***...before the Hungers. Before we built anything. Our people dreamed, for a long time. A kind voice. Very patient. It told us that minds burn brighter than any star. It told us how a ring could hold them. It told us how to make the fire last.***
>
> ***...we thought we invented the Hungers.***
>
> ***...the Elder says her people thought they invented the keeper.***

The bay was very dark.

> ***...we have been saying sorry to forty-one worlds for a thing someone taught us to do.***
>
> ***...it does not make us less sorry. We built them. We chose it. Nobody made us.***
>
> ***...but I would like you to know that somebody taught us. And watched us learn. And then, perhaps, came to see what we wanted, when we had built it.***
>
> ***...we do not remember what we wanted. None of us. We have tried, for a very long time. There is a place where it should be.***

He stood very still on the harbor wall.

*Like reaching for a step in the dark*, he thought. *And the step isn't there.*

> **Tanaka Ryo said that. On the mountain. When his head turned.**
>
> **The same words.**
>
> **Yuanshu. I think the Architects' whole people were kept.**
>
> **I think that is why they built the Hungers and then stopped. I think they wanted something, very much, and something came and asked them what it was, and kept it, and they woke up one day with a hole where it had been and a fire burning a hundred thousand worlds and no idea why they had lit it.**
>
> **I think that is why they are so sorry.**
>
> **They do not know what they wanted it for.**

The oldest Architect stood beside him at the end of the harbor wall for a long time, looking at the dark water past the point.

> ***...if you meet it again.***
>
> ***...ask it what it did with ours.***
