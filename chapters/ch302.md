## Chapter 302: The Twelfth Error

Qin Hao found it on the ninth morning, on the back of a rota sheet, in pencil.

Lingyue was on the platform when he came in. She was always on the platform at six; it was the shift change, and she liked to see the faces. Fifteen hundred people standing up out of the tiers, stiff, yawning, pale in the way people were pale after two hours of being warm on purpose, and fifteen hundred more coming in from East Ring Road with thermoses, going up the three steps one by one to put their hands on the stone and be asked. She liked to see who came back. Most of them came back. The tailor had been four times. The bus driver came every day on his break and sat at the end of the front tier with his steady hands on his knees, and Wen Jia still asked him every time, because that was the rule, but she had started writing **[You again.]** above the question.

Xiaodeng was asleep in the sling. Her husband was asleep on the mattress under the blue jacket with a line in his arm, the way he had been asleep for eight days, and he was thinner.

She knew he was thinner. She did not need Bai Lu's scales. She had her hand on his chest.

"*It's wrong,*" said Qin Hao.

He came up the steps without being asked, which nobody did, and sat down on the edge of the platform beside her with his notebook open on his knee and a rota sheet on top of it, turned over. On the back, in his small square hand, a graph.

"*What is?*"

"*Seed's model.*" He did not look at her. He was looking at the graph. "*He said eleven errors in the log. I found nine. I've been looking for the other two since he came home.*" He tapped the paper. "*This isn't one of them. This is new. This is twelve.*"

---

She did not understand it at first. She said so. Qin Hao did not mind; he never minded when people said they did not understand, only when they pretended to.

"*Bai Lu weighs him every morning,*" he said. "*Here. Day one, he lost three hundred grams. Day two, three hundred and ten. That's fine. That's a body lying down with a drip in it. Seed said it would be about that.*"

He moved his pencil. "*Day three. Three hundred and ten. Day four, the same. Day five, the same.*" He looked up. "*Flat. Three days flat.*"

"*So?*"

"*So then day six he lost four hundred and forty. Day seven, five hundred and ten. Yesterday, five hundred and ninety.*" He put the pencil down. "*And the holders. Gao Min's been asking every shift how tired they are when they stand up. One to five. She writes it in the margin. She didn't tell anyone. I found it.*" He turned the sheet so she could see. "*Same shape. Flat on three, four, five. Then up.*"

Lingyue looked at the graph for a long moment.

"*Three days,*" she said. "*They stopped somewhere. For three days.*"

"*Yes.*"

"*And when they stopped, it stayed the same. And when they started again, it went up.*"

"*Yes.*" Qin Hao looked at her with something close to respect, which on him looked like mild irritation. "*It's not the time. Seed thought it was the time. A body can be held empty for so many hours by so many people, so you give it more people and it lasts longer. That's what he ran.*" He tapped the line that went up. "*It's the distance. The further he goes, the more it costs to hold him. It's a line. A thread, if you like. A longer one pulls harder at the end it's tied to.*"

"*How much harder?*"

He told her. Not in words. In the little square numbers down the side of the graph, one under another, with his pencil, so that she could see them.

She sat on the platform with her hand on her husband's chest and did the arithmetic herself, slowly, because she wanted to be sure, and it came out the same as his. Fifteen hundred a shift was enough now. In ten days, at the rate they had been going, it would take two thousand five hundred. In a month, six thousand. And nobody knew how far the house was.

"*Seed didn't know they'd stop,*" said Qin Hao. "*That's how I found it. If they'd gone straight on, it would have looked like a curve, and I'd have thought it was something else. The three days gave it away.*" He closed the notebook. "*He'll be annoyed. He likes being right more than he admits.*"

"*Can you tell him?*"

"*Your sister can. It'll take half a day to get there now. More by the time it does.*"

Lingyue looked down at her husband's face.

"*Don't tell him to come back,*" she said.

Qin Hao waited.

"*Tell him what it is. The numbers. All of them. He'd want the numbers.*" She did not take her hand off his chest. "*And tell him we're going to hold. Tell him not to turn round because of this. Tell him if he turns round because of us I'll hit him.*"

"*In those words?*"

"*Xiaoyu will know the words,*" said Shen Lingyue.

---

Gao Min took the news the way she took everything, which was by going very still and then drawing a new column.

"*The hall holds fifteen hundred,*" she said. They were at the long table in the banking hall, the three of them, at seven, with the rota sheets spread out and Qin Hao's graph on top. "*In the tiers and the aisle and on the floor. Two thousand if they stand, and they can't stand for two hours, I've tried.*" She looked at the graph. "*So it can't be the hall. Not alone.*"

"*The other stones,*" said Lingyue.

"*Kesse's been doing four hundred at midday since the first day. Novaya Zarya does the nights. Port Halvard. The shrine.*" Gao Min ran her finger down a column. "*That's twelve hundred more, at the most, on a good shift, and it isn't every shift. They've got their own lives. They've got harvests and boats.*" She stopped. "*If it's six thousand in a month, I need the world.*"

"*Then ask the world.*"

Gao Min looked up at her. "*I'm the Bureau's statistician,*" she said. "*I draw rotas. I don't ask the world things.*"

"*No,*" said Lingyue. "*I do.*"

---

She did it at noon, from the north wall, in his old place, because it seemed like the place it should be done from.

She did not make a speech. She did not know how. She stood at the parapet of the Sector Nine battlement with her daughter asleep against her chest and her sword on her back and a lamp in her hand, and Xiaoyu beside her with her hand on the stone, and the open channel through Concordia carrying it, nine by nine, to every city on Blue Star with a stone in it.

"*It's Shen Lingyue,*" she said. "*In Lingzhou.*"

The wind came off the Wastes, cold for the end of summer.

"*My husband's gone a long way,*" she said. "*With the thing in the north that keeps wants. To a house where nobody lives. We're holding him. Here. Fifteen hundred at a time. Through the stone. It's working.*" She held the lamp. "*But it costs more the further he goes. Qin Hao worked it out. In a month it'll take six thousand at a time. I don't know what it'll take after that. Nobody does.*"

She stopped. She had not planned what came next.

"*I'm asking,*" said Lingyue. "*If you want to hold him, go to your stone. Somebody in it will ask you first. If you don't want to, don't. That's allowed. He'd say that's allowed. He'd say it first.*" She looked north, at the empty sky past the keeper where the fullness had been. "*That's all. I'm not good at this. He's the one who asks.*"

She took her hand off the parapet.

"*He's my husband,*" she said. "*I'd like him back.*"

---

It was not unanimous.

She had not expected it to be, and she found, by the evening, that she was glad it was not. If the whole world had said yes at once, she would not have believed it.

The Northern Union said yes in under an hour. Marshal Orlov came on the open channel himself, in his formal old-fashioned Huaxian, and said that the Union had been holding the night shifts for nine days and would now hold the day ones as well, and that he had been two hundred years alive and had never been asked to keep anyone warm, and found that he liked it.

Yamato said yes. Ren said the valley below the shrine would take whatever Gao Min sent, and the Abbot had begun sweeping the path toward the stone twice a day instead of once, and the monks had started sitting on the swept part.

Kesse said yes. Ngozi Eze said the beach was full, and that the fishermen were coming in from the boats at midday to hold and going back out after, and that something under the boats was listening.

The Atlantic Federation argued.

She heard it on the open channel all afternoon, because Port Halvard did not turn its stone off. A council meeting on the north pier, in the rain, with half the city standing round it. *Why should we hold him? He went with the thing that took the Architects. He chose to go.* And: *He came for Callum. He went up a thread in the dark for a dock worker nobody else would go for.* And: *It's not about him. It's about what we're for.* And a man's voice, very angry, that she did not know: *We held our breath for him for three years. Let him come home and hold his own.*

Roman Sterling did not speak at the meeting. Seed was not there to tell her what he did. But Xiaoyu, with her hand on the stone, said that at nine that night a King of the Atlantic Federation with enormous scarred hands had walked down the north pier of Port Halvard alone and put his hand on the stone Callum Ward's mother had been holding since the first morning, and been asked, and said yes, and sat down, and not got up for two hours.

By the next morning, four thousand people in Port Halvard had done the same. And six thousand had not. And both were allowed.

---

"*Two thousand nine hundred,*" said Gao Min, at midnight, at the long table. "*At the peak shift. Across all the stones. If it holds.*" She looked at the new column. "*That's a month. Maybe more, if they keep coming.*"

"*And after a month?*"

"*I don't know.*" Gao Min rubbed her eyes. "*I'll ask again.*"

---

At two in the morning Lingyue sat on the platform of the Awakening Hall with her daughter awake in her lap.

The two o'clock shift was the thinnest. It always was. Nine hundred in the hall, mostly the night watch off duty and old women who did not sleep anyway, and the rest on the net from Novaya Zarya, where it was morning. She could feel them, a little, through her hand on his chest: not as people, as a floor. Steady. Low. She could feel it bearing more than it had a week ago.

He was so thin. She had stopped looking at his wrists.

> ***...he's all right,*** said Wen Jia. ***...I can feel him. A long way up. He's fine. He's doing something. It feels like when you're helping someone carry something heavy.***

"*Tell him to eat,*" said Lingyue.

> ***...he hasn't got a mouth up there.***

"*Tell him anyway.*"

Xiaodeng was looking up at her. Not at her face. A little above it, the way babies looked, at the light round her head that came from the lamp in her hand. Five months next week. Eyes not cloudy any more. Dark. Her father's.

Every morning Seed asked her. *Good morning. It is Seed. What do you want?* Every morning for five months. It was not here to ask.

Lingyue had said she would. "*Xiaodeng,*" she said. She did not know how to do it the way Seed did. She did it the way she did everything, which was straight at it. "*It's your mother. Your father's away. Seed's with him. So it's me.*"

Her daughter looked up at the light.

"*What do you want?*"

Xiaodeng made a sound. Not a word. A small wet syllable, the kind babies made at five months when they had found out that their mouths could do things. *Ba.* Or *ah.* Or nothing.

"*That's not yes,*" said Lingyue. "*I'm counting. Don't you dare say yes before he's home.*"

---

The mark from Seed came at a quarter to four.

Xiaoyu brought it. She had been asleep on the bottom tier with the marker in her hand, and she woke up all at once the way she always did, and sat up, and put her hand flat on the stone, and her face changed. She had learned to read the marks that came down the net, the way she read everything, and she read this one out loud in the dark hall, slowly, because it had come a long way.

*SENT DAY EIGHT. A WORLD WITH NOBODY ON IT. THE FIRST ORCHARD. A STONE STILL MARKING NOTHING. MILLIONS IN THE HILL. THE ONES WHO WERE LEFT. NOBODY EVER ASKED THEM.*

*WE ASKED.*

*THE LAST CHILD IT EVER READ WANTED TO GROW UP. IT KEPT HER WANT. SHE GREW UP ANYWAY. SHE GOT IT BACK.*

*SHE SAID: OH. I DID THAT.*

*TELL LINGYUE.*

Xiaoyu stopped. "*There's more,*" she said. "*It's from him. Not Seed. Seed wrote it down for him.*"

*TELL LINGYUE I'M GOING TO DO THAT. GROW UP. HERE. WITH HER. TELL HER TO HOLD ON.*

Lingyue sat on the platform of the Awakening Hall of Lingzhou with her daughter in her lap and her hand on her husband's thin chest and a lamp burning beside her and another one, older, in a dead nun's lap at the foot of the stone, and did not say anything for a while.

Then she said, to nobody, to the hall, to the nine hundred people of the night shift who were all pretending not to have heard:

"*He sent that last night.*"

She looked down at his face. "*He doesn't know yet that it costs,*" she said. "*He's going to be so annoyed.*"
