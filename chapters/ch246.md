## Chapter 246: What Was Given

On the seventh night, the Collector found what was in the empty place, and it did not tell Xiaoyu what it was. It asked her where Shuanghe was.

"*Shuanghe?*" said Xiaoyu.

She was standing on the platform of the Awakening Hall at one in the morning, in front of the warm stone, with the line of holders stretching down the aisle behind her and out of the side door into the dark. Four thousand two hundred of them now, in shifts, through the night. They had not stopped since the first night. Gao Min had a cot behind the official's table and slept on it in two-hour pieces, and woke, and wrote.

> *A village. Where two rivers meet. Forty of your kilometers south of a place called Heishui.*
>
> *I have never been told the name. I have only been pointed there.*
>
> *I would like to know if that is its name.*

"*Yes,*" said Xiaoyu slowly. "*That's Shuanghe. Two Rivers.*"

The attention rested on her. Warm. Patient. And something else, tonight, that he had not felt in it before, standing at the foot of the steps with the staff. He did not know what to call it. If it had been a person, he would have said it was hesitating.

> *Thank you.*
>
> *I asked you every night what was in the empty place, and you asked me every night what I wanted, and I said I did not know.*
>
> *I know what was in it now.*
>
> *I would like to show you. Not tell. Show. At Shuanghe. Tomorrow night.*
>
> *Will someone be at the river?*

---

They drove south in the afternoon. Gao Min's car, because it was the only one in the Ossuary that would go eleven kilometers on gravel. Gao Min did not come; she said she had four thousand holders to count and she trusted nobody else to count them. She gave Kwan the keys and a list of instructions about the clutch, and Kwan drove, very carefully, with both hands, and said that she had driven in four countries and this was the worst clutch in any of them.

Yuanshu in the front with the staff. Xiaoyu and Lingyue in the back, the pale flame in Lingyue's palm the size of a lamp now, which she had stopped looking at. Old Seven walked. He would not get in a car. He set off from the bronze door the night before and was at Shuanghe before them.

The road ran south across the green plain, past the gray line of the fortifications at Heishui, and then off the asphalt onto gravel, and through willows, and down to a village of sixty gray houses and a school and a shrine with a red door, on a spit of land where a brown river from the north and a green river from the west came together and did not mix.

The ferry was in the middle of the river.

A flat wooden ferry, on a rope. A short skeleton in the rags of a Third Battalion uniform standing on it with his weight off his right leg, pulling it across hand over hand. Two passengers: an old woman with a basket, sitting on the boards with her face in her hands, and a boy of ten beside her with his arm round her shoulders.

Liang Fu had stopped the ferry in the middle, where the two colors met. He was pointing down at the water.

---

The woman in the straw hat met them at the top of the landing. Zeng Yan. She was nearly fifty and smoked and wore rubber boots and had been sixteen the year her uncle marched north with Lu Xuanming. She did not ask what they had come for. She looked at the four of them, and at the tall dead captain who had been sitting on her landing since nine in the morning, facing out, and at the sky.

"*Something's coming,*" she said. "*Isn't it. Your captain wouldn't say.*"

"*Yes.*"

"*For him?*"

"*I don't know,*" said Yuanshu. "*I think so. I think something's being brought back to him.*"

Zeng Yan looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked down at the river, at the ferry in the middle, at the small dead man pointing at the water for an old woman with her face in her hands.

"*He doesn't remember my mother's name,*" she said. "*His own sister. He's been here since spring. He says good morning to me every day like I'm a customer.*" She took the cigarette out of her mouth. "*He takes them across for free if they're sad. Same as before. He remembers that. He doesn't remember anything else.*" Her voice was rough. "*If something's being brought back, I'd like it to be her name.*"

---

The Collector came at one in the morning.

Not through a stone. There was no stone at Shuanghe. Down out of the sky, out of the deep water, the long way, the way it had once come down a lens on a mountain: an attention, from further away than anything, warm and patient, resting on the landing and the willows and the two rivers going the same way in the dark.

And on Xiaoyu.

She was standing at the edge of the landing with the marker in her hand. She did not wait.

"*What do you want?*" she said.

> *Not yet. First, I want to tell you what was in the empty place.*
>
> *I keep wants. I told your brother that, on the mountain. I ask, and a mind tells me, and I keep the answer forever, bright, never used up, never given what it wanted.*
>
> *I went to the place in me where a want of my own should be. It was empty. I looked further. There was a shape in it.*
>
> *The shape was a want. Not mine. Someone else's. One I had been given, and kept, so long ago that I had kept it in the place where my own should have gone, and forgotten it was not mine.*
>
> *It was the first one. The first want I was ever given.*
>
> *It said: home.*

On the landing, Xiaoyu did not move.

> *That is why I kept the soldier's. Behind the curtain, in the cold fire. When he said home, I had to keep it. It was the same shape as the first. I have been following it here because it points, and I have been following the first one my whole long life, because it points too. Both of them, the same way. Home.*
>
> *I do not know whose the first one was. They have been gone a very long time.*
>
> *But I know whose the second one is.*

---

Out in the middle of the river, on the ferry, Liang Fu had stopped.

He had taken the old woman and the boy across and come back empty, the way he did at night, and tied up at the near post, and then untied again, and gone out to the middle, alone, to stand where the two colors met. He did that at night, Zeng Yan said. Stood out there. Nobody knew why. He did not know why.

He was standing there now, with his hand on the rope, looking up.

> *I would like to give it back.*

The attention on the landing did not move. But something in it did. Something small. He felt it the way you feel someone, sitting across a table from you, put their hand in their pocket.

> *I have never given anything back. Not once. I did not know it could be done. I watched the Elder's ring let three million go back to the keeper's field, slowly, one at a time, because a ring with a yes in it can let go of what it took. I watched a girl in a stone hold a thread without sending anything up it. I watched a boy say a word to a stone and the stone open what he said.*
>
> *I learn everything from watching.*
>
> *And I asked myself: if I let go of the soldier's want, what will happen to it?*
>
> *And I did not know. And I wanted to know.*
>
> *Child. You asked me every night what I want.*
>
> *That is what I want. To know what happens when I let go.*

Xiaoyu uncapped the marker.

She did not have a wall. She knelt on the stone of the landing in the dark and wrote it on the step, in square black capitals, where the river would not reach: *THE COLLECTOR. NIGHT 8. WANTS TO KNOW WHAT HAPPENS WHEN IT LETS GO.*

> *Thank you.*
>
> *Now I am going to find out.*

---

It did not come down from the sky. That was what he had expected, and it was not what happened.

It came along the river.

From the north, down the brown water, out of the dark, very small, very fast, so small that he only knew it was there because Seed said *there* and because the river, for a moment, in a line, went still. A want. Not a light; nothing bright. A needle. Very small, very sharp, the shape of a single word, pointing. It had been pointing the whole time. Through the cold fire and behind the curtain and along the arm and in the hand of something older than gardens. Pointing here. To the middle of a river where two colors met.

And when the thing that held it let go, it went where it had always been pointing.

It went into the short dead man on the ferry the way a key goes into a lock.

---

Liang Fu sat down.

Not fell. Sat. All at once, on the boards of the ferry in the middle of the river, with his bad leg out in front of him and his hand still on the rope, the way a man sits down when his knees have heard something before the rest of him.

Then he turned his skull and looked at the landing.

Not at Yuanshu. Not at Old Seven, standing up from the top step for the first time since nine in the morning. At the woman in the straw hat and the rubber boots at the edge of the stones, with a cigarette going out in her fingers.

> **He says...**

Seed stopped. Then went on, slowly, the way it went on when it was not sure it had heard right.

> **He says: Xiao Yan?**
>
> **He says: you've got old. You've got Mei's face. You've got Mei's boots.**
>
> **He says: where's Mei?**

Zeng Yan dropped the cigarette.

"*She's gone,*" she said. Her voice went up in the middle, like a girl's. "*Uncle. She died. Nine years ago. She kept the ferry for you. She tied it the way you showed her.*"

The dead man on the ferry sat very still.

> **He says: Liang Mei.**
>
> **He says: my sister's name was Liang Mei. She shouted at me. Every time I took someone across for free. She said, Fu, you'll starve, you'll starve and I'll have to feed you.**
>
> **He says: she did feed me.**
>
> **He says: I remember.**
>
> **He says: I remember the house. The third one from the shrine. The blue door. The willow by the step, the one that leaned. I remember my mother's name. I remember the day I marched north. Mei stood on this landing and didn't wave. She was angry. She said if I came back dead she'd never speak to me again.**
>
> **He says: I came back dead.**
>
> **He says: home.**
>
> **He says: I'm home.**

---

Zeng Yan went down the steps into the river.

Not onto the ferry. Into the water, in her rubber boots, up to her knees, up to her waist, the brown water and the green swirling round her, out toward the middle, where a small dead man was sitting on a ferry with his hand on a rope. Nobody stopped her. Lingyue started forward and Yuanshu caught her arm and she stopped. Old Seven did not move at all.

Liang Fu pulled the ferry to her. Hand over hand. Slowly. With his weight off his right leg.

She climbed on. She sat down on the boards beside him, soaking wet, in the middle of the river, and put her arms round the small rag-wrapped skeleton of her mother's brother, and held on.

> **He says: Xiao Yan. You're getting the boards wet.**
>
> **He says: Mei would shout.**

Zeng Yan laughed into his shoulder.

---

> *...so that is what happens.*

The attention was still on the landing. On Xiaoyu, kneeling on the step with the marker in her hand. Warm. Patient. And something else again, that he did not have a word for and did not think the Collector had a word for either.

> *I let go, and it went where it was pointing, and it was not used up. It was given what it wanted.*
>
> *I have never seen a want given what it wanted. I kept them so that they would never be. That was the point.*
>
> *I did not know it would look like that.*

Xiaoyu wrote it on the step under the other line. *LET GO. IT WENT HOME.*

"*What do you want now?*" she said.

The attention was quiet for a very long time.

> *...I do not know.*
>
> *I have the first one still. The one that said home. I do not know whose it was or where it points. Somewhere very far. Somewhere older than gardens.*
>
> *I have every other want I have ever kept. More than there are stars in your sky.*
>
> *I do not need the soldier's want to find you now. I know the way. I was following it, and now I know where it went.*
>
> *I am still coming. Slowly. I would like to.*
>
> *Not to ask everyone what they want. I said that on the mountain. I do not think I want that now. I do not know what I want.*
>
> *I would like to come and be asked.*

---

It began to lift. Then it stopped.

> *Child. One more thing. I said I would tell first. I learned that from your brother.*
>
> *The hand that is coming down the thread to the stone by the sea. The one that turned.*
>
> *It is not coming for the stone.*
>
> *It sees by burning. It has a lantern, but the lantern is getting dim. It needs more light.*
>
> *Your people have their hands on the stones. Thousands. And the stones have opened a little holding in each of them.*
>
> *Opening is opening. A little holding is a little bright.*
>
> *To a thing that sees only burning, four thousand people with their hands on a stone are the brightest thing on your world.*
>
> *That is what it is coming for.*

And then it was gone, up out of the dark over the two rivers, the long way, slowly, like a very old person getting up from a table after a visit, thinking about something.

On the ferry in the middle of the river, a dead corporal of the Third Battalion and a soaking woman of nearly fifty sat holding each other where the two colors met and did not mix. On the landing, a girl knelt with a marker over three lines of capitals on a stone step. And on the radio at Kwan's belt, very faint, from a hall by the sea on the Atlantic coast, a heavy accented voice said:

"*Sterling. Port Halvard. Holders: eleven thousand. The queue goes round the harbor.*"

> **Five days.**
