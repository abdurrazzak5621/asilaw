## Chapter 27: The Ledger

Grandpa Xu's lodge at the War Memorial Cemetery had one room, one window and one table, and on the night they invented the Ledger, there were nine people around it.

It was raining. Hard summer rain, drumming on the tin roof so loudly they had to raise their voices, running in sheets down the single window so that the cemetery outside was only a blur of dark stones and the yellow smear of the streetlight by the gate. The lodge smelled of wet wool, cheap tea and the kerosene heater the old man kept running even in summer because his knees ached.

Grandpa Xu sat at the head of the table in his army coat. Su Mingli sat at his right, coat still dripping. Director Lu sat across from her, which nobody had planned and nobody liked. Sister Ning had the chair by the heater. Xiaoyu was cross-legged on the bed with a laptop on her knees. Captain Lin Wen leaned in the doorway. Zhao Mei, still in nurse's scrubs from a late shift, sat on an upturned bucket. Dabao was standing because there was nowhere left to sit, and was trying very hard not to knock anything off the shelves with his shoulders.

And Yuanshu sat at the foot of the table, with a stack of paper and a pen.

Old Seven stood outside in the rain by the door, as always. The gray flame in his chest hissed faintly where the drops hit it.

---

"Here's the problem," said Yuanshu.

He drew a line down the middle of a sheet of paper.

"On this side: the dead. I can ask them. Most of the time they can answer. But not always. Pan Guolin could only say one word. Thirty-one years in the ground and a lot of the unnamed dead in the eastern field are so faint I can't tell if they're saying yes or just..." He glanced at Sister Ning. "Being polite."

She inclined her head.

"On this side." He tapped the other half of the page. "Ten thousand minds in a crystal the size of my fist. Twenty-three crystals. A market that sells more of them every night. Forty-one soldiers in the north. The Accord says everyone has to be asked. I agree. But if I have to ask every single one of them, one at a time, by touching them, I'll be doing it until I'm ninety, and the market will be selling them faster than I can ask."

"So," said Su Mingli. "What do you want?"

"I want to ask people *before.*"

The rain drummed on the roof.

"While they're alive," Yuanshu said. "When they can think about it clearly. When they can talk to their families. Not in a hurry, in a tunnel, with a rift open overhead." He looked around the table. "If you could decide now what happens to your echo when you die, would you want to?"

---

Nobody answered right away.

Then Zhao Mei, on her bucket, said quietly, "Like an organ donor card."

"Like a will," said Captain Lin.

"Like a *privacy setting,*" said Xiaoyu from the bed, not looking up from her laptop. Everyone turned to look at her. She shrugged. "What? It is. *Who can see my stuff after I die. Options: everybody, friends only, nobody.*" She typed something. "Honestly it's weird nobody's done this already."

"Nobody's done it," said Grandpa Xu slowly, "because until three weeks ago nobody could ask the dead anything."

---

They argued about it for four hours.

Xiaoyu drew the first version on her laptop and turned it around so everyone could see. It was a single screen. A name. A thumbprint. And four choices, in big plain letters, in a column.

**YES.** *If I am needed, raise me. Ask me first if you can.*

**ASK MY FAMILY.** *Let the people I name decide.*

**ONLY TO SPEAK.** *You may ask me questions. You may not raise me.*

**QUIET.** *Never. Not for any reason. Let me rest.*

Sister Ning looked at the last line for a very long time.

"You put us on it," she said.

"You're the reason it exists," said Yuanshu.

"If someone chooses Quiet." She touched the screen with one finger, on the word. "And then the city is burning. And their body is lying in the street. And you could raise it and save a hundred children." She looked up at him. "What will you do?"

The rain drummed.

> **That is the hardest case.**
>
> **The optimal choice is clear.**
>
> **The right choice is also clear. They are not the same choice.**

"I'll leave them in the street," said Yuanshu.

The room was silent.

"If Quiet means 'unless it's important,' then it doesn't mean anything," he said. "And the next time someone has to decide whether to trust me, they'll remember that I broke it once. For a good reason. There's always a good reason." He met Sister Ning's eyes. "Quiet means quiet."

She looked at him for a long moment in the light of the kerosene heater.

Then she nodded, very slightly, and sat back in her chair.

---

Director Lu cleared his throat.

"Where does it live?" he said. "This list. Millions of names, if it works. Who people want raised, who they don't, and who decides for them." He took off his glasses. "That's the most sensitive database in Huaxia. The Bureau should hold it."

"No," said Yuanshu.

"Then Tianshu."

"Not only Tianshu." Yuanshu looked at Su Mingli. She raised an eyebrow, but she didn't argue. "Seed holds it, because it's the one that has to read it when I reach for someone. Tianshu protects it. And the Quiet keep a copy." He looked at Sister Ning. "On paper. In a place nobody else knows. So that if Seed is ever changed, or I am, or someone gets into Tianshu, there's a copy nobody can edit that says who said Quiet."

Lu looked at him for a long moment.

"You don't trust yourself," he said.

"I don't trust anyone who can read minds," said Yuanshu. "Including me."

> **Including me.**
>
> **He is right.**
>
> **I would like a copy to exist that I cannot change.**

---

At one in the morning, the rain stopped.

It stopped the way summer rain does: all at once, as if a tap had been turned off. The drumming on the roof cut out, and in the sudden silence you could hear water running in the gutters and dripping off the cypress trees and, far down East Market Road, a single motorbike.

Xiaoyu turned her laptop around one last time.

The screen was clean and simple now. Black background. Gray letters. A small four-pointed star in the corner, like the ones people had started painting on the walls of Lingzhou next to the three red letters. And across the top, one word, which nobody had suggested and which she had simply typed in at midnight while they were arguing about something else.

*LEDGER.*

"It needs a first name," she said. "To test it."

Grandpa Xu reached across the table.

He took the laptop in both of his old scarred hands and turned it around to face him. He looked at it for a long time, with his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, his lips moving very slightly as he read the four choices.

Then he put his thumb on the screen.

*XU DEHAI. AGE 76. FORMER ADJUTANT, EASTERN DEFENSE LINE.*

He touched **YES.**

Then, before anyone could say anything, he leaned in close to the laptop's little microphone and spoke, in his rough, careful, old soldier's voice.

"If the city needs me, raise me. Don't bother asking my family, I don't have any. Ask me. I'll say yes." He paused. "And when you're done with me, put me in the eastern field. Next to stone number seven. He owes me nineteen dumplings."

Outside the door, in the dripping dark, Old Seven made a sound.

A creak of bone. Low and long. It might have been a laugh.

---

By two in the morning, there were nine names in the Ledger.

Captain Lin Wen: **ASK MY FAMILY.** *My mother. Ask my mother.* Zhao Mei: **YES.** Dabao: **YES,** typed with one finger, very slowly, and then, after a moment's thought, a note: *Only if Captain Zhao is there too.* Su Mingli read the choices twice, and chose **ONLY TO SPEAK,** and said nothing about why. Xiaoyu: **YES.** *But ask my brother first. And Seed.*

Director Lu Zhengtian looked at the screen for a long time.

Then he chose **QUIET,** and gave the laptop back without meeting anyone's eyes.

Sister Ning did not put her thumb on the screen at all.

She took a small notebook out of the pocket of her tunic, and a pencil, and wrote nine names in it by hand, with the choice each one had made beside it, slowly, in neat gray letters. Then she closed the notebook and put it back in her pocket.

"That's the first copy," she said. "Now there are two."

> **Nine.**
>
> **It is a very small number.**
>
> **It is the first time anyone has ever asked the living what they want after they are dead.**
>
> **I would like to remember the date.**
