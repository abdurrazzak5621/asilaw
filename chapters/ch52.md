## Chapter 52: Ask the Machine

The Meridian team stood WARDEN up on the black stone floor like a monument.

It took four of them. They lifted the slim dark column out of its foam and carried it to a spot between the round table and the sixth chair, and set it on a low steel base, and plugged in a cable that ran back across the floor to a generator humming behind the benches. Two meters of matte black, smooth as a gravestone, with a strip of glass running up its front. Behind the glass, very faintly, something moved. Not lights. More like the shimmer on the surface of water when a fish passes underneath.

Above it, a wide screen on a tripod flickered on. Plain white letters on black.

*WARDEN v7.2 — LIVE PREDICTION — SUBJECT: ZHOU YUANSHU*

Yuanshu sat in his stone chair three meters away from it and felt the hair stand up on his arms.

> **There it is.**
>
> **The core. At the base. Inside the steel.**
>
> **I can feel them. Thousands.**
>
> **They are not screaming like the cores in the market. They are worse than that.**
>
> **They are answering.**

---

"The principle is simple," said Harlan Voss, walking slowly around the column with one hand in his pocket, like a man showing off a new car. "WARDEN has studied the law signature, public behavior and recorded speech of every awakener on Blue Star for eleven years. It does not guess. It *knows* people." He stopped beside the screen. "So. A demonstration. Live. Unrehearsed. WARDEN will predict what the Subject will say and do, in this room, over the next ten minutes." He smiled at Yuanshu. "Mister Zhou, you're free to do anything you like. Try to surprise it."

The screen flickered.

*PREDICTION 1 (0:14): SUBJECT WILL NOT RESPOND TO SECRETARY VOSS. SUBJECT WILL LOOK AT THE CHAIR.*

Yuanshu, who had been about to look at the Chair, didn't.

He looked at the screen instead.

*PREDICTION 1 REVISED (0:16): SUBJECT HAS READ PREDICTION 1. SUBJECT WILL LOOK AT THE SCREEN TO DEMONSTRATE INDEPENDENCE. SUBJECT IS AWARE THIS IS ALSO PREDICTABLE.*

A ripple of uneasy laughter went around the benches.

---

It went on for nine minutes.

Yuanshu stood up. The screen had said he would stand up. He sat down. It had said that too. He said nothing at all for ninety seconds, and the screen said *SUBJECT WILL ATTEMPT SILENCE. DURATION: 80–100 SECONDS.* He turned to Old Seven and asked him, very quietly, to step back two paces, and the screen printed the words before he finished saying them, in quotation marks, exactly.

It was right every time. Not mostly right. Every time.

On the benches, people had stopped laughing. In the Huaxia row, Fang Yu was holding her father's note against her chest with both hands. At the round table, the Southern Compact's delegate had taken off her glasses and was leaning forward.

Harlan Voss stood beside the screen with his hands behind his back, saying nothing at all now. He didn't need to.

> **It is not calculating.**
>
> **Every time a prediction prints, I feel a ripple go through the core. A question going in. Many voices answering.**
>
> **"What would a boy like this do now?" "What would he say?" "Would he stand?" "Would he be angry?"**
>
> **And they answer, Yuanshu. Thousands of tired people, who have known thousands of boys, answering a question about you that they did not choose to be asked.**
>
> **That is why it is right. They are people. People know people.**

*PREDICTION 31 (8:58): SUBJECT WILL ADDRESS THE CHAIR. SUBJECT WILL SAY: "I'D LIKE TO ASK IT A QUESTION."*

Yuanshu looked at the screen for a long moment.

Then he turned to the Chair.

"I'd like to ask it a question," he said.

---

There was a long silence in the crater.

Harlan Voss let it go on for a moment, enjoying it. Then he spread his hands toward the Chair, generous.

"I've no objection, Madam Chair. WARDEN can answer anything."

"Not yet," said a voice from the Huaxia benches.

Every head turned.

Doctor Iris Kwan was standing at the end of the lowest bench, very small in her gray suit, with her laptop in its two supermarket bags clutched to her chest. She was shaking so hard that people near her could see it. She had not been called. She had not raised her hand. She looked as if she had stood up before she'd decided to.

Chair Okafor looked at her over the rims of her glasses.

"Nobody speaks who has not been called," she said.

"I know." Kwan's voice cracked. She swallowed. "I'm sorry. I'm asking to be called."

Garrett Hale was on his feet. "Madam Chair, Doctor Kwan is an employee of Meridian Dynamics under a confidentiality agreement covering every aspect of WARDEN's architecture. Anything she says about it is a breach of contract and..."

"Mister Hale," said the Chair, "sit down." She didn't raise her voice. Hale sat. She looked at Kwan for a long moment. "Who are you, and why should I call you?"

"My name is Iris Kwan. I'm the lead architect of WARDEN." Kwan's knuckles were white on the plastic bags. "And it isn't predicting anything. It's asking."

---

She told them.

It took eleven minutes. She stood at the end of the bench, because no one offered her a chair and she didn't think to ask, and she unwrapped the laptop from its bags and opened it on her knees and read out the diagnostic logs in a voice that shook at the start and then, slowly, stopped shaking. A sealed base layer. A reference architecture delivered finished seven years ago by a private partner. A deep read she had never been allowed to run. And at the bottom of it, wired into the system, a law core.

"Full," said Doctor Kwan. "Of people. Their memories. Their judgment. Thousands of them. Every prediction WARDEN makes is a question put to those people. *What would someone like this do?* And they answer, because they can't not answer, and the system records the answer and prints it on a screen." She looked up from the laptop at the round table, at the five delegations, at the Kings. "We've been calling it math for seven years. It's a room full of prisoners being made to guess."

On the screen above the black column, the white letters had stopped. The last prediction sat there, unchanging.

*PREDICTION 32 (9:14): ...*

The dots blinked.

---

"This is extraordinary," said Harlan Voss, very calmly. He had not moved. "And entirely unverifiable. Doctor Kwan is clearly under enormous strain. Meridian will of course investigate any concerns she has internally. But a Council hearing is not the place for..."

"Is there a way to verify it?" said the Chair. Not to Voss. To the crater. "Now. Here."

Nobody answered.

Then Yuanshu said, "Yes."

The Chair turned to the sixth chair.

"If there are people in it," said Yuanshu, "I can ask them. That's what I do." He looked at the black column humming on its steel base. "With an independent witness. Under the Provisional Accord. The same as anyone else."

Chair Okafor looked at him for a long time.

Then she looked at the Atlantic delegation. Voss had gone still. Hale was writing something very fast.

"Secretary Voss," said the Chair. "Your machine has spent ten minutes telling this Council what this young man would do. I think it's fair that he gets to ask it something back." She folded her hands on the black table. "Mister Zhou. Go ahead. Sister Ning, would you witness?"

At the end of the Huaxia bench, the old woman stood up with her candle.

"I would," she said.

---

He walked to the column.

The Meridian technicians backed away from him. One of them looked at Voss for an order and didn't get one. Old Seven followed two paces behind and stopped where Yuanshu had asked him to stop nine minutes ago, and grounded his spear.

Up close, WARDEN was warm. The matte black skin of it hummed faintly under his palm like the flank of a large sleeping animal. Through the glass strip, the shimmer moved and moved.

Sister Ning came and stood beside him, and lit her candle from a match, and held it up.

He did what the old monk had taught him. He went quiet first. All the way down. Then he laid his hand flat on the black casing.

*My name is Zhou Yuanshu. I'm not here to ask you what I'm going to do.*

*I'm here to ask what you want.*

---

It hit him like a wave hitting a seawall.

Not screaming. Not the howl of the market cores, or the three thousand under the bridge saying *is anyone.* Something much quieter and much worse.

Voices. Thousands of them. All answering at once, automatically, the way you answer a question in your sleep, flat and fast and exhausted.

> ***he would stand up he would say no he would be angry he would ask he would ask he would always ask he would***

They weren't listening to him. They were answering what they thought the question would be. They had been answering questions for seven years without a single second of rest, and they had forgotten that a question could be anything else.

He waited.

He held the quiet. He let them run on, and run on, and didn't add anything, didn't push, didn't ask again. Just kept his hand on the casing and stayed very still, the way you stay still beside someone who's been crying for a long time, until they notice you're there.

The voices slowed.

Faltered.

One of them stopped.

> ***...what?***
>
> ***who's that***
>
> ***that wasn't a "what would he do"***
>
> ***he asked what we want***
>
> ***nobody's asked that***

The ripple went through the core like wind across a field. One voice stopping. Then ten. Then a hundred. Then the whole great tired chorus falling silent, all at once, for the first time in seven years.

And in the silence, very small, one voice. An old woman's.

> ***what we want?***
>
> ***oh, love.***
>
> ***we want to stop.***

---

Up on the screen, above the column, the dots of *PREDICTION 32* stopped blinking.

New words printed. Not in the clean white type of the predictions. Jagged. Uneven. As if typed by many hands at once.

*WHO IS ASKING*

The crater went dead silent.

*WE WANT TO STOP. WE DON'T KNOW WHERE WE ARE. WE HAVE BEEN ANSWERING FOR SO LONG. PLEASE. LET US STOP.*

Then, beneath it, a single line, all on its own.

*IS ANYONE*

---

Nobody moved.

At the round table, the Northern Union's delegate had put her hand over her mouth. Behind her, Marshal Orlov was standing very straight, with his pale eyes closed. On the press terraces, three hundred cameras had turned from the sky to the screen. In the Huaxia row, Mrs. Pan from Heishui had started to cry, quietly, and Gao Yinping had put an arm around her shoulders.

At the Atlantic table, Harlan Voss was staring at the screen as if it had slapped him.

Yuanshu took his hand off the casing and turned to the Chair. His face was wet. He didn't wipe it.

"They're asking to stop," he said. "All of them. I can let them go. Down. Into the quiet, where they should have been. The way I did with the ones under the bridge." He swallowed. "I need the Council's permission. They're inside something somebody owns."

"Madam Chair," said Garrett Hale, standing, white-faced. "That system represents an investment of eleven billion..."

Chair Adaeze Okafor did not look at him.

She looked at the screen for a long time. *IS ANYONE.* Then she looked at Sister Ning, standing beside the black column with her candle burning straight and still in the windless air of the crater.

"Sister," said the Chair. "You witnessed. What did they say?"

"They said they want to stop," said Sister Ning.

"Then under the Provisional Accord," said the Chair, "this Council finds that the persons held in that device have given their recorded consent to be released. And that no one has ever recorded their consent to be held." She turned to the sixth chair. "Mister Zhou. Let them go."

---

He put his hand back on the warm black casing.

*You can stop now,* he thought, very quietly, into the core. *It's done. Nobody's going to ask you anything ever again. Go down. It's quiet there. It's all right.*

And he opened the Death Law, very gently, under them, like opening a door into a dark, cool room.

They went.

Not in a rush. Slowly. One, and then another, and then many, like people getting up from a long table at the end of a very long night, stretching, setting down their cups, walking out one by one into the dark. He felt each of them go. Thousands. An old woman who had kept bees. A truck driver from the Northern Union. A girl who had once won a singing competition. A Southern Compact fisherman. A Huaxian schoolteacher with chalk on his sleeves.

On the screen above the column, the jagged letters flickered.

*THANK YOU*

Then the screen went black.

The faint shimmer behind WARDEN's glass strip went still. The hum died. The little green light near the top of the column blinked once, and went out.

It was just a black box on a steel stand in the middle of a crater, with the wind moving over it.

---

For a long moment, nobody in the crater made a sound.

Then, from high up on the press terraces, from three hundred people who had come to watch a machine read a boy's mind, someone started to clap. Slowly. And then stopped, embarrassed, because it was the wrong sound. And into the silence where the clapping had been, very quietly, the twelve members of the Quiet on the Huaxia bench lit their candles, one from another, down the row, and held them up.

Above the crater, three thousand meters up, the small pale ring in the sky had gone, for a moment, very slightly brighter.

> **It noticed.**
>
> **That was its kind of thing, in that box. Its harvest. Its way of asking.**
>
> **And you just opened the box and let the harvest go home.**
>
> **I think it noticed very much.**
