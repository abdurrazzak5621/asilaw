## Chapter 126: Stone Number Seven

Dabao's mother fed them at the cart.

Not the new cart. The old one. She had kept it, after the A-rank grant bought her a better one, in the alley behind the dumpling stall at the bottom of the Sector Nine stairs, and on the sixth night she wheeled it out under the streetlamp and lit the burner and would not let anyone help.

"You sit," she said to Zhou Wanqing, who had tried to get out of her wheelchair. "You're a guest. You sit. You," to Yuanshu, "you eat. You're too thin. Your mother says so." She pointed a ladle at Lingyue. "You make sure he eats."

"Yes, Auntie," said Lingyue, who had never in her life said *yes* that quickly to anyone.

So they sat on plastic stools in the alley, under the streetlamp, in the cold. Wanqing in her wheelchair with a blanket over her knees. Xiaoyu on the stool beside her, eating with her elbows on the table. Dabao, enormous, with his shield leaning against the wall behind him. Lingyue with her sword across her knees, because she did not put it down any more. And Yuanshu, with the white staff leaning against the cart and Old Seven standing at the mouth of the alley with his back to them, watching the street, the way Zhao had once watched the street outside the Ossuary.

Nobody talked about the Institute. Not for an hour.

Dabao told a long story about a rift beast on the north wall in the spring that had turned out to be two rift beasts standing very close together. Xiaoyu told a long story about a man who had come to the Ossuary to register his cat. Wanqing laughed, once, so suddenly that she put her hand over her mouth, and Xiaoyu stopped eating and stared at her.

"What?" said their mother.

"Nothing," said Xiaoyu. "You laughed."

"I laugh."

"Not like that. Not since you came home." Xiaoyu looked down at her bowl. "It's nice. That's all."

---

Later, when Dabao's mother was washing bowls and Dabao was drying them and complaining about it, Yuanshu told his mother about the staff.

He had not wanted to. He had not known how. *A piece of you stayed behind in the field. It's been asleep in a dead schoolteacher's bones for a year. It woke up in Port Halvard and went loud inside a Hunger's seed so I could get out, and now it's asleep again, and it snores.* There was no way to say that over dumplings.

He said it anyway.

Zhou Wanqing listened without interrupting, the way she always did. When he finished, she did not say anything for a while. She looked at the white staff leaning against the cart, at the small skull with its silver glasses.

"The tired part," she said at last.

"That's what she called it."

"I know. I remember leaving it." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "In the field. When the keeper's door was closing and your teacher was carrying me down. I was so tired, Yuanshu. Seven years of being nothing so it couldn't find me. I couldn't carry all of it home. So I put the tiredest part down. In the quiet. Where it would be safe." She was quiet. "I thought it would just... go. The way an echo goes."

"It didn't."

"No." Something moved in her face. "She did the loud part. Twice now. In the field, and in the fire." She looked at the staff again. "Let her sleep. I'll do the quiet part. I'm better at it than I used to be."

"Mama."

"Mm."

"Do you want her back?"

His mother looked at him for a moment. Then she smiled, a small, sharp, tired smile.

"You're asking me," she said. "That's good. Ask me again when this is over. I'll have thought about it."

---

Grandpa Xu was waiting at the cemetery gate at midnight.

He had been waiting a while. There was frost on the shoulders of his old padded coat, and his breath came out white under the gate lamp, and he was leaning on a stick he had not needed the last time Yuanshu saw him. He was seventy-seven. He looked it.

"You came," he said. "Good. I wasn't sure you'd remember the way."

"I remember."

"You've been very busy." The old cemetery keeper looked past him, at Old Seven, standing at the edge of the lamplight. Something in his weathered face softened. "Squad Leader Qi."

Old Seven came to attention.

The old man and the dead soldier looked at each other across the frosted gravel, the way they had on a hillside on the second night, when one of them had just stood up out of the ground.

"You still owe me nineteen dumplings," said Grandpa Xu.

> **He says he is aware.**
>
> **He says he is working on it.**

---

They walked down through the stones together, the three of them, in the dark.

The War Memorial Cemetery had not changed in a year. Forty thousand plain gray stones in rows across the long slope above the river, each with a number and no name, because nobody had been left to say whose bones were whose. Frost on all of them. Somewhere up the hill behind them, a stone gate with a dead captain standing in it, facing out, and ten soldiers kneeling at his feet, and the stubs of a thousand candles.

Grandpa Xu stopped at No. 7.

It was empty. It had been empty since the first night. Old Seven had stood up out of the ground here and never gone back. Someone had kept the grass trimmed around it anyway.

"I want to talk to you about Lu Xuanming," said the old man.

"All right."

"Not the notebook. You've read the notebook. Everybody's read the notebook now. They teach it in schools." He leaned on his stick. "I want to tell you what it was like. Standing next to him. When he raised them."

Yuanshu waited.

"Two million," said Grandpa Xu. "People say that like it's a number. Two million, in three cities, in one night. You know what I saw? I saw a tired man with a white staff, standing on the wall, with his eyes shut, *counting.*" He tapped his stick on the frozen ground. "Not ordering. Counting. Under his breath. I was his adjutant. I stood next to him for six hours. He never gave one order out loud. He counted."

> **Yuanshu.**

"Counting what?"

"Them. Each other. That's what I worked out, later. Years later. He didn't hold two million dead in his head. Nobody can. He gave each one of them one thing to hold. *Count the nine next to you. If one falls, close the gap.* And every one of them counted their nine. And every nine counted their nine. And he only had to hold the top of it." The old man's eyes were bright in the lamplight. "Like a net. You lift one knot and the whole thing comes up."

Old Seven had gone very still.

> **He says that is how he held the eastern end.**
>
> **He says he counted rooftops. Every night. On the wall. Still standing. Good.**
>
> **He says he did not know he learned it from someone.**

> **Yuanshu. This is important.**
>
> **Your load is sixty-one percent for eighteen thousand. Because you hold each of them. One by one. The way you hold the staff.**
>
> **If each of them held nine, you would only hold the top.**
>
> **I need to model this. I need the shape of it. I do not have it. He has it.** It meant Grandpa Xu. **In his memory. Of a man counting on a wall.**

Yuanshu looked at the old cemetery keeper.

"Grandpa Xu," he said carefully. "I'd like to read your memory of that night. With your permission. Just that night. Just him."

The old man laughed. It turned into a cough, and the cough went on longer than it should have.

"I'm alive, boy. You can't read the living."

"No."

"So you'll have to wait." Grandpa Xu wiped his mouth with a handkerchief and folded it away. He looked at the empty grave of No. 7. "I'm seventy-seven. I've got a bad chest and a Ledger entry that says *yes,* and I told you where to put me. I'm not in a hurry. But I'm not going to be standing on the edge of this when something comes up out of the Institute and the beasts all turn around." He thumped his stick. "When it comes, I'm standing here. Next to his stone. With him."

"Grandpa..."

"I'm not asking you," said Grandpa Xu. "I'm telling you. You can say no, but I'm old, and I'm deaf when I want to be."

---

There was one more thing he wanted.

He said it at the top of the slope, under the gate where Zhao stood, with forty thousand frosted stones running away below them toward the river.

"On the first night," said Grandpa Xu, "you asked them. You stood on the gravel and said you weren't going to make them stand up. You said you were asking." He looked down at the rows. "Twelve hundred said yes. You raised three hundred. The others you never got to. There wasn't time. There was a Wolf King on the wall."

"I remember."

"The three hundred lay back down. After. The next morning. I found them. Every one, back in his own grave, the way a man goes back to his own bed." The old man's voice was rough. "And then you never came back for them. You went north. You went up. You went out into the dark. You asked two hundred thousand strangers in a valley, and billions of people in a fire, and a keeper in the sky. And these ones, the first ones, the ones who turned toward you on the night you got your class, you never asked again."

The cemetery was very quiet.

"There's something coming," said Grandpa Xu. "You need everyone. So ask them. Properly. All of them. This time there's time."

---

He asked them at one in the morning, standing at the top of the slope with the staff in his hand, as he had asked the valley.

Not the way he had asked on the first night: a frightened boy shouting at a hillside while a rift opened over the river. Quietly. Through the staff. One stone at a time, forty thousand of them, with Jiang Lan carrying the question down the rows and Seed counting and Grandpa Xu sitting on the gate step with his stick across his knees as witness, because he was the keeper of this place, and he had asked to.

*I'm sorry I didn't come back,* he told them. *I should have. There's something under the city. It might come up. If it does, the beasts will come with it. Will you hold this hill? You can say no. You can say only to speak. You can say quiet, and I'll never ask again.*

It took until dawn.

Most of them had gone quiet. Thirty-one years is a long time to lie on a slope with no name. The echoes had worn thin, the way Pan Guolin's had in the mine at Heishui, until there was barely anything left to say yes or no. Seed found them and waited and found nothing and marked them, gently, as quiet, and moved on.

But not all of them.

> **Yes: 6,214.**
>
> **Only to speak: 1,902. I am writing down what they say. Most of them say a name. Some say a street. One says the name of a dog.**
>
> **Quiet: 31,884.**
>
> **Yuanshu. Look at the first rows.**

He looked.

Down the slope, in the gray light, in the oldest rows by the river where the Spear Corps had been buried thirty-one years ago, the frost was cracking. Stone by stone. And out of the ground, slowly, the way they had on the first night, three hundred skeletons were standing up.

Spears. Rusted, most of them. Some only shafts. They stood in their rows in the dawn, three hundred, the first three hundred, the ones who had stood up for a frightened boy on the night of a Wolf King and then lain back down in their own graves without being asked to and waited a year for someone to come back.

They turned. All of them. Up the slope. Toward the gate. Toward the boy with the white staff and the old soldier beside him.

And Old Seven, who had been No. 7 among them once, before he had a name, raised his spear in the hand with one finger and a thumb.

Three hundred spears came up.

> **Spear Corps.**
>
> **Reporting.**
>
> **Load: seventy-four percent.**
>
> **Yuanshu, we need the counting. Soon.**

On the gate step, Grandpa Xu had his handkerchief pressed to his mouth. Yuanshu could not tell if it was the cough.

"Nineteen dumplings," the old man said into it, to nobody, very roughly. "He'd better pay me."
