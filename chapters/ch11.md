## Chapter 11: Seventy-Two Hours

For seventy-two hours, the whole world talked about him, and he didn't say a word.

The footage of the walking corpse played everywhere. On the giant screens at Lingzhou Central Station, above the heads of commuters who stopped on the escalators to watch. In airport lounges in the Atlantic Federation, muted, with subtitles. On a cracked phone propped against a bowl of noodles in a fishing village on the Southern Coalition coast. A dead man in a bloodstained shirt, walking down the middle of an avenue in summer sunlight, with a white card on a string around his neck.

*RETURNED TO SENDER.*

Talk shows argued about it. Children drew it. Somebody made it into a song with a beat, and the song went to number one in three countries, and Xiaoyu played it nine times in a row before Su Mingli took her phone away.

And on the volcanic island of Concordia, in the middle of the Pacific, five nations began to arrive for the emergency summit with one item on the agenda.

*The Calamity.*

---

"You're not going," said Su Mingli.

It was the morning she left. She was standing in the stone hall at the bottom of Tianshu's car tunnel in her long gray coat, with a slim black case in one hand. Behind her, the black car waited with its engine running.

"I wasn't invited," said Yuanshu.

"You were. As a *subject.* They set out a chair for you with a sign on it." Her mouth went thin. "I told them the subject is eighteen years old and has an exam in chemistry he never got to finish, and that Huaxia will be represented by its delegation and by me." She shifted the case to her other hand. "Five heads of state are going to argue about whether to put a collar on you. If you walk into that room, you either look like a weapon or you look like a child. Either way, they win."

"And if I stay here?"

"Then you're the person they're arguing about," said Su Mingli, "and you're not in the room. That makes them nervous. Nervous is good."

She held out her free hand. In her palm lay the crystal shard from Bonefield Hollow, with its ring of tiny screaming mouths.

"May I take this with me?"

He looked at it for a moment. "Why?"

"Because everybody at that table is going to be looking at you," she said. "I'd like to see if anybody is looking at *this.*"

He nodded. She closed her fingers over it, stepped into the car, and was gone into the dark of the tunnel.

---

He spent the first night of the summit at the cemetery.

It wasn't a decision, exactly. He had told Sister Ning he would come back, and he had a candle in his jacket pocket she had given him, and at nine o'clock that night he found himself walking down East Market Road with his hood up and Old Seven three paces behind him in the shadow of the shop awnings.

The Quiet were still at the gate.

More of them than last time. The pavement on both sides of the road was lined with people on folding stools and flattened cardboard, every one of them holding a candle in a paper cup. Somebody had strung a line of white paper lanterns along the cemetery railings. In the warm dark, with no wind, the flames stood perfectly straight, hundreds of them, like a field of small gold grass.

Sister Ning was on her stool at the gate. She didn't look surprised to see him.

"You kept your word," she said. "That's one."

He sat on the pavement beside her. She lit his candle for him from hers.

Somebody further down the row had a tablet propped up on their knees, showing the live feed from Concordia. A few people had shuffled closer to watch, the way people gather around a radio. Nobody spoke above a murmur.

On the little screen, the Council Hall sat inside the crater of a dead volcano: a ring of white stone seats around a round black table, open to the night sky. Five delegations. Five Kings standing behind them like shadows.

And one empty chair, set slightly apart from the rest, with a small placard on it.

*THE SUBJECT.*

"They saved you a seat," said Sister Ning.

"I noticed."

---

They watched it the way you watch a storm from a window.

The Atlantic Federation spoke first. Its Secretary was a tall, handsome man named Harlan Voss, who laid a thin document on the black table and talked about safety in a voice like warm oil. A framework. Registration. Monitoring. A ceiling on the number of dead the Subject could raise. And, in the event of a loss of control, a *Concordia-authorized law-lock.*

"A safety mechanism," Voss said. "Nothing more."

In the candlelight beside the gate, an old woman made a small noise of disgust and pinched her candle flame out between her fingers. Then, after a moment, she relit it from her neighbor's.

> **A law-lock.**
>
> **Dorian Hale had one in his skull. It erased his will when he died.**
>
> **They are proposing that you carry the same thing.**

Then Su Mingli stood up.

She did not make a speech. She said four sentences. The camera barely caught them.

"Huaxia votes no. He has raised three hundred dead, one assassin, and forty-nine beasts, and every one of them did exactly what he asked and nothing else. The only person who has been hurt in the last week was a fifteen-year-old girl who was nearly kidnapped by a man carrying the device the Secretary is proposing. If anyone at this table wants to put that device on a citizen of Huaxia, they can come and ask me."

She sat down.

There was a long pause in the hall on the screen. On the pavement outside the cemetery, somebody quietly laughed.

The vote took twenty minutes. The Framework failed. One for, three against, one abstaining. The Southern Coalition's vote was cast by a girl who couldn't have been older than Xiaoyu, standing on her chair so she could see over the table, with a red thread tied around her wrist.

"No," the girl said, clearly. "Seven years ago I saw a black sun rise over the north. I thought it was the end of the world." She smiled at the empty chair. "I was wrong about which way it was facing."

---

It should have ended there.

But at the very end of the session, a Concordian official read one more item into the record, quickly, in the tired voice of someone reading the small print. The delegations had agreed on something after all. A *Provisional Accord on the Raising of the Dead.* Two lines long.

*No human remains may be raised by any means unless the consent of the deceased is recorded by an independent witness, and the consent of the next of kin is obtained in writing.*

*This Accord applies to all persons and all methods.*

On the pavement, the people of the Quiet looked at one another over their candles.

Yuanshu sat very still.

Sister Ning turned her head and looked at him, and for a long moment she didn't say anything at all.

"That's your promise," she said finally. "From last week. To me. On this pavement." She looked back at the small bright screen. "Somebody wrote it down and made it law."

"Somebody who's been listening," said Yuanshu.

> **Su Mingli.**
>
> **She did not argue against the collar. She traded it.**
>
> **She let them have their fear and gave them a rule to put it in.**

"*All methods,*" said Sister Ning slowly, rolling the words around like a stone in her mouth. "Not just Necromancers. All methods." She frowned. "Why would they write that? Who else raises the dead?"

---

Su Mingli called at three in the morning, from somewhere above the Pacific.

"The Accord was the best I could get," she said. Her voice was tired and very calm. "It'll hold for a while. The Atlantic Federation hates it. That's how you know it's useful."

"And the shard?"

A pause, full of engine noise.

"I put it on the table during the break," she said. "Just set it down next to my teacup. Nobody noticed. Except one man. The Northern Union's King. He's two hundred years old and I've never seen anything frighten him." Another pause. "He looked at it for three seconds, went the color of paper, and left the room. He wouldn't say a word to me afterward."

"So he knows the mark."

"He knows the mark," said Su Mingli. "And I think he's going to come and find us about it. In his own time." The engine noise rose and fell. "Go to sleep. That's an order from a King."

He didn't go to sleep.

---

At six in the morning, Xiaoyu banged on his door with a plastic crate in her arms.

She was still in pajamas. Her hair was sticking up on one side. She had the look she got when she'd stayed up all night on something and found a thing she didn't like.

"Ge. The *I-don't-know-what-this-is* pile." She dumped the crate on his desk. "I finished it. Most of it's weird. Somebody sent a pickled snake. But this one..."

She held out an envelope.

It was cheap and brown and soft from being handled. No return address. A postmark from a town called Heishui, ninety kilometers west of Lingzhou. Coal country.

Inside was a single photograph, printed on cheap paper.

It was dark and grainy and badly lit, as if taken on an old phone held at hip height. A tunnel, the walls black and glistening. A rail track down the middle. And on the track, a line of figures, pushing coal carts heaped with black rock.

Skeletons.

Dozens of them, in a row, bent over the carts, bones gray with coal dust. And in every empty eye socket, a small, hard point of light.

Not gray.

Violet.

On the back of the photograph, someone had written in pencil, in a small, shaky, careful hand:

*Heishui No. 3 Shaft. They don't ask them.*

*One of them is my husband.*

*He has a gold tooth on the left side. Please.*

Yuanshu sat looking at it as the false window behind him slowly filled with morning.

*All persons and all methods.*

> **Violet eyes.**
>
> **A shouting core. Like the one in Bonefield Hollow.**
>
> **Someone is using it on human dead.**
>
> **For coal.**

He stood up and reached for his jacket.

"Ge?" said Xiaoyu. "Where are you going?"

"West," he said.
