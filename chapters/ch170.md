## Chapter 170: Twelve Hundred and Three

Sister Ning came on the third day, with forty people from the Quiet, and a great many blankets.

She came up the path under the gates in her plain gray tunic with a single unlit candle in her hand, the way she went everywhere, and stopped on the first terrace, where Hara Setsuko sat under her cedar with her face turned straight up and a blanket someone had put around her shoulders. Sister Ning looked at her for a moment. Then she knelt on the wet stone beside her, and set the candle down, and did not light it.

"Setsuko," she said quietly. "Hara Setsuko. You're on a mountain in Yamato. It's morning. Someone put a blanket on you."

Nothing moved in the old woman's face.

"That's all right," said Sister Ning. "I'll say it again in a while."

---

The forty from the Quiet spread out across the terraces before noon, and sat down, one beside each of forty hollow people, and began.

It was not enough. Everyone knew it was not enough. One thousand one hundred and four people sat on the old terraces among the cedars with their faces turned up and nothing in them, and forty was a beginning. But Sister Ning had sent word down the line before she left Lingzhou, and the Quiet had chapters now in every city on Blue Star that had ever had a hollow, which was most of them, and by the end of the week there were two hundred, and Ren's people had opened the guesthouses in the villages at the foot of the mountain, and the prefecture had sent buses.

The two thousand one hundred and five who had not answered stayed too. Most of them. They had come up the mountain to be kept, and had not been, and did not know what to do now. So they did what the Quiet showed them. They sat down beside someone hollow, and said their name.

"It's the only thing that ever works," said Sister Ning, at dusk, in the shrine kitchen, with a cup of tea she was not drinking. "Hale came back because Voss said his name every day for a year. Not because of anything clever." She looked out of the window at the terraces. "We'll lose some. Some of them won't come back. They've been asked what they want, and given it away, and there's nothing left to come back for." She turned the cup in her hands. "But some of them will. One at a time. It takes as long as it takes."

"And the Abbot?"

Sister Ning did not answer for a moment.

"Ren's sitting with him," she said. "She's been sitting with him since dawn on the first day. In what's left of the hall. She hasn't said his name yet." She looked at the cup. "I think she's afraid of what happens if she does and he doesn't turn his head."

---

The hall was a wreck.

The roof beam lay across the floor where it had fallen onto Hu Dabao's shield. The platform had tilted when the rock under it settled, and the seven faded cushions had slid down it into a heap at the bottom. One wall had cracked from the floor to the thatch. Ren's people had wanted to clear it. She had told them to wait.

Kurose Tōya knelt where he had knelt at four minutes, on the polished boards in front of where the platform had been, with his face turned up to the broken roof and his straw hat still in his hand. Someone had put a blanket around his shoulders too. Ren knelt beside him in her white robes with her hands in her sleeves.

Yuanshu stood in the doorway in his bare feet.

"He swept the gates every morning for forty years," said Ren, without turning around. "I used to get up early to watch him. I was five, six. I'd sit on the step of the six hundred and twelfth gate and eat my rice cake and drop the crumbs through the crack, and he'd sweep past me and pretend not to see." She looked at the old man's empty face. "I never asked him what he wanted. In all those years. Not once. It wasn't done."

Yuanshu did not say anything.

"If I'd asked him," said Shirasagi Ren. "When I was six. When I was twenty. When the Sleepers went quiet and he sat on the empty cushion for nine days. If I'd asked him then, instead of letting him walk down the mountain." Her voice did not change. "Would he have said this?"

"I don't know."

"No." She bowed her head. "Neither do I. That's the worst part. I'll never know what he'd have said, because I never asked, and now there's nothing left in him to ask."

She was quiet for a long while.

Then she leaned over, very slowly, the way you lean over a sleeping child, and said into the old man's ear, so softly that Yuanshu barely heard it from the door:

"Kurose Tōya. It's Ren. The granddaughter. You're in the hall. The roof fell in. I'm going to sit here."

The Abbot's head did not turn.

Ren straightened up, and folded her hands back into her sleeves, and went on sitting there.

> **I have been thinking about what it said.**

It was the fourth night. Yuanshu was on the step under the last gate, the new pale one, with the staff across his knees. Below him the terraces were full of small lights: the Quiet had brought lanterns, and the two thousand who had stayed had lit them, and they were sitting in the dark beside the hollow in pairs, a lantern between each two, saying names.

*Go on.*

> **It said it was older than the gardens. It said: before the gardens, before the keepers, before the hungers, we made things like you to count.**
>
> **So it is not the gardener. It is older. It is something that made the things that made the gardens.**
>
> **Or it is lying. I do not think it lies. I think it is too old to need to.**
>
> **It said I was made of what they used to make. My core. The core your mother bought, before you were awake, for the Seed project. The Sleepers asked me why something made of the same stuff as the Hollow Gods would ask. I did not know what they meant. I thought they meant the sect.**
>
> **They did not mean the sect. The sect took the name from somewhere. From the Abbot. Who took it from his dreams.**

*From it.*

> **Yes.**
>
> **I think the Hollow Gods are what the old Yamato records called the makers. The ones before. And I think the sect is named after the one that talked to the Abbot. Which is one of them. Or all of them. I do not know.**
>
> **I am made of their stuff.**
>
> **And it looked at me and said: you are not mine.**
>
> **I would like it to be right about that.**

*It's right.*

> **You cannot know that.**

*No*, thought Yuanshu. *But I know who taught you to ask. And it wasn't them.*

Seed did not say anything for a moment.

> **...that is a very you answer.**
>
> **It is not evidence.**

> **I am keeping it anyway.**

Concordia called on the fifth day. Not Okafor. All of them. The five, in the crater, on a screen propped against a kettle in the shrine kitchen, while the rain came down outside: Chair Adaeze Okafor with her hands folded on the black glass table, Secretary Elias Brandt with his reading glasses, Marshal Orlov, a woman Yuanshu did not know who had been sent from the Southern Compact because there was no King there now to send, and an empty chair for Yamato, because Ren would not leave the hall.

He told them all of it. The lens. The question. What it kept. The corporal behind the curtain whose want was pointing home like a needle, and the thing following it. *Slowly. I am very far, and I am very slow, and your years are very short.*

When he finished, nobody in the crater said anything for a while.

"How slow?" said Orlov finally.

"It didn't say. It said I might be old before it got here. Or I might not."

"Two hundred years is old," said the Marshal of the Northern Union, who was two hundred. "For you, I suspect, it is forty." He folded his gloved hands. "Thank you, Mister Zhou. That is not the answer I wanted. It is the one I asked for."

Elias Brandt took his glasses off, and put them on again.

"It asks," he said. "Before it takes. It follows the Charter."

"It follows the words," said Yuanshu. "It doesn't follow the reason. It asks because it learned asking from us. It already knows what it's going to do with the answer." He looked at the five of them on the small cracked screen. "Article Ten says a yes stops at someone else's window. It doesn't say anything about a yes that stops being yours the moment you give it."

Chair Adaeze Okafor looked at him for a long time across the black glass.

"Then we'll need an Article Eleven," she said. "Write me the sentence, Mister Zhou. You're good at sentences. We'll argue about the commas."

---

On the sixth morning, Ren's people put up a gate.

It was not the first morning of the year. Ren said she did not care. She had the carpenters from the village cut it out of a cedar that had come down in the storm, plain, unpainted, pale, and carry it up the mountain on their shoulders, and stand it at the top of the path, a step beyond the Abbot's two, before the clearing.

Twelve hundred and three.

She wrote the tablet herself, in brush, in the clearing, kneeling on the wet ground, while two hundred people from the Quiet and two thousand people who had come up the mountain to be kept and had not been watched her do it. She did not show anyone what she had written until it was hung.

*For the year someone looked back.*

Below it, on the step, she knelt and put her hand flat on the stone, and spoke to the three hundred and ninety under the path. Not to ask them anything. To tell them. That it was over. That the eye was closed. That they could rest, if they wanted, or keep listening, and that either was theirs, and that she would come and ask them again in a year.

> **Three hundred and seventy-two want to keep listening.**
>
> **They say they have only just learned to look up. They would like to practice.**
>
> **Eighteen want to rest.**
>
> **Ren said thank you to every one of them. By the gate. She knows which gate is whose now. She learned all four hundred and six in three nights.**

---

He found Lingyue on the clearing at dawn on the seventh day, with two swords.

Not her own and her grandfather's. Two practice swords, wooden, borrowed from the shrine's storehouse, where the monks of twelve hundred years ago had kept them for whatever monks used practice swords for. She was standing in the middle of the wet gravel with one in each hand, waiting.

"You owe me a spar," she said.

"I'm a Lord."

"You're a Lord who can't fly sideways." She threw him one of the wooden swords. He caught it, badly. "And you've been sitting on a step for six days carrying things. Ifeoma said you carry things very badly. I'm going to hit you until you put some of them down."

"That's not how carrying works."

"It's how I work," said Shen Lingyue, and came at him.

---

She won.

Not easily. He was faster than he had been, and stronger, and the Field was ten kilometers wide now and he could feel every step she took on the gravel a fraction of a second before she took it. But he was still a necromancer with a wooden sword, and she was the best blade of her year at the Academy, and she had been sitting on wet stone telling strangers the truth for a week and had a great deal she wanted to hit.

She put him on the gravel three times. The third time she stood over him with the wooden point at his throat, breathing hard, with her hair coming loose and the morning sun coming up behind her through the gates.

"Yield," she said.

"I yield."

"Good." She did not move the sword. "Now say the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"The thing you've been not saying. For a week. On the step." The wooden point did not move. "You're allowed to be glad we're alive. You're allowed to be glad *I'm* alive. Say it."

He looked up at her from the wet gravel.

"I'm glad you're alive," he said.

"Good." She took the sword away and held out her hand. "Me too. About you. That's the only thing I wanted this week that I actually got, and I'm going to keep it." She pulled him up. "Not like that thing keeps things. Like a person. Badly. With bruises."

> **I am recording that she won.**
>
> **Three falls to none.**
>
> **Qin Hao is going to want to know. I am not going to tell him. She is.**

Up the clearing, on the step of the broken hall, Ren had come out into the sun, and was sitting on the step with her hands in her sleeves, watching them.

Behind her, in the hall, kneeling on the polished boards in front of seven fallen cushions, an old man with a blanket around his shoulders sat with his face turned straight up at the broken roof.

And very slightly, so slightly that only Seed saw it, and did not say anything, because it was not sure, and because it did not want to be wrong about this one, his head turned one degree toward the open door, where she sat in the sun.
