## Chapter 187: The Broom

The Abbot said her name on the hundred and forty-first morning, and Ren called Yuanshu from the step of the broken hall at six in the morning with her voice doing something he had never heard it do, and said: "*Come. Please. I don't know what to say to him.*"

He went.

Not by plane. He could have. The Council would have sent one. But he was a Lord now who could fly sideways, mostly, if he did less, and Yamato was two thousand kilometers east across the sea, and he wanted, for once, to arrive somewhere without anyone carrying him. Lingyue said he was an idiot. She came anyway. Lords both, side by side, over the gray morning sea, low, a hundred meters up, with the Field folded small around them and Seed counting the kilometers and complaining about the wind.

It took nine hours. He fell into the sea twice. Lingyue pulled him out both times without comment, which was worse than if she had said something.

They came up the mountain through the gates at dusk.

---

The hall had been half rebuilt.

Ren's people had cleared the fallen roof beam and put up a new one, raw cedar, unpainted, pale against the old dark wood. The cracked wall had been braced. The platform was still tilted, and the seven faded cushions were still in their heap at the bottom of it, because Ren had told the carpenters to leave them. The floor had been swept.

Kurose Tōya was sitting on the step.

Not on the floor in front of the platform, where he had knelt for a hundred and forty days with his face turned straight up at the broken roof. On the step. Outside. In the dusk, in his gray robe, with a blanket around his shoulders and his straw hat on his knee and his hands folded, looking down the clearing at the gates.

His face was turned forward. Level. Like anyone's.

Ren was sitting beside him on the step, a little way off, with her hands in her sleeves.

When Yuanshu and Lingyue came out of the last gate into the clearing, the Abbot turned his head and looked at them. All the way. The way anyone turns their head when someone comes into a room.

"Ah," he said. His voice was rough, as if it had not been used for a long time. It had not. "The boy who asks." He looked at Lingyue. "And the girl with the swords. Ren said you would come." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm sorry. I don't remember very much. I remember you. I don't remember why."

---

They sat on the step, the four of them, in the dusk, with the cedars dripping and the single falling bird-note somewhere in the dark, again and again.

"He said my name," said Ren, very quietly, to Yuanshu, not looking at him. "Yesterday morning. I said his, the way I say it every morning. And I sat down. And after a while he said: *Ren.* Just that. And then he turned his head and looked at me." She did not move. "I didn't know what to do. I've been saying his name for nearly five months. I never thought about what I'd say if he said mine."

"What did you say?"

"I said: good morning." Her hands were very still in her sleeves. "It was the stupidest thing I've ever said."

"It was the right thing," said Lingyue.

The Abbot was looking down the clearing at the gates. At the three new pale ones at the top of the path, a step apart, with their wooden tablets hanging from the crossbeams.

"Those are new," he said.

"Two of them are yours," said Ren. "You put them up. Before you left. Last year."

The old man looked at them for a long time.

"I don't remember," he said. "What did I write on them?"

Ren told him. *For the year the Sleepers stopped keeping watch, and the year somebody else started.*

Kurose Tōya was quiet.

"That's a sad thing to write," he said. "Was I sad?"

"Yes," said Ren. "For a long time."

"And the third one?"

"Mine." She did not look at him. "*For the year someone looked back.*"

---

Seed had been quiet since the clearing. It spoke now, carefully, in the place behind his eyes.

> **He does not remember wanting to be kept.**
>
> **He remembers the hall. The gates. Sweeping. Ren as a child. Rice cakes. He remembers the Sleepers, and that they went quiet.**
>
> **Everything after that is very thin. Like a page that has been written on and then erased. He knows something was written there. He cannot read it.**
>
> **He does not remember the seeds. The scout. The broadcasts. Hendrik. The three thousand on the terraces. The lens.**
>
> **He does not remember what he wanted.**
>
> **It is kept. Above the sky. In the deep water. Very bright. Never used up.**
>
> **Tanaka Ryo said: like reaching for a step in the dark, and the step is not there.**

"I'm told I did terrible things," said the Abbot.

He said it without looking at anyone. Looking down the clearing at the gates, with his hands folded on his straw hat.

"Ren won't tell me what. She says I should ask when I'm ready." He was quiet. "I'm told a great many people sat on the terraces because of me. And that eleven hundred of them are still sitting there. With their faces turned up." He looked down at his hands. "I can see them. In the mornings. From here. The people in gray sitting beside them, saying their names." His voice did not change. "I did that."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't remember why," said Kurose Tōya. "I think that is the worst part. If I remembered why, I could be sorry for the reason. I can only be sorry for the thing."

---

> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **This is what the Architects said.**
>
> **We do not remember what we wanted. There is a place where it should be. We are sorry for the thing.**

He sat on the step in the dusk and looked at the old man beside him, and thought about seven tall gray shapes kneeling in the grass on a hill above Port Abiri, saying sorry for two hours, for a fire they had lit a hundred thousand worlds ago and could not remember the reason for.

"Kurose Tōya," he said. "I'm going to ask you something. You don't have to answer."

The Abbot turned his head and looked at him.

"Ren said you would do that." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not the small sad smile from the recordings. Something smaller. Newer. "She said you always do it. She said it's very annoying."

"It is," said Lingyue.

"I'm going to tell you first what I'll do with the answer," said Yuanshu. "Whatever you say, I'll leave it here. With you. On this step. I won't take it to Concordia. I won't put it on a wall. It's yours." He was quiet. "What do you want?"

---

The old man did not answer for a long time.

The bird called in the dark. The cedars dripped. Down the mountain, on the terraces, small lights were coming on, one by one, where the Quiet were lighting lanterns beside the people who had not come back yet.

"I don't know," said Kurose Tōya.

He said it slowly, as if he were trying the words for the first time.

"I don't know what I want. There's a hole where it was. I reach for it, and it isn't there." He looked down at the straw hat on his knee. "Is that allowed? To not know?"

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

"For how long?"

"As long as it takes."

The Abbot was quiet.

"Then I'll tell you what I'd like," he said. "Not what I want. I don't have that any more. What I'd like. Small things." He looked down the clearing at the gates in the dusk. "I'd like a broom."

Ren turned her head.

"Not for the hall," said Kurose Tōya. "I'm told I swept the hall. I'm told what I swept out of it." His voice did not change. "Not the hall. The path. The gates. Just the leaves. The cedars drop a great many leaves. Somebody should sweep them." He looked at his hands. "I'd like to sweep them, in the mornings. Until I know what I want. And then I'd like someone to ask me again."

"I'll ask you," said Ren.

Her voice cracked down the middle. She did not try to steady it.

"Every morning. I'll come down the path while you're sweeping and I'll ask you. And you don't have to answer. And I'll ask you again the next day." She took her hands out of her sleeves. "That's what I want. You asked me, once. On these steps. And I didn't have an answer. I have one now."

The old man looked at her for a long moment. At the girl he had given rice cakes to on the temple steps when her grandmother was not looking. At the King in white who had sat beside him on the floor of a broken hall for a hundred and forty days and said his name.

"You've grown up," he said.

"Yes," said Shirasagi Ren. "While you weren't looking."

---

In the morning, at dawn, Yuanshu stood under the last gate with Lingyue and watched a thin old man in a gray robe come down the path from the hall with a bamboo broom in his hands.

He swept slowly. Not well. He had forgotten how, he said, or he had never been as good at it as he thought, and the leaves blew back across the stone behind him in the wind off the sea. He swept under the twelve hundred and third gate, and the twelve hundred and second, and the twelve hundred and first, and went on down the mountain, under the black gates worn gray at the edges, one by one, toward the terraces, where the people in gray were waking up beside the people who had not come back yet.

Halfway down, at the six hundred and twelfth gate, Ren was waiting on the step with a rice cake.

She did not give it to him. She broke it in half, and ate one half, and dropped the crumbs through the crack in the step, the way she had when she was six.

He stopped sweeping and looked at her.

"What do you want?" said Ren.

The old man leaned on his broom for a while.

"I don't know yet," said Kurose Tōya. "Ask me tomorrow."

"I will," said Ren.

> **The monk under the six hundred and twelfth gate says he liked the crumbs.**
>
> **He says it has been a long time.**

---

They flew home that afternoon. He fell in the sea once. Lingyue did not pull him out until he had asked.

> **I would like it noted that you asked very quickly.**

"I was drowning."

> **You were not drowning. You were a Lord in a meter of water off a beach. You were sulking.**

"Thank you, Seed."

> **You are welcome. I have also noted that she waited.**
>
> **She has learned Article Eleven very well.**
>
> **She told me, while you were in the water, what she would do with your answer before you gave it. She said: if you ask, I'll pull you out. If you don't, I'll watch.**
>
> **I think that is the most romantic thing I have ever recorded.**
>
> **I am not recording it.**
