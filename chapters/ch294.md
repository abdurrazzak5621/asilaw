## Chapter 294: Tomorrow

On the eleventh morning after the Bow, Ostrava Iron went down to the arrays of her own ship and asked the first cell.

Halvarde told him. She came across from the flagship on the little ship the size of a bus, which she said made her sick, to the yards at Anthos, where he was sitting on the floor of a long bright hall full of children learning to wear shoes, with his back against a cradle somebody had planted beans in. She sat down beside him with the sound old people make sitting down, and did not say anything for a while.

"*She asked me every day,*" said Halvarde at last. "*Ostrava. For ten days. What I wanted. Same as I asked her.*" She looked at the beans. "*I said: to open the walls. Every time. She'd nod and go away.*"

"*And today?*"

"*Today she said: show me how.*" The old C rubbed her eyes with the back of her hand. "*So I took her down to the first array of the Iron Tower. A hundred and ten thousand people. I showed her the cradle. I showed her how you ask first. By name. You have to know the name. The ship's registry has them; nobody's read it in three hundred years.*" She was quiet. "*She read the first one out loud. Off the registry. A boy. F. From a world called Dunmere. Sixty-one years in the wall.*"

"*What did he say?*"

"*He said: what's your grade,*" said Halvarde. "*They all say that first. The ones who've been in a long time.*" She almost smiled. "*And Ostrava Iron, Paragon of the House of Iron, who walked past the First of the Ladder in a corridor and never got a scratch, said: I don't have one. Not any more. What do you want?*"

---

Not all of them opened.

Of the twenty-seven Houses who had walked out of the Assembly, eleven opened their arrays in the forty days after the Bow, asked first, the way the Assembly had decided. Nine of them did it because their Paragons came back from the Lens chamber with their burning turned down and went to their own arrays and asked. Two did it because their C's asked first, and their Captains said yes.

Sixteen did not.

They folded on the twentieth night. Not toward the Shallows. Out. Away from Anthos and the Assembly and the inner worlds, outward along the arm, toward the far edges of the Federation where the Ladder's lists ran out. Sixteen Houses. Eighty ships. Burning their cores the old way.

> **Ichiro saw them go.**
>
> **He can see them still. Through their faces. They did not throw their faces away. They still wear them.**
>
> **Two lines. True. They cannot unread it.**
>
> **They are going somewhere they think they can build the Ladder again. Where nobody has read the mark.**
>
> **Ichiro says there is nowhere like that any more. He says every face in every House was in the Lens when the mark went out.**
>
> **He says they will find that out.**

"*They'll come back,*" said Seri, at the window of the observation deck, watching the eighty lights go out one after another in the dark. "*Or we'll go to them.*"

She had said it once before, on the north wall of Lingzhou, watching twenty-nine go. Nobody had forgotten.

---

On the twenty-first morning, the Speaker of the Assembly was asked.

She had said *tomorrow*. Every day. For twenty-one days. Every morning, at the opening of the Assembly, a Paragon of the House of Teller, young, with tears he had stopped trying to wipe off his face and a face of his own in a box in his pocket, stood up in the eighth tier and asked the Speaker what she wanted. And every morning, from the seat of white stone at the front of the lowest tier, Aurelle Teller, who had been Speaker for a hundred and nine years, said: *tomorrow*.

On the twenty-first morning, she said something else.

He was there. Kael had asked him to be. He sat in the fourth tier beside Corvane, who had come up from the reading-hall below the House for the first time in three weeks, and watched the young Paragon stand up in the eighth tier and ask.

"*Speaker,*" said Kael Teller. Without the grade. "*What do you want?*"

The old woman in the seat of white stone looked up at him for a long time.

"*To sit down,*" said Aurelle Teller.

The Assembly was silent.

"*I have been standing up in this room for a hundred and nine years,*" said the Speaker. "*Every time I speak. That is the rule. The Speaker stands.*" Her voice did not change. "*I would like to sit down. And have someone else stand. And ask me things. And listen to me answer.*" She looked at the young Paragon in the eighth tier. "*I would like it to be you.*"

Kael Teller stood in the eighth tier with his mouth open.

"*Not because you're Teller,*" said the Speaker. "*Because you were the first Paragon who knelt beside the First of the Ladder and asked him what he wanted.*" She put her hands on the arms of the seat of white stone, and pushed, slowly, the way old people stand up when they have decided to. "*And because you're crying. You've been crying for twenty-one days. A Speaker who can cry in this room is the only kind I'd sit down for.*"

She came down out of the seat of white stone.

And sat on the step below it. On the bare black stone of the lowest tier. With her hands folded in her lap.

"*Ask me tomorrow,*" said Aurelle Teller, "*what I want next. I'll have thought of something.*"

---

He went to the Lens chamber that night to ask Ichiro and Kiyo.

He had been putting it off. He knew he had. Seed had said so, gently, on the eleventh morning and again on the twentieth. He had said *tomorrow* to Seed, and Seed had said *that is allowed*, and then, on the twenty-first night, Seed had said *Yuanshu, it is tomorrow*, and he had gone.

The chamber in the bow of the flagship was quiet. The hole the ten Paragons had made in the wall had been sealed by the C's with something like glass, so that the stars of Anthos showed through it. The floor where a man in a torn gray coat had sat with his back against the root had been cleaned. Halvarde had done it herself. She had not let anyone else.

And the Lens. Whole. The width of the chamber. Ground so thin you could see through it, with light moving in it like water, and a lamp burning in it, pale, steady, the color of whale oil in a fog.

He sat down on the floor in front of it, where Severin had sat.

*Ichiro,* he thought. *Kiyo.*

> **He says: you're doing the face.**
>
> **He says: the step face. He's heard about it. The whole net has. Your sister told the Collector and the Collector told everyone.**
>
> **He says: you've come to ask if we're coming home.**

*Yes.*

> **He says: we're not.**

He sat on the floor and did not say anything.

> **He says: he's sorry. He knew you'd come. He's been thinking what to say for twenty days. He says that's the longest he's ever thought about anything before saying it, and Kiyo's very proud.**
>
> **He says: the Lens is the root. Every stone in every human orchard is cut from it. And it's whole now. The man in the gray coat put the last bit back.**
>
> **He says: he can see so far. Further than the Federation. Further than the Shallows. Stones he didn't know were there. Thousands of orchards nobody's ever been to. Out along the arms. Past the deep water.**
>
> **He says: nobody in any of them has ever been asked.**
>
> **He says: he'd like to ask them.**
>
> **He says: he can't do that from Shiomi bay. He has to be in the root.**

He looked at the lamp in the Lens.

*And Kiyo?*

The answer came after a moment. Dry. Twelve hundred years old.

> **She says: someone has to hold the lamp.**
>
> **She says: he's going to talk to every orchard in the dark between the arms, for as long as there are orchards. He will never stop. He has never stopped in his life.**
>
> **She says: I sat on the shore for twelve hundred years and waited for a boat. I am not going to sit on the shore while he goes out again.**
>
> **She says: I'm already in the boat.**

---

He sat on the floor of the Lens chamber for a long time.

Then he said, out loud, because it seemed like the kind of thing that should be said out loud: "*Shiomi will ring you out.*"

> **He says: they already did. When we left the bay. He heard it. He says it was the best thing he ever heard, and he heard the boy's daughter born.**
>
> **He says: tell them to ring the boat calls sometimes. Not the going-out bells. The coming-in ones. So he can hear where the shore is, even from out here.**
>
> **He says: tell Kiyo's body in the hall on East Ring Road to keep the lamp lit. The other lamp. Lingyue's first one. He says it's important there's a lamp on both ends.**

*It's her body. It's yours, Sister.*

> **She says: then I'm telling it.**
>
> **She says: keep it lit.**

---

> **And one more thing.**
>
> **He says he's been saving it. He says he put it at the end this time, not the middle. He'd like that noted.**
>
> **He says: when he looked out. Past the Federation. Past the orchards. Further than he's ever seen. With the root whole.**
>
> **He saw something.**
>
> **He says: warm. Patient. Very old. Very slow. Coming along the arm toward the Shallows. The way a very old person walks toward a house they've been invited to.**
>
> **He says: it's closer than it was.**
>
> **He says: much closer.**
>
> **He says: your sister has been asking it every night what it wants. And it has been telling her tomorrow.**
>
> **He says: it's not going to be tomorrow much longer.**

---

They left Anthos on the forty-first day after the Bow, on the Eighth Candle, rowed.

Not the flagship. The flagship stayed, with the Lens in its bow, in the orbit of Anthos, rowed by two hundred and forty thousand people who had chosen to stay in its walls and carry it wherever a fog-ringer wanted to go next. Halvarde stayed with it. She said she was not going to leave thirty-one thousand names on a ship without someone to say them.

Iselde captained the Candle home. She said she would come back to Anthos afterward. She said the Assembly needed people who had cried on a bridge. She said Maelis would be thirteen in the spring and she was not going to miss it.

Maelis came to the yards to see them off.

She stood at the end of the long bright hall full of children learning to wear shoes, by the window, in her gray coat that was a little less too big than it had been, beside her mother. Twelve in the body, still, for four more months. Twenty-four inside.

"*Next year,*" she said, "*I'm going to tell the stone what thirteen was like.*"

"*He'll ask,*" said Yuanshu.

"*He'd better,*" said Maelis Varr-Anthos. "*He owes me. He said.*"

Beside her, the Matriarch of the House of Anthos, in deep red, with no face at her throat. She had taken it off on the morning of the first reading, Iselde said, and put it in a box, and given the box to Maelis, who had two now.

"*Tell your mother,*" said Corvane, "*I asked her.*"

He looked at her.

"*Every day,*" said Corvane Varr-Anthos. "*A hundred and thirty-two days. Maelis. What do you want.*" She did not look away from the window. "*Tell the woman in the wheelchair that I asked my daughter. Tell her I'll keep asking. Tell her she was right. You open your mouth and you find out.*"
