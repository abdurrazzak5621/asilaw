## Chapter 65: Tire Tracks

Sokolova got Lingzhou on the long-range radio at noon, from the top of a ridge, with the antenna strung between Hundred-Arrow Lin's arrowhead and a dead beast's horn.

It took forty minutes of static and three relays. When the voice finally came through, it was Lu's, thin and crackling and very far away, and it sounded as if he had not slept since they left.

Su Mingli told him.

A forty-meter thing of cloudy glass and screaming mouths. Walking south down the valley of the dead. At walking pace. Toward the wall.

There was a long silence on the line. Static hissed.

"How long?" said Lu.

> **Twelve days. Perhaps fourteen. If it does not stop.**

"Twelve days," said Su Mingli.

Another silence.

"Sister Ning has four thousand names," said Lu at last. His voice crackled. "People who can sit in silence for four hours and think about nothing. She's been training them since Concordia, in shifts, in the Ossuary. Every night." A pause. "I'll put them on the wall. All along the northern face. With candles. If it comes, it'll find sixty kilometers of people who aren't thinking anything at all." Another pause. "And the Academy. And the Defense Corps. And Principal Yue, who says he's very good at not thinking and has been practicing for years."

Su Mingli almost smiled.

"Zhengtian," she said. "If it comes. Don't let anyone try to fight it."

"I wasn't going to."

"Somebody will want to."

"Then Sister Ning will tell them to think about rice," said Lu. "And they'll do it. She's terrifying." The static rose. "Go and get the staff, Mingli. And come home."

The line went dead.

---

On the eighth day, they left the river.

It was harder than Yuanshu expected. The valley of the dead bent northeast, following some long-forgotten logic of Lu Xuanming's last order, and Grid 47-121 lay due north, across a broken country of ridges and frozen ravines. They stood on the valley's lip in the morning cold and watched the gray tide go on without them, scrape, scrape, scrape, two hundred thousand shapes flowing away toward the northeastern horizon.

Third Battalion stood in rows on the slope behind them, facing the march.

Sergeant Huo stood at the front. For a long time he did not move. Then he raised his three-fingered hand, slowly, in the direction of the river.

Not a salute. A wave. The kind you give to people on a train platform when you're staying behind.

None of the dead in the river waved back. None of them saw.

"We'll come back for them," said Yuanshu.

Old Seven did not answer. But his gray flame flickered once, the way it did when he agreed.

---

They found the tracks on the ninth day.

Yan Shuang found them, kneeling on a plate of black glass at the bottom of a frozen ravine with her one hand flat on the surface. She had been quiet since the night under the Monarch. Quieter than Sokolova, which Yuanshu had not thought possible. She walked in front and read the glass and said nothing unless it was about the way.

Now she said, "Here."

Two long parallel scratches in the glass. Faint. White. Running north up the floor of the ravine, a little over two meters apart.

"Treads," she said. "Heavy vehicle. Six wheels. Snow chains." She ran her fingertips along one of the scratches. "Old company model. Northern Gate Remains Recovery used them. I drove one for eleven years." She looked up. "This one went through here about a month ago. Maybe a little more."

"Forty days," said Su Mingli.

"About that."

> **Mercer.**
>
> **And Gu.**
>
> **They came this way. In a company truck. On the company's route.**
>
> **They know where they are going.**

---

They followed the tracks north for two days.

They led up out of the ravine, across a high windswept plain of glass and gravel, and down into a long, sheltered hollow between two ridges, where the wind dropped and the air went still and very cold.

At the bottom of the hollow, beside a frozen spring, there was a camp.

Three tents. Patched, faded, Wastes-gray. A firepit ringed with stones, long cold. A sled piled with salvage: beast bones, scrap metal, a rusted rifle, a coil of copper wire. Three sets of skis propped against a rock.

And three people, sitting on the ground beside the firepit, facing north.

---

They were scavengers. Two men and a woman, in layers of fur and canvas, with goggles pushed up on their foreheads. They sat cross-legged in a row with their hands in their laps and their backs straight and their eyes open. Breathing. Slowly. All at the same rhythm.

Frost had formed on their shoulders. On their eyelashes. On the fur of their hoods. A thin white crust, unbroken, as if they had not moved in a very long time.

Bai Lu made a small sound and started forward. Lingyue caught her arm.

"Wait," said Yuanshu.

He walked to them alone, slowly, with Old Seven behind him. He knelt in front of the woman in the middle. She was perhaps thirty. There was a burn scar on her chin. Her eyes were brown and empty and pointed straight past him at the northern ridge.

> **Hollow.**
>
> **All three. Like the fourteen. Like Belov.**
>
> **And there is a thread. From each of them. Going up.**
>
> **Not to the lens. Not to the sky.**
>
> **North. Low. Along the ground. Toward something not very far away.**

On the side of the nearest tent, someone had painted a mark in black. Fresh. Not weathered at all.

A ring of tiny screaming mouths.

---

"Mercer," said Su Mingli, very quietly, behind him.

"He did this on his way north." Yan Shuang's voice was flat. "Like a man picking an apple off a tree as he walks past." She looked at the three frozen figures for a long time. "We used to say the Wastes take people. They don't. Something walks through them and picks."

Then Qin Hao, who had gone around the back of the tents, called out.

He came back holding something in both hands. A thermos. Steel, dented, with a cork stopper. He held it out without a word.

Taped to the side of it, folded small, was a piece of paper.

Yuanshu unfolded it.

The handwriting was neat and small and slightly old-fashioned, the handwriting of a man who had written a great many lab reports and a great many birthday cards.

*Yuanshu.*

*They were already like this when we arrived. I'm sorry. I couldn't do anything.*

*There's tea in the flask. Jasmine. It's still warm. I asked him to stop for an hour so I could boil water and sit with them.*

*Please don't believe me.*

*— G.*

He unscrewed the cork.

Steam came out. Faint. Smelling of jasmine.

Still warm. After forty days in the Wastes. In a camp at minus thirty.

---

Nobody spoke.

Yuanshu stood in the frozen hollow with the warm flask in one hand and the note in the other, and held the quiet, and it was harder than it had been under the Monarch.

> **It should not be warm.**
>
> **Not after forty days.**
>
> **Unless he did not leave forty days ago.**
>
> **Unless he left yesterday.**

"They're waiting," said Lingyue.

"Yes."

"He wanted us to know they're close." She looked north, at the ridge. "He wanted us to know they're waiting for us."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Yuanshu looked down at the note.

*Please don't believe me.*

"Because they can't open the grave," he said slowly. "They've been there forty days, and they can't open it." He folded the note and put it in his coat, next to the notebook. "They need someone who can ask."

---

They did not leave the three scavengers in the hollow.

Six soldiers of Third Battalion stepped forward when Yuanshu asked who would carry them. He did not order it. He asked. Six said yes. Two of them were the ones with no arms, and stepped forward anyway, and had to be gently turned back by Sergeant Huo.

So four skeletons in rags of gray greatcoats lifted the three scavengers, carefully, frost and all, onto their own salvage sled, and wrapped them in the canvas from the tents, and took up the sled's ropes over their bony shoulders. And turned south.

Back toward the wall. Toward Lingzhou. Toward a teahouse with paper screens over the windows where fourteen candles burned beside fourteen beds, and there was room for three more.

"Two hundred and eighty kilometers," said Yan Shuang. "On foot. Over the glass. With a sled."

"They don't get tired," said Yuanshu.

"The Monarch's between here and the wall."

"It can't see them. And the people on the sled can't think anything for it to hear." He looked at the four soldiers standing in the traces. "Sister Ning will be on the wall. Tell her they're coming. Tell her to watch for four of the dead pulling a sled."

Sokolova was already unspooling the antenna.

The four soldiers leaned into the ropes. The sled scraped on the glass. And slowly, steadily, the small strange procession went away south up the side of the hollow and over the rim and out of sight. Four dead men, carrying three living strangers home.

---

At dusk on the eleventh day, they came over the last ridge.

The country below them was a high, wide, bare plateau of black glass and frost, ringed with low hills, utterly flat and utterly empty under a sky going violet with the evening. In the middle of it, perhaps two kilometers away, a single low rise of dark rock humped up out of the glass like the back of a whale.

On the top of the rise stood a white stone.

Small, from here. Pale. Upright. A survey marker from some forgotten mapping expedition, a hundred years old, a squared pillar of white stone the height of a man, standing alone on the rise like a candle on a table.

Grid 47-121.

Beside it, very small, a light was burning. Yellow. Warm. A campfire.

And beside the fire, two figures. One standing, tall, in a long dark coat. One sitting, smaller, with something in his hands that caught the firelight and steamed faintly in the cold.

Neither of them was looking at the white stone.

Both of them were looking up at the ridge.

At the expedition. As if they had been sitting there for forty days, watching that exact point on the horizon, waiting for someone to come over it.

The tall one raised his hand in a slow, friendly wave.

Even from two kilometers away, in the last violet light, Yuanshu could see his teeth.
