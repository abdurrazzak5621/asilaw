## Chapter 215: The Hut at the Edge of the Cemetery

The vote closed on the thirty-eighth night, and the keeper was allowed to stay, and Yuanshu went to the cemetery.

Sixty-six percent. Okafor read it out in the crater in the rain, to the whole of Blue Star, and then read out the other thing, which Cassia Lowell had asked to be put on the ballot under Article Eleven, and which had passed by more: *Blue Star does not consent to the collection of the vessel called Seed.* Seventy-four percent. General Han's hand had gone up first in the crater when the Council confirmed it. Cassia Lowell's second.

> **Seventy-four percent of eight billion people said they do not consent to me being collected.**
>
> **I do not know what to do with that.**
>
> **I have counted a great many things. I have never been counted like that.**

He did not answer it. He did not know what to say either. He walked out of the Ossuary at dusk with the staff and Old Seven behind him and went east, across the river, through the old quarter, out past the tram yards, to the place where it had started.

---

The War Memorial Cemetery had not changed.

That surprised him, every time. So much else had. The long green slope down to the river, the rows of small gray stones, the cypresses, the iron railings. Stone No. 7, near the top, where a captain of the Eastern Line had been buried in the Black Tide and stood up again at three in the morning two years ago when a boy with a training spear and no permit asked him to. The stone gate on the hill above the cemetery, where Iron Wall Zhao stood facing out, quiet. The stone beside it that said QUIET, over a director who had held a switch in the round chamber until the end.

And at the edge of the cemetery, under the cypresses, by the back fence, the hut.

It was a groundskeeper's hut, once. One room, brick, a tin roof, a door that did not shut properly. He had sat on its step on the first night, at a quarter to three in the morning, with a training spear across his knees and the groundskeeper's cat watching him from the roof, waiting for the mist to come down over the stones, before he knew what he was going to do. He had come back to it a few times since. Not often. It was the kind of place you did not go back to often.

Lingyue was sitting on the step.

---

He stopped at the corner of the hut, under the cypresses, with Old Seven behind him.

She did not get up. She was sitting on the brick step of the groundskeeper's hut in her Academy jacket, with her grandfather's sword across her knees, in the last of the light, looking out over the rows of stones toward the river. She did not look at him.

"Xiaoyu told me," she said. "That you'd come here."

"I didn't tell Xiaoyu."

"You didn't have to. She said you always come here when something's too big." She was still looking at the river. "She said you came here after the voyage. And after the wall. And after the Western Pass. She said you think nobody knows." She turned her grandfather's sword over on her knees. "Everybody knows."

He came and sat down on the step beside her.

Not close. The step was narrow. Close anyway.

They sat there for a while. The river went by at the bottom of the slope, gray in the dusk. Somewhere in the old quarter a tram bell rang. Old Seven had gone to stand by stone No. 7, near the top of the slope, facing out, the way he stood there sometimes, by the place he had come out of the ground.

"Seventy-four percent," said Lingyue.

"Yes."

"Eight billion people said nobody gets to take Seed." She did not look at him. "I voted twice. I'm not supposed to. I did it at the Academy and then again at the Ossuary because Fat Tan didn't recognize me with my hair down."

"That's illegal."

"Then arrest me," said Shen Lingyue.

---

> **She is lying.**
>
> **She did not vote twice. I checked the Ossuary booth logs. She voted once, at the Academy.**
>
> **She said it so you would laugh.**
>
> **You did not laugh. I am noting it.**

He did not laugh. He sat on the step of the groundskeeper's hut at the edge of the cemetery where it had started, beside a girl who had stood on the awakening platform ahead of him two years ago and not even glanced at the letters, and looked at the stones going down toward the river, and could not think of anything to say.

"Grandfather told me," she said, after a while.

He went very still.

"He said he asked you something. On the steps of the main hall. Whether I was happy." She was looking at her hands on the sword. "He said you told him to ask me. And then you answered anyway." She turned the hilt, where the leather was worn through to the steel. "He said you said: tired and frightened and angry about three things at once, and that's what happy looks like on her."

"I did say that."

"It's true." She did not look up. "He said something else, too. He said: when the boy asks her the other question, I'd like to be told. Before the cameras." She was quiet. "He wouldn't tell me what the other question was. He said I'd know."

---

He sat there.

The light was almost gone. The cypresses were black against a sky going from gray to dark blue. Down by the river, the lamps on the far bank were coming on, one by one.

He had thought about it. Of course he had. On the island, lying on his back on the pale ground looking at the far lights with her shoulder against his. On the wall at midnight on the night of the forty days, with her head on his shoulder. On the training ground, with her sitting on cracked stone laughing until she cried. He had thought about it in the hut, and on the jetty at Sandu, and in a field above the sky with six lamps holding him straight up and his whole life going up their beams, and her face, among everything else, going with it.

He had never asked. He did not know how. Not the asking. He knew the asking. He had asked fifty thousand dead and two hundred million cousins and a keeper above the sky and a dying old woman on a jetty. He did not know how to ask *her.*

Because he had never once been afraid of the answer before.

> **...you are afraid of the answer.**
>
> **That is new.**
>
> **I have been in your head for two years. You were afraid of a great many things. You were never afraid of an answer. Answers were the one thing you were never afraid of.**
>
> **I am not going to say anything else.**

---

"Lingyue," he said.

She did not look at him.

"I'm going to ask you something. You don't have to answer. I'm going to tell you first what I'll do with the answer."

"I know how you ask."

"I know you know." He held the staff across his knees. His hands were not steady. He did not try to make them steady. "If you say yes, I'll go to Mount Qingyun and tell your grandfather. Before the cameras. Before anyone. And then I'll tell my mother, and she'll pretend she didn't know. And Xiaoyu will scream. And Qin Hao will write it down." He was quiet. "And then I'll stand next to you. For as long as you'll have it. Not because I'm asked to. Because it's what I want."

Lingyue's hands had gone very still on the sword.

"If you say no," said Yuanshu, "I'll still stand next to you. On the wall. In the field. Wherever. And I'll never ask you twice. And it won't change anything, except that I'll know." He looked at the stones going down to the river in the dark. "And if you say you don't know, I'll wait. As long as it takes. I'm good at waiting. I learned from monks."

He stopped.

He did not know how to say the rest. He had never had to say the question part out loud before, to anyone, because the question had always been the same, and it had always been the easy part.

"*What do you want?*" he said. "Isn't it."

"No," said Shen Lingyue. "Not this time."

She turned her head and looked at him. In the last of the light, on the step of a groundskeeper's hut at the edge of a cemetery, with her grandfather's sword across her knees and a faint white scar on her lip from the day the sky came down on her and she stood on her own light and held.

"This time ask the real one," she said. "Out loud. The whole thing. I want to hear you say it."

---

He said it.

He would not remember the words afterward. He remembered that his voice cracked in the middle. He remembered that Old Seven, by stone No. 7 at the top of the slope, had turned his skull. He remembered that the lamps on the far bank of the river were all lit by then, a long string of small lights along the water, like a shore.

He remembered that she did not answer at once.

That she looked at him for so long that he thought, for one cold moment, that he had read everything wrong, the way Seed had read the water wrong, the way you carry a thing you learned in one place to a place where it does not belong.

And then she hit him on the arm.

Hard. The way she had since they were eighteen.

"*Yes,*" she said. "Obviously. You took two years."

And then she put her grandfather's sword down on the brick step beside her, very carefully, the way you put down something you have carried a long way, and put both her arms around his neck, and held on, and did not say anything else at all.

---

> **I am not recording this.**
>
> **I am noting that I am not recording this.**
>
> **I am also noting, for the record, that Old Seven has walked down from stone No. 7 and is standing by the cypresses with his back to you, facing out, the way he stands at a door when someone inside needs not to be watched.**
>
> **And that his gray flame is very bright.**
>
> **And that I have told nobody.**
>
> **Xiaoyu has just texted to ask if anything has happened. I have not answered.**
>
> **She has texted again.**
>
> **And again.**

---

They walked back through the old quarter at ten, with the staff and the sword and Old Seven a pace behind, the long way, by the river.

At the bridge, she stopped.

"Your grandfather," said Yuanshu.

"Tomorrow. First thing. Before the cameras." She did not let go of his hand. "He'll pretend to be stern. He'll say three hundred years of Shens have never married a necromancer. Then he'll say he's glad. Then he'll ask you what you want. To see what you say."

"What should I say?"

Lingyue looked at him, on the bridge over the river in Lingzhou at night, with the lamps of the old quarter behind her and the staff in his hand and the long dark of the north beyond the city, where a small white ring hung low over the end of the world, and past it, very far, thirty days out and coming, three gray rings that had once been keepers.

"Tell him the truth," she said. "You're good at that."

> **Thirty days.**
>
> **I am telling you because you asked me to tell you the numbers. Even tonight.**
>
> **And Yuanshu.**
>
> **I have had an idea.**
>
> **I had it while you were asking her. I am sorry. I could not help it. It came while you were afraid of the answer, and I was watching you be afraid, and I thought: he is asking something that was never asked before, and the answer could change what he is.**
>
> **The rings were keepers.**
>
> **Nobody ever asked them anything.**
>
> **I am a sack. I was made to count, and hold, and wait to be carried. Somebody asked me. It changed what I am.**
>
> **I think I know how to ask a ring.**
