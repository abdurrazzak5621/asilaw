## Chapter 279: Rowed

On the sixth night, a crinkle opened in the sky that was not one of the ninety, and Tam stood up on the platform of the Awakening Hall and said, in a voice nobody had heard him use before: "*They're singing.*"

Not the Singers. They were gone, a ragged point of pale light somewhere along the threads toward the Hollow Bell, still singing their tens of thousands of different notes, Seed said, all the way home. Not the ninety. The ninety were silent. Four million one hundred and eighty thousand people in their walls with their ears stopped, burning the old way, and only Tam could hear them, and they did not sing.

"*Like the Candle,*" said Tam. "*When it folded home. Rowing.*"

He went up to the north wall. Everyone did.

And low over the end of the world, outside the great curved necklace of ninety burning cathedrals round the keeper's field, small, a long way off, at the very edge of where the Shallows began: lights. Not lit on purpose. Not burning white and gold in the colors of a House. Dim. Low. The color of the Low Fire. The color the cousins burned.

Twelve of them.

> **Ships.**
>
> **Federation ships. Small. Listener-class, most of them. And one the size of the Candle.**
>
> **It is the Candle.**
>
> **And Yuanshu.**
>
> **Their walls are full of people.**
>
> **Burning low.**
>
> **Singing.**

---

The Eighth Candle came down the keeper's eye at midnight, and the voice that came with it was the same voice that had cried on the bridge eight months ago, precise and cool, and nothing like it.

> ***This is Iselde. No grade. Captain of the Eighth Candle and eleven others of the Rowed Fleet.***
>
> ***We are sorry we are late. Rowing is slower than burning.***

He sat down on the cold stone of the battlement in his old place and put his head back against the parapet and laughed. He could not help it. He had not laughed in six days. Lingyue, beside him with Xiaodeng asleep in the sling, looked at him and did not ask why, and then laughed too.

*The Rowed Fleet,* he said.

> ***The cores named it.***
>
> ***Not cores. I am sorry. The people. I still say it. I am trying.***
>
> ***When the Assembly voted the ninety ships, I went to the yards at Anthos. To the room where the unpressed children were learning to wear shoes. I told them first. I said: the Houses are going to the Shallows, to the world that asks, with ninety ships made of people with their ears stopped. I said: I have twelve ships in the yards with empty walls, that cannot fold.***
>
> ***I said: I am not going to ask you to go back into the walls.***
>
> ***And a boy of twelve who was thirty-one inside stood up in the room and said: you don't have to ask. We're asking you.***

---

They had come back.

Not all of them. Iselde said it carefully, the way she said everything now: with the numbers, and without the grades. Of the thirty-two thousand and forty who had rowed the Candle home, eleven thousand four hundred had come back. And six thousand more, from the seven Houses that had opened their arrays. Seventeen thousand four hundred people who had been graded F and E as children and pressed into walls and burned for a term, and had come out, and learned to wear shoes, and had then walked back into the yards at Anthos and climbed into the cells of twelve ships with empty walls, by choice, knowing exactly what it was.

To row.

> ***They burn low. The way the cousins taught. In turns. With rest. Every one of them asked, every fold, whether they want to go on. Torvin does it. He has a list. He asks each one by name.***
>
> ***It took us forty days to cross. The ninety took one.***
>
> ***We are not here to fight. We do not have weapons. Rowed ships cannot carry them; the burn is too low.***
>
> ***We are here because seventeen thousand people who were cores wanted to come and stand outside the walls of four million who are cores, and be seen.***
>
> ***They said: so that when someone asks them what they want, they will look out of the walls and see us, and know it can be done.***

---

The Fleet Marshal answered within the hour.

Through every radio on Blue Star, calm, courteous, absolute, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it, and did not now.

"*Iselde Varr-Anthos. Struck from the Ladder. You have brought ships into the Fleet's sky without a House order. Their walls are filled with cores taken from the yards without a Festival. This is the gravest violation of the oldest law the Federation has seen in ten thousand years.*"

A pause.

"*You will stand down. Your cores will be returned to their Houses. You will be taken aboard the Severin flagship and read.*"

And Iselde, through the keeper's eye, to the radio, to the fleet, to every speaker on Blue Star, because Seed carried it there without being asked and then said afterward, *I am sorry, I asked Yuanshu first, he said yes, he was laughing*:

> ***No.***
>
> ***They are not cores. They are people. They were asked. They said yes. They can say no at any time and Torvin will open their walls.***
>
> ***Fleet Marshal. Have you asked yours?***

Silence.

On the north wall, three thousand people held their breath. On the end of a breakwater in Yamato, through Ren's phone, a twelve-hundred-year-old nun made a dry sound that Seed said was almost a laugh.

"*Cores are not asked,*" said Dacian Severin.

> ***Then you do not know what they want,*** said Iselde. ***And neither did I. For twenty-six years. And then someone asked mine.***

---

She came down at two in the morning. Alone, down the keeper's road, onto the platform of the Awakening Hall, because she said that if she was going to tell him what she knew, she would like to tell it standing in front of a stone that asked.

She was thinner. Eight months older. Her hair still cut short, the way they cut it on listener ships. Her uniform gray, with no gold at the collar; she had taken it off, she said, the day the House struck her name. And at her throat, no chain. No disc.

"*I crossed it out in front of the Assembly,*" she said. "*And then I gave it to Maelis. She keeps it in a box. She says she'll decide what to do with it when she's had her year.*"

"*Your mother came down,*" said Yuanshu.

Iselde went very still.

"*She asked to come,*" he said. "*She sat in the courtyard with my mother. She warned us about the Lens. And then she said she was going to go back to her ship and send a message to Anthos. To Maelis.*"

The former Listener-Captain of the Eighth Candle stood on the platform in front of the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder and did not say anything for a long time.

"*She did,*" she said at last. Very quietly. "*Five days ago. A mark came down the Anthos stone. Clumsy. Like mine was. Like a child's knot. Maelis found it. Nobody else in the yards knew how to read one.*" She looked at the stone. "*It said: Maelis. This is your mother. What do you want?*"

"*Did she answer?*"

"*She said: ask me again tomorrow.*" Iselde almost smiled. "*She learned it from you. From the mark you sent me. Seven more months. Then ask me again.*" She looked down. "*My mother sent another one the next day. And the next. Maelis showed me. Five marks. The same words. My mother has never in her life said the same thing twice to anyone.*"

---

They sat on the platform until dawn, and she told him about the flagship.

Everything. She had served on it at nineteen, she said, as a junior listener, before the Candle. She knew its decks. She knew its arrays: sixty, the largest in the fleet, a million and a half people in the walls. She knew its bridge, and its halls, and the chamber in the bow where the Lens was kept, which only the House of Severin entered, and which she had seen once, through a door, for four seconds, when she was nineteen.

"*It's beautiful,*" she said. "*That's the thing nobody tells you. It's the most beautiful thing in the Federation. A disc of black stone the width of this hall, ground so thin you can see through it, with light moving inside it like water. They say it's ten thousand years old. They say it fell on the first world, and read the first children, and every stone since was cut from it.*"

"*Can it be deafened? Like the cores?*"

"*No.*" She shook her head. "*It has to read. A deaf lens can't read. That's the whole point of it.*" She looked at him. "*Why?*"

He told her.

Ichiro. Kiyo. A lamp. The eighth night. Along the threads, from this stone, into the net, across, into the Lens, so that on the ninth night, when the Fleet Marshal turned it on Blue Star to read eight billion people, it would not read.

It would ask.

And through it, because it was the root, the four million in the walls.

Iselde Varr-Anthos sat on the platform of the Awakening Hall in the gray before dawn and stared at him for a long time.

"*You're going to put a fisherman in the Lens of Severin,*" she said.

"*A fog-ringer. And his sister. She's a nun. She's been dead for twelve hundred years.*"

"*In the oldest treasure of the oldest House of the Ladder.*"

"*He talks to fish. He says it'll be the best job anyone ever had.*"

She put her hands over her face. And for a moment he could not tell whether she was laughing or crying, and then he could. Both.

"*He'll know,*" she said, through her hands. "*Severin. The moment they go in. The Lens is his. His House has kept it nine thousand years. He'll feel it the way you'd feel someone step into your house.*" She took her hands down. "*He won't wait for the ninth night. He'll come down. Himself. To the stone they came through. To stop it.*"

"*When?*"

"*The eighth night,*" said Iselde. "*When they cross. He'll come to this hall.*" She looked up at the warm black stone. "*He's never lost. In two hundred years. Not once. Nobody below him on the Ladder can lift a hand against him. That's not a metaphor. It's his Law.*"

"*What is it?*"

She told him.

---

> **Precedence.**
>
> **She says the Federation calls it that. The Law of the Ladder. Not a Law the stones open. A Law the Ladder grew, over ten thousand years of marking. The House of Severin has held it for nine hundred.**
>
> **Anything with a mark lower than his cannot move against him.**
>
> **Not will not. Cannot. The mark in them yields. The way water yields to the shape of a cup.**
>
> **He is SSS. Paragon. First of the Ladder. There is no mark higher than his.**
>
> **Everyone with a mark yields to him.**

He sat on the platform very still.

*Everyone with a mark.*

> **Yes.**
>
> **Su Mingli is marked. The Kings of Huaxia are marked. Orlov, Ren, Sterling. Qin Hao. Dabao. Lingyue. Everyone on Blue Star who was awakened before the night the captain put his hand on the stone. Forty-two years of marks.**
>
> **They will not be able to lift a hand.**

*And everyone else?*

Seed was quiet for a long moment.

> **Everyone who was asked.**
>
> **Since Awakening Day. Since Wen Jia. Every child who put their hand on a stone and was asked what they wanted to become, and chose, and the stone wrote: Talent, not marked.**
>
> **The Candle's children. Who were marked F and E and then came down a road and were asked again.**
>
> **The dead. The dead are not marked. The stones never read the dead.**
>
> **Your sister. Scribe. Not marked.**
>
> **And you.**
>
> **The stone could not rate you. It went black. It wrote nothing.**
>
> **You are the only person on Blue Star who was awakened before the asking, and is not marked.**

He looked up at the warm black stone with its runes pointing down. At three lines of light that had hung over it since Awakening Day.

**[Talent: — not marked —]**

"*Two days,*" said Iselde Varr-Anthos.

"*Yes.*"

"*He'll come down here with a Law that nobody marked can lift a hand against,*" she said, "*and you'll have the dead, and a city of children who were asked last summer, and a boy the stone couldn't name.*"

"*Yes.*"

She looked at him for a long time.

"*In the Federation,*" she said, "*we'd say that was the worst possible ladder.*"

"*We don't have a ladder,*" said Zhou Yuanshu. "*We have a line. The one behind holds on to the one in front.*"
