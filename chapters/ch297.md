## Chapter 297: Old

The children came at seven.

He had not known about the children. Nobody had told him, because nobody in Lingzhou thought it needed telling any more; it was simply what happened now, the way the night watch changed at six and Dabao's mother's cart opened at half past. Seed told him on the steps of the Awakening Hall, in the gray before the sun, while he stood with the staff in his hand and Lingyue beside him with Xiaodeng asleep in the sling, and watched them come up East Ring Road.

> **They started in the summer. After Awakening Day. While we were on Anthos.**
>
> **A boy of nine. On his way to school. He came in and put his hand on the stone and told her he wanted to be a train driver. She said: okay. He came back the next morning and said he had changed his mind and wanted to be a vet. She said: okay. That's allowed.**
>
> **He told his class.**

They came up East Ring Road in ones and twos and small loud knots, with school bags, with their parents or without them, with half a steamed bun in one hand. Six years old. Ten. Fourteen. Not to be read. Nobody was going to be read until their own Awakening Day, years off, and some of them would not want to be then. They came because there was someone in the stone who asked, and they wanted to tell her.

They went up the aisle of the Awakening Hall past the lamp in a dead nun's lap and up the three steps of the platform and put their hands on the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder, one at a time, and the letters came in the air above the stone, in a girl's hand, quick and slightly slanted, the hand of someone who had been seventeen in the year two of the Black Tide and had never once been asked.

**[What do you want to become?]**

*A dancer.* **[Okay.]**

*A doctor like my aunt.* **[Okay.]**

*I don't know.* **[That's the best one. Come back tomorrow.]**

*A dragon.* **[Okay. I'll think about it.]**

> **She has been doing it every morning for sixty-one days.**
>
> **She told Xiaoyu it is the best part of being a rock.**

---

At a quarter to eight the bus came.

Number Nine. Old. Blue and white, with a dent in the back where a beast had hit it in the year of the Black Tide that nobody had ever fixed. It came round the corner of East Ring Road on its first run of the day and pulled in at the stop outside the Awakening Hall and stopped, with a hiss, and did not open its doors.

Behind the wheel, a man of fifty-one with gray in his hair and steady hands sat and looked through the windscreen at the doors of the hall.

He counted. Seed said so. He counted to ten, the way he had counted to ten outside the Awakening Hall twelve times a day since the morning his sister went into the stone.

Then he opened the doors, and got out, and left the engine running, which you were not allowed to do, and came up the steps.

"*She told me,*" said Wen Hao. He stopped on the step below Yuanshu and looked at him, and then at Lingyue, and at the baby, and then at the doors of the hall. "*Yesterday. On the last run. Through Seed. Through your sister, I mean. She said: tomorrow he's going to be asked. I want you to be there.*" He looked at his hands. Steady. "*Twenty-six years I've driven this route. I've never been late. I'm going to be late this morning.*" He almost smiled. "*She said that was allowed.*"

---

His mother came in her wheelchair, pushed by Fat Tan. Xiaoyu came with the marker. Qin Hao came, and stood at the back by the doors with his notebook shut. Dabao came, and Su Mingli, and Gao Min with her ledger, and Bai Lu, and Tam and Seri, barefoot on the cold floor, holding hands. Old Seven came, one-armed, and stood in the side door of the Awakening Hall, facing in.

Nobody had asked them to. Nobody had told them. Seed said Wen Jia had told the children, and the children had told everyone.

He did not mind.

He stood at the foot of the platform and waited while the last of the morning children went up, a girl of six who wanted to be *a bigger girl*, **[Okay. That one's easy. Eat your breakfast.]**, and came down, and ran up the aisle to her grandmother at the door.

Then the hall was quiet.

> ***...okay,*** said Wen Jia. ***...your turn.***

---

He went up the three steps.

He had not touched it since he was eighteen. Except once, at a run, when she was screaming. He had stood on this platform on Awakening Day three years ago with four thousand people watching and put his hand on this stone, and it had gone black. The whole hall. Eleven halls in eleven cities. The light gone out of everything, and four thousand people holding their breath, and his hand on the stone, and the stone trying to read what was in him and not being able to, and going dark, because there was nothing on the tree inside it that had a name for what he was.

Nobody had asked him. The stone had tried to tell him, and failed, and then the Federation and the Institute and the War Zones and the world had spent three years telling him. Calamity Necromancer. Unique. A danger. A weapon. The boy with the dead. *The one who asks.* He had been called a great many things. He had never once been asked.

He stopped in front of the stone.

It was warm. Its runes pointed down. A bright blue jacket hung over its shoulder, unworn, the one a bus driver had brought on the day. And at its foot, cross-legged, with one dry hand flat on the stone and a lamp burning in her lap, the body of a nun who had sat on a shore for twelve hundred years.

> **It is listening.**
>
> **The Collector. I can feel it. It is very close. Closer than I can count. It is not doing anything. It is only listening.**
>
> **The way the cousins in the walls listened to Tam sing. Before they knew they could answer.**

He put his hand on the stone.

**[What do you want to become?]**

---

He did not answer at once.

Wen Jia did not hurry him. The letters hung in the air above the stone in a seventeen-year-old's slanted hand, and the hall waited, and outside, on East Ring Road, a bus sat at a stop with its engine running and its doors open, and nobody got on.

He thought: *a King.*

He thought it the way you pick up a coin you see on the pavement, to see what it is, and put it down again. It was what the stone would have read, if it could have read him. It was what the Federation would have graded. A planet's worth. The Death Law past the cup. A Fragment. It would take a long time, and it would be his, and he found, standing there with his hand on a warm black stone, that he did not want it. Not against it. He just did not want it. It was a number.

He thought: *the one at the door.*

But he was that already. He had been that since a cemetery at three in the morning. He would go on being it whether he said so or not. You did not have to ask for the thing you already were. Maelis had not asked to be twenty-four.

He thought of Maelis. *Thirteen.* A girl who had been twelve for twelve years and wanted, more than anything in the Federation, to have one more birthday.

He thought of Kiyo, who had sat on a shore for twelve hundred years and waited, and then gone out in the boat. Of Ichiro, who would never stop talking. Of Yaw, who had asked nine hundred thousand of the lost in the cold at the edge of the cloud, and then wanted to lie at the bottom of the sea off Kesse and hear the boats go over.

He thought of Old Seven. Who had got up. Who was still saying it.

He thought of his mother. Who had sat in a chair for three years and read the names and let him go, every time, because he asked. Who was sitting at the foot of the platform now with her hands folded on the blanket, looking at the place a little above his eyes, where Seed was, where the thing she had built in a basement with a cold was, and not at him, because she knew him, and knew that if she looked at him he would not be able to say it.

And he thought of his daughter. Asleep in a sling against her mother's chest at the foot of the steps. Four and a half months. Who could not say yes yet, and was asked every morning anyway, and would one day, in a year or two, look up at him with eyes that had stopped being cloudy, and say something. And then, in ten years, something else. And then in thirty years, something he could not possibly imagine, in a room he had never been in, and he would not understand it, and he would have to ask her what she meant.

He wanted to be there for that.

He wanted to be there for all of it.

He found, standing in the Awakening Hall with his hand on the stone that had gone black, that that was the whole of it. That it had always been the whole of it. That the thing he wanted was so ordinary that he had walked past it every day for three years without seeing it, the way you walk past your own front door.

"*Old,*" said Zhou Yuanshu.

---

The hall was very quiet.

"*I want to be old,*" he said. To the stone. To the girl in it. He did not look round. "*I want to get old. Here. In Lingzhou. With her.*" He did not have to say who. "*I want to be as old as her grandfather, and sit on the north wall in the morning and not know what to say when my daughter asks me what I want. I want her to have to ask me twice.*"

His voice did not do anything strange. He had thought it might.

"*I want to stand at the door for a long time,*" he said. "*Not because I have to. Because I like it there. I want to open it for people when they ask, for fifty years, sixty, until I'm slow at it, until I'm the slowest one at the door, and then I want somebody to ask me if I want to go through. And I want to say: not yet. Ask me tomorrow.*"

He stopped.

"*And then one day I want to say yes,*" he said. "*When I'm old. And go. And have it be all right.*"

He took a breath.

"*That's what I want to become,*" he said. "*Old. I didn't know until you asked.*"

---

The stone did not answer for a while.

He felt her, the girl in it. Not her words. Her. The way he had felt her in the basement layer of Seed for three years without knowing she was there, one small warm yes among four thousand, *only if it's something good*. A girl who had been seventeen in the evacuation of the Wastes, in the second year, when the Monarch came, and had been taken in a sack, and kept, and never awakened, and never asked, and had gone into a stone so that nobody in Lingzhou would ever have to stand where she had not been allowed to stand.

She had never been old. She had never been eighteen.

> ***...me too,*** said Wen Jia.

Very small.

> ***...that's what I wanted. I never said. Nobody asked.***
>
> ***...I wanted to be old and annoying and tell my brother he drives too slow. I wanted to be fifty-one with him. I wanted to see what he looked like with gray in his hair.***
>
> ***...he looks good. I can see him. He's at the back. He's crying. Don't tell him I said he looks good.***

At the back of the Awakening Hall, by the doors, a bus driver of fifty-one with gray in his hair stood with both hands over his face.

> ***...okay,*** said Wen Jia. ***...okay. I'm going to write it now.***

**[Then get old.]**

The letters came slowly. Not the quick slanted hand of the morning children. Slower. Careful. The way you write something you want to be able to read again in fifty years.

**[That one takes the longest.]**

**[It's the only one I can't open for you. Nobody can. You just have to do it. Every day. On purpose.]**

**[I'll ask you every year how it's going.]**

**[Chosen.]**

And then, under it, smaller, in a hand that had written it once before, for his sister, on the Awakening Day a year ago:

**[Only if it's something good.]**

**[It is.]**

---

He took his hand off the stone.

The letters stayed. Hanging in the air above the platform of the Awakening Hall of Lingzhou beside his sister's three lines, in a different hand. **[Then get old.] [Chosen.]**

He turned round.

His mother had not moved. She was looking at him now. Not at the place above his eyes. At him. Her face was doing something he had never seen it do, not in three years, not in the basement nine years ago, not ever. It took him a moment to understand what it was.

She was not afraid.

For the first time since he was eighteen, since the Stele went black, his mother was looking at him and was not afraid of what he was going to become.

"*Good,*" said Zhou Wanqing. "*About time somebody in this family wanted something sensible.*"

Lingyue was at the foot of the steps with the baby. She did not say anything. She did not have to. She had heard the part about her grandfather and the north wall, and her face had done the thing it did when she was about to fight something, and then it had stopped doing that, and done something else, and she came up the three steps of the platform and stood beside him and put her free hand flat on the stone beside where his had been.

**[What do you want to become?]**

"*Old with him,*" said Shen Lingyue. "*Obviously.*"

**[Obviously.]** wrote Wen Jia. **[Chosen.]** And then, smaller: **[I knew you'd do that.]**

---

At the side door, one-armed, a tall dead captain stood facing in.

He did not come up the steps. Seed said he would not. Seed said he said the stone was for the living, and he had been asked already, at three in the morning, by the only one he needed.

> **He says: old.**
>
> **He says: he never got to be. Not many on the eastern wall did.**
>
> **He says: it is a good thing to want. It is the thing they were all holding the wall for. They just never said it.**
>
> **He says: he will stand at the door with you. For as long as it takes. He is very patient. He has been dead more than thirty years. He can do fifty more.**

---

And far away, and not far at all, closer than Seed could count, something warm and patient and very old that had been listening the whole time, through the stone, through a lamp in a dead nun's lap, through a girl on the bottom step of the platform with a marker in her hand, said one word.

His sister heard it. She looked up.

> *Old.*

Then nothing.

> *I am old,* said the Collector. *I did not choose it. I did not know you could.*
>
> *I said, on the mountain, that he might be old before I arrived.*
>
> *I was wrong. I am going to arrive first.*
>
> *I am sorry, child. I would have liked to come later. When he was old. I would have liked to see what that looks like on one of you.*
>
> *But the want in the empty place in me has started to pull. Since the picker stopped. Since the house was told it was empty. It pulls the way a thing pulls when it knows it is nearly home.*
>
> *I am here tomorrow.*
>
> *Will you ask him then? The thing I asked you to ask.*

Zhou Xiaoyu looked at her brother.

He was standing on the platform of the Awakening Hall, under two sets of letters, with his wife beside him and his daughter between them and his hand still warm from the stone.

"*Yes,*" said his sister. To the dark. "*I'll ask him.*"

She uncapped the marker. She went to the white wall beside the side door of the Awakening Hall, where nobody had written anything yet, past a tall dead captain who moved out of her way, and wrote, at the very top, in square black capitals:

*ZHOU YUANSHU. ASKED. OLD.*

And under it, smaller:

*THE COLLECTOR. TOMORROW. FOR REAL THIS TIME.*
