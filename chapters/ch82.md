## Chapter 82: The Trial of Gu Hanshan

They held the trial in the old Lingzhou Exhibition Hall, because no courtroom in the city was big enough.

It was a vast echoing barn of a building by the river, built eighty years ago for trade fairs, with a curved roof of iron and glass and a floor of scuffed gray concrete. The Bureau had filled it with folding chairs. Four thousand of them. In rows. Facing a long plain wooden table at the far end, under the great curved window, where three judges sat in black.

Every chair was taken by seven in the morning. People stood in the aisles. They stood along the walls. They stood outside in the cold on the riverbank, in their thousands, watching it on screens propped on car roofs.

Not to shout. Nobody shouted.

They had come because almost every one of them had someone who had been hollow.

---

Gu Hanshan sat alone at a small table to the right of the judges, in a plain gray prison jacket, with his hands folded on the wood.

He had no reading glasses on a cord. They had taken those. He had no cardigan. His gray hair had been cut very short. He looked older than he had in the Wastes, and thinner, and very small in the enormous hall.

But his face was quiet. Not calm. Not kind. Just quiet. The way it had been on the frozen plateau, the morning after the thread was cut. Like a house with all the windows open.

His lawyer sat beside him. A young woman from the public defender's office, who had argued for three weeks that he should plead not guilty by reason of coercion. That the thread had been in him since he was nine. That nothing he had done since could be called his own.

Gu had listened to her very politely, every day, for three weeks.

Then he had stood up at the arraignment and pleaded guilty to everything.

---

"Every charge," said the presiding judge, a heavy gray-haired man named Lin Shoucheng, looking at the papers in front of him. "The trafficking of persons, two hundred and nine counts. The sale and purchase of human remains in violation of the Charter, eleven counts. Obstruction of a Bureau investigation. Falsifying a report from the Western Pass." He looked up. "Concealment of a foreign... presence. In your own person. For fifty years."

"Yes," said Gu.

"Your counsel has argued, at length and with some force, that you were not responsible for many of these acts. That you have no memory of them. That they were done by something using your hands."

"Yes."

"And you reject that argument."

"I don't reject it. I'm sure it's true." Gu looked at his folded hands. "I just don't want to hide behind it." He was quiet for a moment. "If I say *it wasn't me,* then two hundred and nine people were sold out of the back of a truck, and nobody did it. Nobody signed for them. Nobody stood at the loading dock." He looked up at the judge. "My initial is on the receipts. I'd rather it was me than nobody. Nobody can't be sorry."

The hall was very quiet.

---

They heard the witnesses.

It took four days. Lu Zhengtian, for the Bureau, in a suit he had obviously slept in, laying out the trail of receipts and gold bars and loading docks with his voice perfectly flat, never once looking at the man at the small table. Doctor Iris Kwan, for the Institute, explaining what a thread was, and a hollow, and a core. Fat Tan, with his ledger. Yan Shuang, standing at the witness stand with her one hand on the rail, saying *I drove the trucks* in a voice like gravel, and then, after a pause, *so did he. He just never knew which truck.*

General Qin Zhenyue, eighty-one, one arm, who laid a small recorder on the table and pressed play. And a dead colonel's voice, rough and tired and amused, filled the vast hall from the speakers. *A very kind man came out from Lingzhou to look at them. Gray cardigan. Brought tea for everyone. He said it was altitude sickness.*

Gu listened to that with his eyes closed.

When it ended, he opened them, and looked across the hall at the old general, and said, very quietly, "I'm sorry, General. I don't remember. But I'm sorry."

General Qin looked at him for a long time. Then he picked up the recorder and put it back in his pocket and walked out of the hall without a word.

---

On the fifth morning, the judges went to sentencing.

Under the old law, it was simple. Lin Shoucheng read it out in his heavy gray voice. Two hundred and nine counts of trafficking. The minimum was life.

The hall did not move.

"But this court," said Judge Lin, setting down the paper, "now sits under the Concordia Consent Charter. And Article Nine of the Charter, which this nation signed a hundred and forty days ago, says something that no court in Huaxia has ever had to consider before." He picked up a second sheet. Thin. Four pages. "It says: *In any proceeding concerning harm done to a person's mind, body or remains, the persons harmed shall be asked what they want.*"

He looked up at the four thousand people in the folding chairs.

"So we asked."

---

It had taken the Ossuary three weeks.

Two hundred and nine people Gu had signed for. Hollows sold out of the back of a truck, carried north to a bowl at the bottom of the world. Most of them had come home in the gold rain. They were in the hall now. Sitting together, in the first six rows, in coats and hats and wheelchairs. Some of them still had frost scars on their cheeks. Some of them still turned their heads north, sometimes, three degrees, and had to be talked back.

And some had not come home.

Thirty-one of them. Their bodies had been in the bowl, breathing. Their minds had been in the field. And when the keeper had asked them what they wanted, they had said *quiet.*

Their bodies were in the Listening Pavilion now. In clean white beds. Still breathing, slowly, all at the same rhythm. Empty. All the way down. Nothing at the bottom of the well. Not waving. Not anything.

The Quiet sat with them. Day and night. In shifts. Until their bodies, gently, in their own time, stopped.

Their families had been asked too.

---

"One hundred and seventy-eight of the persons harmed," said Judge Lin, reading, "and the families of the thirty-one who chose quiet, gave answers to this court. Through the Ossuary. Witnessed." He turned the page. "I will not read them all. I will tell you what most of them said."

He set the paper down.

"Most of them said they did not want him in a cell."

A sound went through the hall. Not loud. Like wind.

"Not because they forgive him," said the judge. "Some do. Most don't. Some said they never will. Many of them said things I will not read aloud." He paused. "But one hundred and forty-one of them, independently, said some version of the same thing." He picked up one sheet and read from it, flatly. "*He signed for us. He should have to sit with us. He should have to sit with the ones who didn't come back.*"

He put it down.

"Gu Hanshan. This court sentences you to thirty years. To be served not in prison, but at the Listening Pavilion on Teahouse Lane, under the supervision of the Quiet and the Bureau. You will sit with the thirty-one. Every night. In silence. Until each of their bodies has passed." He paused. "And then you will sit with whoever comes next. For thirty years. Or until you die." He looked at the small man at the small table. "Do you understand the sentence?"

---

Gu Hanshan stood up.

He looked at the judge. Then he turned, slowly, and looked out over the hall. At the four thousand people in the folding chairs. At the first six rows, with their frost scars and their wheelchairs.

At the back of the hall, standing against the wall, a boy with a white staff in his hand. And beside him, a small thin woman in a gray coat, with her hair pinned up under a field cap, leaning on her daughter's arm.

Gu's eyes found Wanqing's across the hall. And stayed there.

She didn't nod. She didn't look away. She just looked at him, steadily, across four thousand people, the way you'd look at someone across a kitchen table at the end of a very long night.

Gu turned back to the judge.

"I understand," he said. "Thank you." He paused. "May I ask one thing?"

"You may ask."

"The lemon tree," said Gu Hanshan. "In Wanqing's room. May I visit it? On Thursdays?" A very small, very tired smile. "Somebody has to water it properly."

---

The hall emptied slowly.

People stood up from the folding chairs and went out into the cold gray afternoon on the riverbank, not talking much, in twos and threes. Some of them were crying. Some of them were angry, and said so, quietly, to each other. Some of them just looked tired. In the first six rows, people sat for a long time without getting up, holding each other's hands.

Sister Ning came down the aisle against the stream of people, slowly, with her candle. She stopped at the small table where Gu still sat with his hands folded, between two Bureau guards.

She looked at him for a long moment.

"You'll have to learn," she said. "To sit. In silence. With nothing in your head. For four hours at a time."

"I know."

"You're very bad at it. You think much too loudly. I heard you all through the trial."

"I know."

Sister Ning held out her candle. Unlit.

"Tonight," she said. "Seven o'clock. Teahouse Lane. Don't be late. The first shift is the hardest."

Gu Hanshan looked at the candle in her hand for a long time.

Then he took it.

---

Yuanshu walked his mother home along the river in the dusk.

She was slow. She had to stop and rest every hundred meters on the railings. Xiaoyu walked on her other side, holding her elbow, scowling at anyone who looked at them. Old Seven walked behind with his spear.

At the third stop, Wanqing leaned on the railing and looked out at the river, gray and wide, running down to the sea.

"Thirty years," she said.

"Do you think it's right?"

His mother was quiet for a long time.

"I think nobody's ever tried it before," she said at last. "I think that's what the Charter is. Nobody's ever tried it before." She looked at him. "I don't know if it's right. I know it was asked."
