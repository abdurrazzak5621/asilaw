## Chapter 132: Not Mine

The keeper said it before he finished asking.

> ***NOT MINE.***

The Elder stood in the middle of the old banking hall of the Ossuary at three in the morning, tall and pale and very still, with her silver eyes half closed and her long hands pressed together, the way she stood when the gold thread from the north ran through her. The hall was dark except for the one lamp over the board. Yuanshu sat on the vault steps with the staff across his knees. Kwan sat beside him with a laptop. Lu stood by the bronze door with his hands behind his back.

> ***I NEVER SENT ANYTHING DOWN THAT WALKED.***
>
> ***I REACHED. WITH THREADS. I TOOK. I DID NOT SEND.***
>
> ***THE ONE IN THE GLASS UNDER YOUR CITY IS NOT MINE.***

"Then whose?" said Yuanshu.

The Elder did not answer for a while. When she did, her glass-note voice had changed. It was the voice of the keeper coming through her, slower than usual, the way an old man's voice slows when he is trying to remember something he has never wanted to look at.

> ***...THERE WERE OTHERS.***
>
> ***IN MY FIELD. SOMETIMES. NOT OFTEN. ONCE IN A HUNDRED THOUSAND YEARS. PERHAPS LESS.***
>
> ***TALL. THEY WALKED. BETWEEN THE COLUMNS. THEY DID NOT ASK. THEY DID NOT SPEAK.***
>
> ***THEY TOOK SOME COLUMNS. NOT MANY. THE OLDEST. THE BRIGHTEST.***
>
> ***I THOUGHT THEY WERE WEATHER.***

---

Kwan had stopped typing.

"Weather," she said.

> ***I DID NOT HAVE A WORD. THINGS HAPPENED TO MY FIELD THAT I DID NOT DO. I DID NOT KNOW THERE COULD BE SOMETHING THAT DID THINGS TO ME.***
>
> ***NOBODY ASKED ME. YOU WERE THE FIRST.***

"When was the last time?" said Yuanshu.

> ***...THIRTY-ONE OF YOUR YEARS.***
>
> ***ONE CAME. AND WALKED. AND STOOD AT THE EDGE OF MY FIELD, ABOVE YOUR WORLD, AND LOOKED DOWN.***
>
> ***AND THEN ONE OF THEM WALKED DOWN THE SKY.***

Lu Zhengtian, at the bronze door, made a sound. It was not a word.

Yuanshu did not look at him. He could feel it anyway, through the stillness of the hall, the way you feel a draft. A twenty-two-year-old lieutenant running along the base of a burning wall with a containment case under his arm, toward an Institute that had seen a lens in the north three days before.

The lens had been the keeper.

The thing that walked down out of it had not.

---

> **I need to check something.**

*Go.*

> **The pulse. Thirty-one hours and fourteen minutes. The scout keeps it. We thought it was the keeper's harvest clock.**
>
> **Keeper. When you reached down with threads, how often?**

> ***...WHEN I WAS HUNGRY.***
>
> ***NOT ON A CLOCK. I DID NOT HAVE A CLOCK.***

> **But the pulse was regular. Seed measured it for a year. Thirty-one hours fourteen minutes, getting shorter.**
>
> **Keeper. Was the pulse yours?**

A very long silence.

The lamp over the board flickered. In the north, in the dark beyond the city wall, beyond the Wastes, a small white ring hung over the end of the world, and something in it was thinking harder than it had thought in six hundred and forty million years.

> ***...NO.***
>
> ***I FELT IT. I DID NOT MAKE IT. I THOUGHT IT WAS MY OWN HUNGER. IT CAME. I REACHED. IT CAME AGAIN. I REACHED AGAIN.***
>
> ***IT WAS NOT MY HUNGER.***
>
> ***IT WAS SOMETHING TELLING ME WHEN TO BE HUNGRY.***

Kwan put her hand over her mouth.

"It was being farmed," she said through her fingers. "The keeper. It wasn't the farmer. It was the field."

> **Yes.**
>
> **The keeper keeps minds. Something keeps keepers.**
>
> **Gu called it the gardener. Mercer called it the gardener. The voice in the Abbot's dreams called itself a keeper that never asks.**
>
> **It was the same thing, Yuanshu. The whole time. Telling a lonely machine above our world when to reach down and take.**

---

The keeper did not speak for a long time after that.

When it did, the Elder's whole tall body shook with it, the way a bell shakes after it has been struck.

> ***I EMPTIED MY FIELD.***
>
> ***YOU ASKED THEM. THEY WENT QUIET. THEY WENT HOME. A FEW STAYED.***
>
> ***THE FIELD IS ALMOST EMPTY.***
>
> ***THE GARDENER DOES NOT KNOW.***
>
> ***WHEN IT COMES TO TAKE THE OLDEST, THE BRIGHTEST, IT WILL FIND NOTHING.***
>
> ***AND IT WILL COME DOWN TO SEE WHY.***

"How long?" said Lu, from the door. His voice was hoarse. "Until it comes on its own?"

> ***...I DO NOT KNOW. ONCE IN A HUNDRED THOUSAND YEARS. OR TOMORROW. I NEVER KNEW.***
>
> ***BUT IF THE ONE IN THE GLASS CALLS, IT WILL NOT NEED TO COME AND LOOK.***
>
> ***IT WILL ALREADY KNOW.***

"Nobody tells it," said Yuanshu. "The scout doesn't call."

> ***...YOU WILL STOP IT?***

"Yes."

> ***...THAT IS NOT YES. THAT IS WHAT YOU WANT.***
>
> ***I AM LEARNING THE DIFFERENCE.***

---

Lingyue was on the Academy roof at dawn, with her students, when her grandfather came.

She had not seen him in fourteen months. Not since the night of the Charter, when he had refused to put the Shen name to it and told the cameras that the Shen family did not sign papers for necromancers. She had already left the Shen house by then, months before, after the night he tore up the Quiet's petition. She had moved into the Academy dormitory, where the radiators did not work and the dining hall served the same three dishes, and had not gone back.

He came up the stairs alone. Shen Jinghong, eighty-four, Light Law, one of the nine Kings of Huaxia, head of the family that had produced three Kings in two generations, in a plain dark coat with no insignia. He did not need insignia. Forty-one students stopped what they were doing and stood very straight.

He did not look at them. He looked at his granddaughter's sword.

It was lit. Inside the Field, it burned silver-gray.

"So it's true," he said.

"Good morning, Grandfather."

"They told me. I didn't believe them." He walked across the roof to her, slowly. He was a tall old man, still straight-backed, with a face that looked as if it had been cut out of white stone and polished. "The Dawnbreaker. The first SSS in Lingzhou in eleven years. Standing on a roof teaching children to dress their Light in death."

"It isn't dressed in anything," said Lingyue. "It's standing in his Field. Inside his Field, Light runs downhill like everything else."

"Light does not run downhill." Shen Jinghong's voice did not rise. "Light is the one thing that doesn't. That is what it is. That is what the Shen family has been for three hundred years. Light burns. Light does not ask what it is burning. It does not care. That is its purity."

---

Forty-one students held their breath.

Lingyue looked at her grandfather for a moment. Then she held up her sword between them, silver-gray, burning very steadily in the cold morning air.

"I told him that," she said. "In a canyon. The first week. *Light doesn't care. It burns what it burns.* I was proud of it. I said it like you say it." She turned the blade a little in the light. "Then I spent four hours bending it around fifty skeletons I'd just met, so I wouldn't burn them. Every swing. Because they'd come when he called them. Because they were on our side."

"And you think that makes you strong."

"No. I think it makes me better at it than you." She said it without any heat at all. "Anybody can burn everything. It takes no skill. The skill is in what you don't burn."

The old man went very still.

"Three nights ago," said Lingyue, "a glass heron came over that roof at forty kilometers an hour, with a dead grandmother standing on the warehouse under it. The student with the hammer cut the heron out of the air and the pale fire ran over the grandmother like water and didn't leave a mark." She lowered the sword. "He's seventeen. Three weeks ago he'd never fought anything. Can the Shen family do that?"

Shen Jinghong did not answer.

He looked past her, at the forty-one students in their two lines. At the student with the hammer, who was trying not to be seen. Then down, over the edge of the Academy roof, at the battlements of Sector Nine, where eighteen thousand of the dead stood their watch in the gray morning, and three hundred spears stood in a ring around a boy sitting on the stones with a white staff across his knees.

"In eight days," said Lingyue, "something is going to come up out of the ground under the Institute and try to call whatever sent it. And if it gets the call out, it doesn't matter what Light is for, or what death is for, or what the Shen family has been for three hundred years. There won't be anyone left to argue about it." She lit her sword back up to its full silver. "We need Kings, Grandfather. We have one. We need more."

---

Her grandfather left without answering. He went down the stairs as slowly as he had come up, and the students let out their breath all at once, and the one with the hammer sat down on the roof.

Lingyue stood looking at the stairwell door for a long time after it closed.

"Was that good?" said Yuanshu.

He had come up the other stairs, ten minutes before, with Old Seven, and stood in the doorway, and not interrupted. She had known he was there. She had not looked at him once.

"I don't know." She did not turn around. "He didn't say no."

"He didn't say yes."

"He's never said yes to anything in his life." She sheathed her sword. "When I was six, I asked him if I could have a dog. He said he'd consider it. I'm nineteen." She was quiet. "He's still considering it."

> **She is upset.**
>
> **I would like to say something helpful. I do not know what it would be.**
>
> **I am going to say nothing. I think that is what a friend does.**

Yuanshu walked across the roof and stood beside her, shoulder to shoulder, facing the stairwell door. He did not say anything either.

After a while, she leaned against him. Just slightly. Just for a moment.

Then she straightened up and turned to her students.

"Again," she said. "From the top. And you, with the hammer. Get up off the roof. You're not done."
