## Chapter 155: Akosua

The buses started at seven, and by nine the coast road was a single line of vehicles from Port Abiri to the hills, crawling, nose to tail, with their windows open and children's faces in every one.

Yuanshu walked through the middle of it on the way up to the graves.

The town was emptying the way a town empties when it has done this once before. Quickly. Without arguing. People who had stood on the beach at Kesse with lanterns a year ago, listening to their dead speak out of the air, did not need to be told twice that something was coming up out of the sea. They packed what they could carry and locked their doors and got on the buses. An old man sat on a suitcase outside a closed shop with a parrot in a cage on his knee, waiting for his grandson's truck. A woman handed a crying baby up through a bus window to someone she did not know and climbed in after it. Ifeoma's household went from door to door with lists. Hu Dabao carried a refrigerator down three flights of stairs for a family who refused to leave without it, because, he said afterward, it was faster than arguing.

The hill was the only place that was not moving.

---

She was sitting on the grass beside her husband's stone with her basket beside her and her hands in her lap.

The same black dress. The same hard, cracked fisherman's hands. A small brass key on the stone beside the shell, gone green at the edges from a year of salt air. A second stone beside it, smaller, newer: *KWAME MENSAH. 19.* Someone had put a shell on that one too.

She watched him come up the path between the white stones and did not get up.

"You're the boy," she said.

"Yes."

"You're going to tell me to get on a bus."

"No," said Yuanshu. "I'm going to ask you your name."

The fisherman's widow looked at him. Behind him, at the bottom of the hill, a bus horn went, twice, and a dog barked, and the long line of traffic on the coast road shuffled forward another ten meters.

"Nobody's asked me that," she said. "In a year. They ask me how I am. They ask me if I need anything. The Architect asked me if it could say it again." She looked at her hands. "Akosua. Akosua Mensah."

"Akosua." He sat down on the grass, a little way from her, facing the sea. "Something's coming up out of the bay tomorrow morning at dawn. The same kind of thing that came last year. We think it'll bite. We're going to try to stop it at the bottom, before it comes up. We don't know if we can."

"I know. The radio said."

"The town's being moved inland."

"I know." She did not look away from the sea. "And you want to know if I'm going to go. No."

He had known she would say that. He had known it walking up the hill.

"Why?"

Akosua Mensah turned her head and looked at the two stones.

"Because they're here," she said. "Kojo and my son. They're under this hill. If that thing comes up and bites again, it'll bite them first. I'm not going to sit on a bus on the hill road and watch it eat my husband twice." She folded her hands. "And because I'm waiting for something. You know what. You were there."

"The Architects."

"In five months. They said a year." She looked out at the bay. "I come up here every morning and I sit here and I wait for them. I'm not going to stop the day before something tries to stop me."

---

Article Ten, he thought.

He had stood in a crater on Concordia four days ago and heard it read. *Where one person's choice would bring harm upon others who have not chosen it, that choice may be refused.* He had said the sentence himself, first, on a screen in the Ossuary. *Your yes is yours. It stops at someone else's window.*

He looked down the hill. At the town emptying. At the line of buses on the coast road. By dawn tomorrow there would be nobody left in Port Abiri but the dead in the bay and the dead on this hill and an old woman sitting between them.

She was not in anyone's window. There was no one behind her.

> **Yuanshu. If you stay with that sentence, you cannot move her. She will probably die.**

*I know. It's the right rule.*

> **I know it is the right rule. I am allowed to not like it. I checked.**

"All right," said Yuanshu.

Akosua Mensah turned and looked at him.

"That's all?"

"That's all. It's your choice. You're not in anybody's way." He looked at the two stones. "I'd like you to get on a bus. I'd like it very much. But it's not mine to want. It's yours."

The widow did not answer at once. She picked a blade of grass and turned it in her cracked fingers.

"The Architect," she said finally, "said it again for an hour. I made it. And I still didn't believe it. I don't know if I ever will." She looked at him. "You're the first one who didn't try to talk me out of something."

"I'm going to try to stop it at the bottom," said Yuanshu. "So you can keep waiting."

---

There were three hundred and nine of them on the beach at noon.

He had known about the Hollow Gods of the Southern Compact. Everyone did. After the bite, after the night on the beach at Kesse with the lanterns, some of the families of the three thousand had gone looking for somewhere their grief could be held, and the Abbot's broadcasts had found them first. Three hundred and nine people from Port Abiri and Kesse and the villages between, men and women, old and young, who had joined a sect that believed being kept was heaven.

They had not got on the buses either.

They were sitting in long quiet rows on the sand at the edge of the bay in the heat of the day, facing the place where the water turned dark, in white clothes. Not praying. Waiting. And among them, in his orange coveralls, with his captain's cap on his knee, sat Hendrik Maas of the *Patience*, who had been put ashore by a boy the day before and told he could go anywhere he liked.

"They're going to walk into the sea," said Lingyue. "At dawn. When it comes up."

"Yes."

"And they're not in anybody's window either." She stood beside him at the top of the beach with her arms folded and both swords on her back. "The town's empty. Everyone else is on the hill road. If they want to drown themselves in the bay, nobody's behind them."

"No."

"So you can't move them." She did not make it a question.

He stood and watched them. Three hundred and nine people in white, on the sand, in rows, the way the sitters had sat on the pavement outside the Institute in Lingzhou. The way the four hundred had knelt in rings on the summit of the Western Pass.

> **Yuanshu. Do you want my numbers?**

*Yes.*

> **The trunks eat embers. Every ember they eat makes them grow faster and bite harder.**
>
> **A living person who walks into the bay at dawn becomes an ember in about four minutes.**
>
> **Three hundred and nine embers, fresh, warm, at the top of the trunk, at the moment it surfaces. Not the slow old cold ones from the bottom. New ones.**
>
> **My guess, and it is a guess: that is enough to make the Port Abiri spire grow its first bite twice as wide. Six kilometers instead of three.**
>
> **Six kilometers reaches the hill road. Where the buses are.**

---

He went down the beach and sat in the sand in front of them.

Not in front of the first row. In the middle. Between two rows, cross-legged, in the heat, the way he had sat on a pavement in Lingzhou, with the sea at his back. Three hundred and nine faces turned and looked at him, calm and kind and very tired, and he looked back.

"I'm going to ask each of you what you want," he said. "And then I'm going to tell you something. And then you can decide."

He asked them.

It took two hours. He did it the way he had done it on the deck of the *Patience*, one at a time, crouched in the sand in front of each of them, so their faces were level. Most of them said the same thing in different words. *To be kept. To go where they went. To not be the one who is left.* A man whose two daughters had been in the bite. A woman whose whole family had been at a funeral on the beach when it came down, all of them, and she had been in town buying ice. A boy of fifteen whose father had been on the same boat as Kwame Mensah.

When he had asked all of them, he stood up in the middle of the rows, with the sea at his back.

"Your choice is yours," he said. "I'll defend it to anyone. If you want to go into the sea, you can go into the sea." He was quiet for a moment. "But here's what happens if you do."

And he told them. Seed's numbers. The fresh embers at the top of the trunk. The bite twice as wide. Six kilometers. The hill road. Eleven thousand people from this town on buses, crawling, nose to tail, with their windows open.

"Your families," he said. "Some of them. The ones who didn't come with you. They're on that road right now."

Nobody on the beach said anything.

"I'm not going to move you," said Yuanshu. "Not yet. I'm telling you, in person, with the reason, so that you know. If you're still here at dawn, I'm going to move you then. Gently. And you can hate me for it afterward. But your yes stops at the hill road." He looked along the rows. "That's all. You've got until dawn to choose where you want to be when I do it."

---

He walked back up the beach.

Halfway up, someone fell into step beside him. Orange coveralls. A captain's cap in one hand.

"That was the same speech as the boat," said Hendrik Maas.

"Mostly."

"You're getting better at it." The old captain walked beside him in the sand for a while. "You know what they're going to do, don't you? Half of them will go. Up the hill road. Because of what you said about their families." He was quiet. "And half of them will wait for you to move them at dawn, so that it's your fault and not theirs. So that they can say they were taken."

"I know."

"That doesn't bother you?"

"It bothers me," said Yuanshu. "I'm going to do it anyway."

Hendrik Maas stopped at the top of the beach and looked back at the rows of white on the sand. Then up at the hill, where a small figure in a black dress sat beside two stones, with her basket.

"That woman," he said. "On the hill. She's not getting on a bus either."

"No."

"Are you going to move her?"

"No."

The captain looked at him.

"She's not in anyone's window," said Yuanshu. "She's not walking into the sea. She's sitting with her husband and her son, waiting for someone to say they're sorry. If it comes up and bites her, nobody else gets hurt." He did not look away from the hill. "That's hers."

Hendrik Maas did not say anything while a bus went by below them on the coast road, and then another. Then he put his cap on, and straightened it.

"Well," he said, in his flat northern voice. "I've driven a bus before." He looked down at the line of traffic crawling up the coast road toward the hills. "I'd like to be kept where my wife is kept. I still would. But I'm not going to get there by making some poor sod on the hill road die for it." He turned away. "Tell the Abbot I said so, if you ever see him."

He walked off down the street toward the bus depot.

---

That night, in the harbor master's office, with the dead ceiling fan overhead and the hand-drawn chart on the table, Seed ran the fight.

All of it. The three trunks. The drowned at the bottom, two thousand eight hundred and forty-six of them in the Field's reach and more beyond it in the long dark smear of sand that ran south toward Sandu, where nobody had been to ask them yet. The Spear Corps. Pale fire. The Banked Field. Mo Xing's folds, Mo Shi's three seconds. Lingyue. Ifeoma's water.

It ran it four thousand times.

Yuanshu watched the runs go past on the inside of his eyes, one after another, faster than he could follow, until they started to blur into a single shape. Then the shape settled.

> **I can hold two.**
>
> **Port Abiri and Kesse. If everything goes right, and some things go wrong, we can stop both trunks at the bottom before they surface. Seventy-one percent for Port Abiri. Sixty-four for Kesse.**
>
> **Sandu, no.**
>
> **Sandu is forty kilometers from here. It is at the end of the smear, where the sand is thickest and the drowned are not asked. By the time we have stopped Kesse, Sandu will have surfaced. Four hours, at the least.**
>
> **And Sandu still has about eleven thousand people in it. On the river road.**
>
> **I have run it four thousand times, Yuanshu. I have tried every order. Every split. Every way of dividing what we have.**
>
> **I cannot make three.**

He sat with that under the dead fan while the clock on the wall went round to midnight.

Then the door opened behind him, and he heard the soft rubber squeak of wheels on the whitewashed floor, and a dry old voice said, without any particular emphasis:

"Then you will have to use something you haven't counted, child."
