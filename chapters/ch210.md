## Chapter 210: Where Two Rivers Meet

Shuanghe was not on most maps any more, and the road to it was gravel for the last eleven kilometers, and Liang Fu walked all of it with his weight off his right leg and would not get in the truck.

"*He says he would like to arrive on foot,*" said Seed, when Yuanshu asked. "*He says the sergeant always said you should arrive at a place the way you left it. He left on foot. On the march. In the Black Tide.*"

So they walked.

It was spring. The plain south of Heishui was green, with the old fortifications of the Northern War Zone running along the horizon to the north like a gray line drawn with a ruler, and the gravel road went between fields of young wheat toward a line of willows. Yuanshu walked with the staff. Lingyue walked beside him with her grandfather's sword. Old Seven walked a pace behind the corporal, the way a captain walks behind a man who is going home, close enough to catch him and far enough not to have to.

Nobody else had come. Liang Fu had asked them not to. He said he did not remember enough to want a crowd.

---

The village was small.

Sixty houses, perhaps, gray brick, tiled roofs, on a low bank where two rivers came together under the willows: a wide slow brown one from the north and a narrow fast green one from the west, meeting in a long swirl of two colors that did not mix for a kilometer downstream. A school. A shop. A shrine with a red door. And at the point of land where the two rivers met, a stone landing, old, worn into steps, and tied to it, rocking on the brown water, a flat wooden ferry with a rope running across the river to a post on the far bank.

Liang Fu stopped at the edge of the village and looked at it for a long time.

> **He says he does not remember it.**
>
> **He says: but the rope is in the right place.**

---

There was a woman at the ferry.

Fifty, perhaps. Broad. Sunburned. In a straw hat and rubber boots, sitting on the stone steps of the landing with a cigarette and a thermos, waiting for somebody to want to cross. She looked up when they came down the gravel road between the willows: a young man with a white staff, a young woman with a sword on her back that was much too big for her, and two skeletons.

She did not get up. She did not drop the cigarette. She looked at them for a long moment, the way people on Blue Star had learned to look at the dead in the last two years: carefully, and without running.

"You're him," she said. "From the television."

"Yes."

"The one who asks." She looked at the shorter skeleton, standing at the top of the landing with his weight off one leg. "And who's that?"

"His name's Liang Fu," said Yuanshu. "He was a ferryman here. Before the Black Tide. He marched north with Lu Xuanming and didn't come back." He was quiet. "He's come back."

The woman did not say anything for a while.

Then she put the cigarette out on the stone, carefully, and stood up.

"My mother's brother," she said. "My uncle. Liang Fu. I was sixteen when he marched north. He had a bad knee. He talked to the river." She looked at the short skeleton at the top of the landing. "He took people across for free if they were sad. My mother used to shout at him about it."

---

Liang Fu did not remember her. He did not remember her, at sixteen, on this landing, or the house she had been born in, or the shrine with the red door, or the names of the willows. Seed said so. He did not remember most of anything. There was a hole where it had been.

But he walked down the stone steps of the landing to the ferry, slowly, with his weight off his right leg, and stood on the edge of it, and looked at the rope.

And put his hand on it.

> **He says: it's the right rope.**
>
> **He says: not the same rope. It's been more than thirty years. Ropes don't last thirty years.**
>
> **He says: but it's in the right place, and it's the right thickness, and it's tied the right way at the post on the other side. He can see it from here. He says it's tied the way he used to tie it.**

The woman at the top of the landing made a sound.

"My mother tied it," she said. "After. She took the ferry when he didn't come back. She said she tied it the way he showed her." Her voice was rough. "I tie it the way she showed me."

> **He says: then it's the right way.**

---

He asked if he could take them across.

Seed relayed it. The woman at the top of the landing did not answer at first. Then she came down the stone steps, in her rubber boots, and untied the ferry from the iron ring on the landing, and stepped onto it, and held the rope out to the skeleton with the bad knee.

"It's yours," she said. "It always was."

Liang Fu took the rope.

They crossed. Yuanshu and Lingyue and Old Seven and the woman in the straw hat, standing on a flat wooden ferry in the middle of a river where two colors met and did not mix, while a dead ferryman with a bad knee pulled them across hand over hand on a rope tied the way he used to tie it, slowly, with his weight off his right leg.

Halfway across, he stopped.

He stood in the middle of the ferry, in the middle of the river, where the brown water from the north and the green water from the west swirled against each other under the boards, and looked down at it.

> **He says: I remember this.**
>
> **He says: not the village. Not the house. Not the names.**
>
> **This. The middle. Where you can see both colors. He says he used to stop here when he had someone sad on the ferry. He says he'd stop and show them. Two rivers, going the same way, not mixing. He says he'd tell them: they get there anyway. Both of them. To the sea.**
>
> **He says he doesn't know why he remembers that and not his own mother's name.**
>
> **He says: maybe that's the part that was his.**

---

On the far bank, he tied the ferry to the post.

The way he used to tie it. The way the woman's mother had tied it, and then her. Then he stood on the bank under the willows with his hand on the post, and turned, and looked back across the river at the village, at the sixty gray houses and the school and the shrine with the red door, at the place where two rivers met.

Then he turned to Yuanshu.

> **He says: you said you would say the sergeant's name. At the ferry.**

"Yes."

Yuanshu stood on the far bank of the river under the willows, with the staff in his hand, and looked north. Past the village, past the green plain, past the gray line of the fortifications at Heishui, past the Wastes and the wall of Lingzhou and the small white ring over the end of the world, out past all of it, into the dark of the Sea. To a cold wheel of cracked gray stone with no eyes, hanging dead in the black, very far away. And at the edge of the quiet part, in front of a curtain, sixty soldiers of the Third Battalion standing in a line, facing out, so that the cold would come to them first.

"Huo Changsheng," he said.

Out loud. On the bank of a river in the spring. To nobody. To the room.

"Sergeant. Third Battalion. You held."

> **He says: again. Tomorrow.**
>
> **He says: that's how you do it. Every day. He told me.**

"Every day," said Yuanshu.

Liang Fu stood with his hand on the ferry post for a while longer. Then he sat down on the bank under the willows, slowly, with his bad leg out in front of him, and looked at the river.

> **He says he would like to stay here.**
>
> **He says: not for always. He doesn't know what always is. For now.**
>
> **He says: ask me again in a month.**
>
> **He says: I'd like to take people across. For free. If they're sad.**

The woman in the straw hat, on the ferry, was crying. She had not stopped since the middle of the river. She did not seem to mind.

"I'll ask him," she said. "Every month. I'll come down to the landing and ask him." She wiped her face on her sleeve. "I'll ask him what he wants."

---

That night, in the old banking hall of the Ossuary in Lingzhou, the bell rang.

Not the old way. The monks under the gates in Yamato had been listening every night while the island was gone, through Ren's phone, and Sister Kiyo had heard it change on the night the eyes of the Mouth went out. She had not wanted to tell anyone until the boy was home.

Ren called at midnight. Seed played it.

The long low tone. The wait. The small bells saying *Shiomi*, shorter now, faster, closer. The five quick notes of Ichiro's boat, a little too fast. And then, in the fog-code of a fishing village that had not existed for a thousand years, slower than before, carefully, as if whoever rang it wanted to be very sure it was understood:

> **WE SEE THE COLD FIRE.**
>
> **WE SEE THE SIXTY.**
>
> **THEY ARE STANDING. FACING OUT.**
>
> **WE WILL WAIT WITH THEM.**

Nobody in the banking hall said anything.

At the end of the INSIDE column, round the corner on the second wall, where the seconds were written small under every line, a playing card was fixed to the plaster with a single pin. A queen. Red. Worn soft at the edges.

Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder. She had the marker. She wrote, under the card, in her careful capitals, very small:

*HUO CHANGSHENG. SIXTY. AT THE COLD FIRE. FACING OUT. SOMEONE IS WAITING WITH THEM.*

And under that, smaller still:

*SAY HIS NAME.*
