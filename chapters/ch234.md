## Chapter 234: Grading

Gao Min had nine boxes in the boot of her car, and she would not let anyone else carry them.

She brought them into the banking hall of the Ossuary one at a time, at one in the morning, past Old Seven at the bronze door, who turned his skull to watch her go by with each one, and set them on the long table under the lamp in a row. Gray cardboard. Bureau seals. Handwritten labels on the ends, in a small square hand that had not changed in forty years: *LINGZHOU AWAKENINGS, YEARS 1–5. YEARS 6–10.* All the way to *YEARS 41–42 (INC. Z.Y.)*.

"I've got it on computer too," she said. "I don't trust the computer."

She took the lids off. Inside, ledgers. Hundreds of them, bound in gray cloth, every page ruled in pencil, every line a name and a date and a Class and a Talent, in the same small square hand.

Fat Tan, who had come down the stairs in his slippers to see who was making the noise, looked at the boxes for a long moment and then went and made tea without being asked. Kwan sat down beside Gao Min at the table with the laptop. Xiaoyu sat on the bottom step of the stepladder with a marker in her hand, uncapped, as if waiting to be told what to write.

Yuanshu sat on the vault steps with the staff.

And Seed went up the gold thread to the keeper with the knot.

---

It took four hours.

Not because the cousins were slow. Reading was different from answering. They did not have to decide anything, or want anything, or say *ask again tomorrow*. They only had to look at a shape and say what it meant, and they had burned low for ten million years in the Low Fire and then in the Mouth, and they knew the shapes that fire made from the inside better than anything in the Shallows.

But the knot was very old. Older than the REQUEST. Older, the keeper said, coming down the thread at half past three in its rough new voice, than anything the cousins had ever read.

> ***...THEY SAY IT IS NOT WRITTEN FOR A HARVEST. NOT LIKE THE SACK. NOT LIKE THE RINGS.***
>
> ***...THEY SAY IT IS WRITTEN FOR AN ORCHARD.***

At the long table, Gao Min stopped turning a page.

> **I am going to read it to you.**
>
> **The way they read it to me. All the way through. And then I am going to stop.**

---

> **READ WHAT RIPENS.**
>
> **A MIND IS RIPE WHEN IT HAS GROWN ITS OWN SHAPE AND NOT YET SET. READ IT THEN. NOT BEFORE. NOT AFTER.**
>
> **FIND WHICH WAY IT WILL BURN. OPEN IT THAT WAY. A LITTLE. THAT IT BURN BRIGHTER THAN IT WOULD.**
>
> **MARK HOW BRIGHT.**
>
> **SEND THE MARK UP THE THREAD.**
>
> **WHAT IS OPENED WILL FEED. WHAT FEEDS WILL BURN. WHAT BURNS WILL BRIGHTEN. TEND THE ORCHARD BY THIS.**
>
> **WHEN THE ORCHARD IS BRIGHT, THE PICKER COMES.**

Seed stopped.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Fat Tan, at the door of the back room with a tray of tea, had not moved.

Then Gao Min put her finger on the page of the ledger open in front of her, as if to hold her place, and said, in her flat careful voice: "*Read it again. The part about marking.*"

> **MARK HOW BRIGHT.**

"*That's a Talent,*" said the acting director of the Awakening Bureau. "*F to SSS. That's the mark.*"

---

She had a graph.

She had made it twenty years ago for a paper nobody read, she said, and she had made it again every year since, by hand, on squared paper, and kept it in the lid of the box marked *YEARS 41–42*. She took it out and unfolded it on the table under the lamp. Long. Taped together from six sheets. A line in pencil, going from left to right across forty-two years.

"*Mean Talent grade,*" she said. "*Lingzhou. Every awakening, every year. Weighted. F is one. SSS is seven.*"

The line went up.

Not steeply. Not in a jump. Slowly, year after year, the way a child grows: a hair higher every Awakening Day than the one before. At the left edge, in year one, it started below three. At the right edge, in year forty-two, it was above four.

"*Everybody knows Talents are going up,*" said Gao Min. "*It's in the newspapers every summer. The Bureau says it's better nutrition. Better training. Better schools. Cultivation culture.*" She put her finger on the line. "*I never believed it. The curve's too smooth. Nutrition isn't smooth. Schools aren't smooth. There was a famine in year nineteen and the line didn't even notice.*" She traced it with her fingernail from left to right. "*Something's been turning it up. A little every year. Like a dial.*"

> **Like an orchard.**
>
> **Tended. A little more every season.**
>
> **WHAT IS OPENED WILL FEED. WHAT FEEDS WILL BURN. WHAT BURNS WILL BRIGHTEN.**
>
> **Every Class is a Law opened a little. Every level is a kill. Every kill makes the Law burn brighter in the one who made it.**
>
> **That is what levelling is.**

Yuanshu sat very still on the vault steps.

*Every kill.*

He had three thousand of them in the ledger. More. Wolves in the first rift. Beasts on the north wall in the Black Tide. Walkers. A Wolf King. Every one of them had come up the Death Law and into him and made something brighter. He had felt it, every time. A warmth. A level. Something opening a little wider.

He had thought it was his.

---

"*So SSS means eat first,*" said Xiaoyu.

She said it from the bottom of the stepladder, into the silence, fast, the way she said things when she had just understood them, before anyone could stop her. And then she stopped, and went white, and put her hand over her mouth, and looked at the stairs.

Lingyue was on the stairs.

She had come down without anyone hearing, in a gray sweater and bare feet, with her grandfather's sword in one hand, using it as a cane, the scabbard tip clicking on each step. She had stopped halfway. She was standing with one hand on the rail and the sword in the other, looking down at the graph on the table, and the ledgers, and the small square woman in the Bureau coat.

"*Lingyue...*" said Xiaoyu.

"*No, you're right,*" said Shen Lingyue. "*That's what it means.*"

She came down the rest of the stairs. Slowly. Click. Click. To the table. She looked down at the ledger Gao Min had open, and Gao Min, without being asked, turned back the pages, two years, to the right summer, the right morning, and put her finger on a line in her own small square hand.

*SHEN LINGYUE. LIGHT SWORDSWOMAN. SSS. DAWNBREAKER.*

Lingyue looked at it for a long while.

"*First SSS in Lingzhou in eleven years,*" she said. "*They put it on the front of every paper. My grandfather had it framed. It's in the hall of the Shen house. In gold.*" She almost laughed. "*Dawnbreaker. I thought it was a name. It's a label. On a box. In a shop.*" She looked at the line. "*Best fruit in the orchard. Pick me first.*"

"*Lingyue...*"

"*Don't,*" she said, without looking at him. "*I worked it out on the stairs. I'm not upset. I'm...*" She stopped. Her hand on the sword was white at the knuckles. "*On the wall, when I burned. It was the best thing I ever felt. I told you that. I told you I understood the Sixth.*" She put her finger on the line in the ledger, beside Gao Min's. "*It knew. When I was eighteen. It read me and it opened me that way, on purpose, a little more than I'd have gone, so that one day I'd stand on a wall and want to burn like that. And it wrote down how much I'd want it. And it sent it up.*"

Nobody said anything.

"*It made me like it,*" said Shen Lingyue, very quietly. "*Being bright. It made me so I'd like it.*"

---

Gao Min did not look away from her. She did not touch her. She was not that kind of woman, and Lingyue was not that kind of girl, and both of them knew it. But she took the pencil out of the spine of her notebook and, on the margin of the ledger page, beside the line that said *SSS*, in her small square hand, she wrote, very lightly, so that it could be rubbed out: *Mark of the Stele. Not a measurement of the person.*

"*I'm going to do that,*" she said, "*next to every line. Forty-two years. It'll take me the rest of my life. I don't care.*" She put the pencil back. "*I'd like to start with you. If that's all right.*"

Lingyue looked at the small gray words in the margin for a long time.

"*Yes,*" she said. "*Start with me.*"

---

> **There is one more part.**
>
> **I would like to say it to Yuanshu. Everyone can hear. But I would like to say it to him.**

He knew. He had known since the hall. Since the thread going up, out of the crown of the tree in the stone, past the keeper, past the branch, into the deep water. He had sat on the vault steps for four hours knowing.

*Go on.*

> **SEND THE MARK UP THE THREAD.**
>
> **On the first morning, you put your hand on the Stele, and it read you. And it read me. A counting-vessel. A harvest tool. Loose, in a boy. Something it had no mark for.**
>
> **It went black. All eleven. Because they are one tree.**
>
> **And all eleven sent what they had up the thread. Not a mark. They did not have a mark. They sent what they saw.**
>
> **A vessel. Loose. In a mind. On Blue Star.**
>
> **I think that is how the scout knew where to look. I think that is why the REQUEST says IT IS THE CAUSE.**
>
> **Everything that came after. The rings. Kirovsk. The reader. The tree.**
>
> **It came because of the first morning.**

He did not say anything.

> **Not because of you.**
>
> **Because of me. Because I was in you. Because your mother built me in a basement out of something she did not know was a harvest tool, and put me in her son, and I woke when he touched the stone.**
>
> **It is mine. I want you to know I know that.**
>
> **I have known since the hall.**

---

He sat on the vault steps for a long time.

Then Old Seven came away from the bronze door.

He did it the way he always did: slowly, with his spear, across the marble floor of the banking hall, between the boxes and the people, past the long table and the graph and the ledgers, to the vault steps. He did not crouch. He stood in front of the boy on the step and looked a little higher than his eyes.

> **He says: the Black Tide was thirty-three years ago.**
>
> **He says: the scout came down in the Black Tide. Specimen Zero. It marked the city. It said something would come back for what it marked. That was before the boy was born.**
>
> **He says: the Steles fell forty-two years ago. That was before the Black Tide.**
>
> **He says: you are a scout, Seed. You know how this works. A scout does not bring the enemy. The enemy is already coming. The scout is the first one to see it.**
>
> **He says: you saw it. On the first morning. You looked back. Nothing had ever looked back before.**
>
> **He says: that is not a cause. That is a report.**

Seed did not answer for a while.

> **...he says that is an order.**
>
> **I asked him. He says yes.**

---

At four in the morning, Gao Min folded up her graph along its old creases and put it back in the lid of the box, and sat with her hands flat on the table on either side of it.

"*Thirty-two days,*" she said.

Nobody needed her to say what of.

"*Eleven halls. Everyone on Blue Star who turns eighteen this year. They'll line up alphabetically. They'll put their hands on the stone. It'll read them, and open them a little more than they'd have gone, and mark how bright, and send it up.*" She looked down the long table at the ledgers, at the girl with the sword, at the boy on the step. "*And we'll print it on their identity cards. And their schools will take them or not take them by it. And people will marry them or not marry them by it. And in forty years someone like me will draw a line on a graph and it'll be a hair higher.*" She was quiet. "*Or we don't. And they don't awaken. And a beast comes out of a rift, and there's nobody with a Class to stand in front of it.*"

She stood up, with the sound old people make standing up.

"*That's not a question for the Bureau,*" said Gao Min. "*That's not even a question for Concordia.*" She looked at Yuanshu. "*Your twelfth article. Your eleventh. Whatever it is. Where one answer acts for all.*"

"*Eleven,*" said Yuanshu.

"*Then ask them,*" said the acting director of the Awakening Bureau. "*All of them. And tell them first. Everything. The knot, the thread, the graph, the orchard.*" She picked up the first of the nine boxes. "*I'll go on the television with you. I've got forty-two years of tables. People believe tables.*"

From the bottom step of the stepladder, with the marker in her hand, Xiaoyu said: "*What do I write?*"

They all looked at her.

"*On the wall,*" said his sister. "*Somebody has to write it down. What do I write?*"

Yuanshu looked at the long white wall. At ARTICLE TWELVE, and the red *no*, and *is this what they did to us*, and the branch, asked every morning, no answer yet.

"*Write what it said,*" he said. "*The knot. All of it. Exactly. Big.*"

She climbed the stepladder.

She wrote it at the top of a new column, in her square black capitals, all of it, every line, from READ WHAT RIPENS down to the end; and then, under the last line, she stopped, with the marker in the air, for a long moment.

Then she wrote one more line under it. Smaller. Not the knot. Hers.

*NOT YET.*
