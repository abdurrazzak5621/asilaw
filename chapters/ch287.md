## Chapter 287: Corraine

Tam asked on the twenty-sixth day, and he asked the rowers first.

Not the First of the Ladder. Not Halvarde. Not the boy from Blue Star, though he told him afterward that he had wanted to. He went down to the first array on the morning of the twenty-sixth day, barefoot, with Seri holding his hand, and stood in the middle of the vast curved room like the inside of a bell, among the cells that still burned low with people in them rowing, and asked them.

In the Federation's language. Without the grade. Through Halvarde, who could not hear them but knew all their names, and through himself, who could.

"*We fold past Corraine tomorrow,*" he said. "*It's on the way. Halvarde says it's half a day if we stop. I'm from Corraine. Seri's from Corraine. Some of you are from Corraine.*" His voice was very steady. "*I'd like to stop. Half a day. I'm asking you. You're the ones rowing.*"

And the rowers answered. Each to its nine. Round the arrays, cell to cell, slow, the way the net answered things. Halvarde came back up to the observation deck at noon with a slate in her hand and her face doing something Seed said it had never seen a C's face do.

"*Twenty-nine thousand and four say yes,*" she said. "*Eleven hundred say ask me tomorrow. Six say no.*" She looked at the slate. "*Four hundred and twelve are from Corraine. All four hundred and twelve say: stop.*"

---

Corraine was gray.

He saw it from the observation deck when the flagship came out of the fold, with the twelve small lights of the Rowed Fleet coming out round it one after another. A world of gray seas and gray skies and long low gray islands, like a world drawn in pencil and never colored in. One small sun, cold. And on the largest of the islands, at the mouth of a gray harbor, a town: low houses, boats drawn up on shingle, a long stone quay. And above it, on a headland, a black stone.

"*That's it,*" said Tam. He was at the window with his hands flat on the glass. "*The stone. On the headland. You can see it from every house in the town. They put it there so you'd see it every day.*"

"*Why?*"

"*So you'd remember,*" said Seri. She was beside him, twelve in the body and thirty-three inside, with her chin up. "*That you'd be read. On the morning you were twelve. That it was coming.*" She looked at the black stone on the gray headland. "*Fuel orchard. Every family on Corraine gives. It's the law. You grow up looking at it.*"

> **Two hundred and eleven thousand people on Corraine.**
>
> **The stone reads the children every year. Four thousand a year. Most of them are marked F or E. Corraine was tuned for it. The Ladder planted it that way, Iselde says. A world where most children are dim. So there is always fuel.**
>
> **The reading is in nine days.**

---

The First of the Ladder went down to Corraine first.

Not in a ship. He walked. Down out of the flagship's bay onto nothing, on his invisible staircase, at the same even pace, unhurried, in white without gold at the collar, with the cracked face in his pocket. Down through the gray sky of Corraine to the long stone quay of the town at the mouth of the harbor, where two hundred and eleven thousand people had come out of their low gray houses to stand on the shingle and look up at a cathedral that was not burning the way the ships of the Great Houses burned, and twelve small lights round it the color of the Low Fire.

He stood on the quay.

And the people of Corraine went down on their knees.

All of them. On the shingle, on the quay, on the roofs. The way people go down when the First of the Ladder walks onto their world. Not Precedence; his face was cracked, and Precedence was broken, Seed said, for him and for everyone, because the Ladder had no top any more. Just habit. Ten thousand years of it.

Dacian Severin stood on the quay of Corraine and looked at them kneeling.

"*Get up,*" he said.

Nobody did.

He stood there. Then he said it again, in the Federation's language, which has the grade in every sentence about a person, and this time he took the grade out. Seed heard him do it. Badly. Like a man saying a word in a language he learned from a book.

"*Please get up. I'm not here to read anyone. I'm here because two children from this town asked to come home.*"

---

Tam's mother was on the quay.

He knew her before Seed did. Before Tam said anything. He came down the gold-less road that Severin had walked, behind Tam and Seri, with Old Seven one-armed behind him and the Third Battalion's sixty ash shapes behind that, and Lingyue with the baby and the sword, onto the long stone quay of a town on a gray world, and saw a woman standing at the front of the crowd, not kneeling, with her hands at her sides, staring at a boy of twelve walking toward her down the quay.

Forty-something. Gray in her hair. A fisherwoman's hands. Tam's face, older.

Tam stopped.

Ten meters away. On the quay. With Seri's hand in his.

His mother did not move.

"*Tam,*" she said.

In the language of Corraine, which was the Federation's language with the corners worn off, the way the language of a harbor wears down. Seed translated it.

"*You're... you're...*" She stopped. "*You're the same.*"

"*The pressing stops you,*" said Tam. Very steadily. "*I was twelve when I went in. I'm twelve. I'm thirty-one inside.*"

His mother put her hand over her mouth.

"*They paid us,*" she said, through her fingers. "*The House. When they took you. It was the honor. Everyone said. The whole street came. They paid us three years of catch.*" Her voice cracked. "*We bought a boat.*"

Tam did not say anything.

"*Your father named it after you,*" said his mother. "*The boat. It's in the harbor. He died four winters ago. I still go out in it.*" She took her hand away from her mouth. "*Tam. Tam. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I took the money. I stood in the box and watched them read you and I let them take you and I took the money.*"

---

He watched Tam stand on the quay.

He did not go to him. Nobody did. Lingyue started forward and stopped herself. Seri did not let go of his hand. Old Seven stood at the back, one-armed, facing out. On the quay, two hundred and eleven thousand people of Corraine, still kneeling, most of them, watched a boy of twelve who was thirty-one look at his mother.

"*Mum,*" said Tam.

She did not move.

"*I'm going to ask you something,*" said the boy. In the language of Corraine. Without the grade. "*I learned it on the world that asks. They tell you first what they'll do with your answer. So I'm telling you first.*" He swallowed. "*Whatever you say, I'm going to go and sit in Dad's boat for a while. And then I'm going to come back and have tea. And then I'm going to go home. To Blue Star. Where I'm not anything until I say what I want to be.*"

His mother stared at him.

"*What do you want?*" said Tam.

It took her a long time.

And when she answered, it was not to him. It was to the black stone on the headland above the town, the one every house could see, which she had looked at every day of her life and every day of his.

"*To have said no,*" said Tam's mother.

---

Tam let go of Seri's hand.

He walked the last ten meters down the quay, barefoot, on the old stone, to his mother, and stopped in front of her. He was a head shorter than her. He had been a head shorter than her for nineteen years.

"*Then say it now,*" he said.

His mother looked down at him.

"*It's too late.*"

"*It's not,*" said Tam. "*There are four thousand children on Corraine being read in nine days. The stone's going to read them and mark them F and E and send the mark up, and the Houses are going to come for them.*" He looked up at the black stone on the headland. "*Unless someone's in it.*"

---

They went up to the headland at dusk.

The whole town. Not because anyone asked them to. Because Tam's mother went, and then Seri, and then the First of the Ladder, walking up the long path from the harbor to the headland with the people of Corraine standing up off their knees on the shingle one at a time as he passed, not because he told them to, but because, a woman said afterward, he did not look like someone you knelt to any more. He looked like someone walking up a hill.

And at the top, on the headland, in the cold gray wind off the sea, the stone.

Black. Warm. Ten meters high, driven point-down into the gray turf. Runes dull. Nobody in it.

And beside it, on the slope of the headland, facing the sea, a field of small gray stones.

> **The dead of Corraine.**
>
> **Fishermen. Most of them. Lost at sea, most of them, with no bodies. The stones say so. Names, and a boat's name, and a year.**
>
> **And some who died in their beds.**
>
> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **Tam's grandmother is here.**

---

He knelt on the gray turf of the headland at Corraine with the staff across his knees, in front of a small gray stone with a name on it he could not read and Seed could, and asked.

The way he had asked on the first night. Gently, through the Death Law, through the staff, as quietly as he could. Not to raise. To ask.

*My name's Zhou Yuanshu. I'm from a world called Blue Star. Your grandson is standing behind me. Tam. He's twelve. He's thirty-one. He came home.*

The answer came slowly. The dead of Corraine had never been asked anything. The stones never read the dead.

But it came.

> **She says: Tam?**
>
> **She says: the little one? The one who cried when the boats went out?**
>
> **She says: they took him. I stood on the quay and told them no. I was the only one. They took him anyway. My daughter took the money. I didn't speak to her for a year.**
>
> **She says: I died the next winter. I didn't speak to her for a year and then I died.**

Behind him, on the turf, Tam's mother had made a sound.

*He's here,* said Yuanshu. *He's home. Your daughter's here too. She's just said she wishes she'd said no.*

The dead woman under the small gray stone on the headland was quiet for a long time.

> **She says: tell her I know.**
>
> **She says: tell her I knew the whole time. That's why I didn't speak to her. Because I knew she wished it, and she didn't say it, and I couldn't stand it.**
>
> **She says: tell her she's said it now. Tell her that's enough for one night.**

---

He told her about the stone.

All of it. Wen Jia. The knot. The question. Four thousand children in nine days. What it would mean: to go into the black stone on the headland that she had stood under on the quay and said no to, and sit in it, and ask every child who touched it what they wanted to become. And never come out, unless she wanted the door.

He told her first. He did not ask her. He said, at the end, *you can say no, and nobody will ask you again*.

The dead woman under the gray stone did not answer at once. The wind came in off the sea.

> **She says: I said no once.**
>
> **She says: on the quay. When they took him. I was the only one. Nobody listened.**
>
> **She says: if I'm in the stone, they'll have to listen. Every child. On the morning. Before anything else.**
>
> **She says: yes.**
>
> **She says: put me in it. I've got a lot of no's saved up. I'd like to spend them.**

---

Old Seven put his hand on the stone at Corraine at nine in the evening, the one arm, the right one, with all five fingers still on it, while Yuanshu wore him like a coat, and the gray wind came in off the sea. It was not one of the forty. The stone did not read him. It did not read the boy inside him. It felt a dead captain's hand flat against it, and nothing it was made to see, and went on humming like a machine left running in an empty building.

And a dead grandmother of Corraine, who had stood on a quay nineteen years ago and said no to the House that took her grandson, went down the dead captain's arm into the black stone on the headland, and through the tree of Laws inside it, to the small tight old knot at the bottom where it had been told, ten thousand years ago, what it was for.

READ WHAT RIPENS.

She sat down in it.

And every rune on the black stone on the headland of Corraine turned, all at once, like a crowd in a street, and pointed down.

Tam's mother stood on the headland in the gray wind with her hand over her mouth. Beside her, her son, twelve in the body and thirty-one inside, who had been taken from this hill. And in front of them, the black stone, with its runes pointing down.

She did not touch it.

Tam did.

He put his hand flat against it, the way he had on the morning he chose, and the letters formed in the gray air above the headland of Corraine, in the language of Corraine, in a hand that was not the stone's, and every one of the two hundred and eleven thousand people of the town on the slope below could see them.

**[What do you want to become?]**

Tam did not answer it.

He looked at the letters for a long time. Then he took his hand away, and stepped back, and turned to his mother.

"*It's your turn,*" he said. "*You've never been asked either.*"

His mother looked at the stone. At her son. At the letters in the gray air over the headland, in the language of the harbor she had been born in, asking.

She put out her hand.

**[What do you want to become?]**

"*Someone who says no,*" said Tam's mother. "*When they come for the children.*"

The stone was quiet for a moment.

**[Chosen.]**
