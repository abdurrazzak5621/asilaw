## Chapter 150: The Window

Four hundred and twelve steps, and no flying, even for Lords.

Qin Hao complained about it the whole way up. He had been a Lord for nine days and had flown, he said, exactly twice, both times by accident, and he did not see why a rule written for Kings should apply to him. Lingyue told him that if he flew up the steps of Concordia in front of the Council on his ninth day as a Lord, she would personally make sure his tenth day was spent explaining it. He walked.

The crater was the same as it had been more than a year ago, on the day of the hearing. A ring of white stone benches open to the sky. The long black volcanic-glass table in the middle, with its five chairs and five flags. And a sixth chair, at the end, that someone had once labeled THE SUBJECT.

The label was gone. Someone had taken it off. The chair was still there.

"Sit," said Chair Adaeze Okafor, from the middle of the table, before he could ask. "It's the witness chair now. We changed the sign."

---

The Atlantic Federation's new Secretary spoke first. He always did now. He was a careful man named Elias Brandt, with a careful voice and a habit of putting his reading glasses on and taking them off again when he was about to say something he had thought about for days.

He put them on.

"I'd like to read the Council something," he said. "A letter. It was sent to my office last week by a citizen of the Atlantic Federation who was in Lingzhou on the fifteenth day, outside the Institute. Her name is Fang Lili. She has given permission for it to be read."

He took the glasses off.

*Mister Secretary. I was one of the eleven thousand. I was moved. I want you to know that nobody hurt me. A King folded me ninety at a time onto a football pitch and a woman from the Quiet sat beside me in silence for three days and brought me soup I didn't eat. I was very angry. I still am.*

*But I want you to know that the boy came and sat on the pavement in front of me the night before, and asked me what I wanted, and listened to the answer, and then told me no. To my face. He didn't send a policeman. He didn't send a law. He sat on the ground and said it himself.*

*I don't agree with him. I'm writing so that whatever you decide, you decide it knowing that.*

Brandt put the letter down.

"The Atlantic Federation has asked for this session," he said, "to discuss two things. The draft of Article Ten. And the death of Major Zhuo Wenli of the Western War Zone, on the summit of the Western Pass, eleven days ago." He looked down the table, at the witness chair. "Mister Zhou. Did you tell Major Zhuo that he could not have what he had asked for?"

"Yes."

"And then he died."

"Yes."

---

The crater was very quiet.

There were not three hundred people on the benches this time. There were perhaps fifty. Delegations. Kings. Witnesses. Orlov, with Sokolova behind him. Shirasagi Ren, alone, in white. Ifeoma Adeyemi in her wheelchair, with her green headwrap and a blanket over her knees despite the heat. Sister Ning, with the Ledger. Su Mingli, who had come from Lingzhou on the Council plane the night before and still did not look as if she had slept.

General Qin's letter lay on the black glass in front of Okafor. *I gave the order. Not the boy. Write it down that way.*

"The general says it was his order," said Brandt.

"The general's being kind," said Yuanshu. "I told Major Zhuo he couldn't have it. Qin Hao did what I told him needed doing. The general wasn't on the summit. He was at the gate, asking his battalion to come down, so that four hundred people wouldn't die." He looked at the letter. "He put his name on it because he thinks I'm too young to carry it. He might be right. But it's mine."

"Why?" said Brandt.

He had expected the question. He had sat with it on a gatehouse step for six hours.

"Because the spire was going to shout," he said. "To something above the sky that farms keepers and has been watching Blue Star for thirty-one years. It was going to tell it that our field was empty and our keeper had turned, and that the cause was me, and that our dead stand up again inside a slope. And then it was going to ask for something, in a knot we can't read." He kept his voice level. "Major Zhuo wanted to be the voice that said it. He'd been asked. He'd said yes. His yes was real. I'd defend it to anyone." He looked at Okafor. "But it was his yes, Chair. Not eight billion people's. He was going to shout through every window on Blue Star."

---

Chair Adaeze Okafor had been a judge for thirty-one years before she sat at the black glass table. She listened to things the way judges listen, with her hands folded and her face perfectly still.

"The drafting committee," she said, "has a text for Article Ten. They've been arguing about it for forty days. They've changed it eleven times." She picked up a sheet of paper. "I'm going to read the twelfth version, and then the Council is going to vote on it, today, and if anyone wants to argue about the commas they can do it in writing."

She put on her reading glasses.

"*Article Ten. A person's consent governs what is done to their own mind and remains.*"

"*No consent, however freely given, extends to harm done to those who have not consented.*"

"*Where one person's choice would bring harm upon others who have not chosen it, that choice may be refused. Whoever refuses it shall do so openly, in person, with the reason given to the one refused, and shall answer for it before this Council.*"

"*To shelter the dead from harm is not to raise, read, harvest or hold them. Shelter given without consent shall be the least necessary, shall end when the harm ends, and shall be recorded and reported.*"

She took the glasses off.

"Your yes is yours," she said. "It stops at someone else's window." She looked down the table at the witness chair. "That's the boy's sentence. The committee tried for forty days to write a better one. We couldn't." She looked at Brandt. "Mister Secretary?"

Elias Brandt was quiet for a moment.

"*In person,*" he said. "*With the reason given.*" He put his glasses on, and took them off. "He sat on the pavement and told her himself."

"He did."

"The Atlantic Federation votes yes," said Brandt.

---

It passed five to nothing.

The second vote was shorter. The Article Seven Commission put the Western Legion on its register: twenty-five thousand six hundred and ninety-five dead soldiers of the Western Pass, standing on their own mountain, defending their own pass, under a dead sergeant who had been a miner, and held from a thousand kilometers away by a nineteen-year-old who had asked every one of them. Sister Ning read the asking aloud into the record, number by number. Orlov asked for a copy. *For my sailors,* he said. *In case they ever want to know what they could say.*

> **Article Ten. Filed next to Article Seven.**
>
> **I have also filed Sergeant Ma's accent. Phonetically. Four pages. In case I need to ask him to repeat himself again, which I will.**
>
> **Yuanshu. You told the Council it was yours. Nobody made you.**

*Somebody had to.*

> **Yes. I am only writing down that it was you.**

Afterward, on the rim of the crater, in the wind off the sea, Ifeoma Adeyemi had her wheelchair turned to face south, and was looking at the ocean.

Yuanshu went to her. The oldest King on Blue Star, two hundred and thirty-one, Tide Law for a hundred and ninety years. She had sunk a spire into the deep sea once with her bare will, and dragged its sand into water so deep the maps showed it as black, and somebody had dredged it up again for gold.

"You felt it," she said, without turning her head. "The one at sea."

"It's going south. Toward you."

"It's going toward Port Abiri." Her very bright black eyes did not move from the horizon. "I can feel it in the water. A ship. A big one. A cargo hauler out of the Atlantic ports, with no flag and no name, and a hold that the sea doesn't like." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "It went past Concordia two days ago, eighty kilometers out. It will reach my coast in nine days. Perhaps eight."

"Why Port Abiri?"

"Because that's where its mother died," said Ifeoma Adeyemi. "That's where I killed the first one. A seed goes home to where it was planted, the way a salmon goes home to the river." She turned her head at last and looked at him. "And because of the hill, child. The fisherman's widow goes up it every morning. She sits by her husband's grave and waits for seven Architects to come back and say they're sorry. In five months it will be a year. They promised." Her mouth thinned. "She's been sitting there every morning since. In a black dress. Waiting to hear it."

"The Abbot knows that."

"Of course he knows that. He's not a fool. He's a kind man who wants to be kept." The old King looked back at the sea. "What better place to give a word its voice than a hill full of graves, where the seven who built the Hungers have promised to come back? He wants them to find it there. He wants them to know what they were apologizing to."

---

He found Lingyue on the steps at dusk, sitting halfway down, with two swords across her knees, looking at the sea.

"South," she said.

"South."

"And Ren's going home to the mountain. To the shrine." She did not look at him. "The Abbot's shrine. She thinks the other seed's there. She says it's hers to deal with."

"She's right."

"I know she's right." Lingyue turned her grandfather's sword over on her knees. "I'd like to go with her."

He looked at her.

"Not instead of you," she said. "After. Or before. I don't know." She was quiet. "She's a King, Yuanshu. She carried a dead monk's mind into three hundred people in this crater. And she let him walk away down the mountain because she thought grief would pass. She's going home to fight the man who gave her rice cakes." She looked at him at last. "Someone should stand next to her. The way you've stood next to me."

"Then go."

"You're not going to argue?"

"No."

"You're not going to ask me if I'm sure?"

"You're sure," said Yuanshu. "I can tell. You get a line here." He touched the place between her eyebrows. "Lingyue. I asked you once if you wanted to come. You told me to stop asking you things I know."

She stared at him. Then she laughed. Out loud, on the steps of Concordia, so suddenly that a Council guard at the bottom turned to look.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. Port Abiri first. Then Yamato." She stood up and slung both swords onto her back, the King's one too, and did not let him see how heavy it was. "And Yuanshu."

"Mm."

"When we get back. You owe me a spar."

"I won the last one."

"That's why you owe me one," said Shen Lingyue.
