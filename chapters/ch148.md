## Chapter 148: Sand on the Pass

The sand covered the summit to a depth of a meter, and it took the Ninth Mountain Battalion three days to dig the fortress out of it.

They did it themselves. Nobody asked them to. On the morning after the spire fell, the soldiers who had come down off the walls to stand with their general, and the soldiers who had knelt in the rings and been folded or eased or walked backward out of the courtyard, and the ones who had sat in the sand afterward with their faces in their hands, all of them, got up and found shovels and began to dig. In silence. In their winter whites. The way the dead had once dug through eighty meters of cracked rock in the dark.

Nobody had died. Not one of the four hundred.

Except one.

They found Major Zhuo Wenli's body on the second day, under the sand at the foot of where the spire had stood, curled on his side with his hands open. General Qin had him carried down to the memorial wall at the end of the saddle, the low stone wall with the soldiers' families cut into it, and laid him at the foot of it, under three names.

*Zhuo Meilan. Zhuo Lili. Zhuo Yuping.*

The general did not say anything over him. He stood at the wall with his one hand on the stone for a long time, in the wind. Then he went back to the gatehouse and sat down at his camp table and wrote a letter, in his own hand, slowly, to the Concordia Council, and signed it, and gave it to Yuanshu to read.

*Major Zhuo Wenli, my chief of staff for twenty years, died on the summit of the Western Pass on the morning of the twenty-ninth. He was inside the spire when it fell. He was asked, twice, to come out, and said no. Mister Zhou Yuanshu then told him that he could not have what he asked for, and the spire was destroyed by my grandson, Qin Hao, on my order, with Major Zhuo inside it.*

*I gave the order. Not the boy. Write it down that way.*

*Qin Zhenyue.*

---

"You didn't give the order," said Yuanshu.

"I'm a general," said General Qin. "Every order on this mountain is mine. That's what a general is for." He did not look up from the camp table. "You're nineteen. You're going to make a great many decisions like that one before you're my age. Some of them will be right. Some of them will be like mine on the ridge in the Black Tide." He folded his one hand on the table. "You don't need to carry this one too. I've got room."

"He asked me what he wanted. I told him he couldn't have it."

"Yes."

"I've never done that. Not once. Not since the Stele."

"I know." The general looked at him at last, with eyes as hard and clear as winter water. "You did it because he was going to shout into the sky and bring something down on eight billion people. I read your report. I read the bit where you asked him twice." He was quiet. "That's more than anyone ever asked my men on the ridge."

Two thousand one hundred and thirteen. Who had said *quiet* and been burned anyway. Who would never be asked anything again, because there was nobody left to ask.

*If I'd come back sooner,* he thought. *A year ago. If I'd come back to check on them, the way I came back to the cemetery.*

Qin Hao found him on the step at dusk.

He had changed. Not to look at. He was still Qin Hao, in dark green Western War Zone fatigues with his father's colonel's star over his heart, a little too tall, a little too thin, with a face that had always looked as if it were about to argue with you. But he walked differently. He walked like Su Mingli walked. Like a very large thing pretending to be a person.

He sat down on the step beside Yuanshu without asking.

"Lord," he said.

"I heard."

"Level sixty-one. On a spire. With white fire." He looked out at the dark mountain. "I'm the first in my class. You know that? First Lord from our year at the Academy. Shen Lingyue's still Elite. You're still Elite." He was quiet for a moment. "I always thought, when it happened, I'd come and find you and say it. *I'm first.* I thought about it for two years. What your face would do."

"What's my face doing?"

Qin Hao looked at him.

"Nothing," he said. "It's doing nothing. You look like you've been sitting on a step for six hours counting dead people." He looked away. "It's not as good as I thought."

They sat there.

"He wanted to be kept," said Qin Hao after a while. "Zhuo. He taught me to read maps. When I was eight. On this mountain. He used to give me sweets from his pocket when Grandfather wasn't looking." His voice was very flat. "I burned the thing he was climbing, and he fell out of the sky, and the keeper took him." He looked at his hands. "Is he all right? Up there?"

> **The keeper says he is in the field.**
>
> **The keeper says it asked him this morning if he still wanted to be kept, and he said yes.**
>
> **The keeper says he asked to sit by the three empty places where the columns of his family stood a year ago.**
>
> **The keeper says it said yes.**

Yuanshu told him.

Qin Hao did not say anything for a while. Then he nodded, once, and stood up, and put his hand on Yuanshu's shoulder, briefly, the way Old Seven put a hand on someone's head in a crowd.

"I'm still first," he said. "Don't forget."

"I won't."

"You will. You'll be a Lord by next month and act like it never happened." He walked off down the frozen road toward the trucks. Over his shoulder: "I'm writing it down. In a ledger. With witnesses."

He looked up.

South. The Southern Compact. A long coast of white towns with blue shutters, and a bay where a spire had once grown out of the sea, and an old woman in a green headwrap who had told seven Architects to come back in a year and say it again, to her, because she was too stubborn to die while they still owed her.

She was still waiting for them.
