## Chapter 32: Night Market of Bones

The north gate of Lingzhou closed at ten every night, and at midnight the market opened on the other side of it.

You couldn't see it from the wall. That was the point. A kilometer out on the Wastes, past the last checkpoint, past the rows of rusted steel tank traps that had stood on the black glass since the Black Tide, there was a dip in the ground where an old artillery bunker had collapsed. Inside the dip, out of sight, lanterns came on one by one. Paper lanterns. Hundreds of them, strung on wires between the broken concrete, red and yellow and a dirty white, swaying in the cold wind off the plain.

From above, Seed said, it looked like a wound that had learned to glow.

---

They came across the glass at 12:40 with a handcart.

It was a flat wooden cart with two bicycle wheels, the kind scrap dealers use, and it squeaked on every turn. Yuanshu pushed it. He wore a stained canvas jacket, a knitted cap pulled low, and a cheap disposable breathing mask over his mouth and nose, the kind people wore near the Wastes against the glass dust. Qin Hao walked beside him in a padded coat and a scarf wrapped up to his eyes, with a battered leather satchel over one shoulder. His red jacket was folded at the bottom of the satchel. He had complained about this for four kilometers.

On the cart, under a sheet of blue plastic tarpaulin, lay a skeleton.

It was very still.

> **He is doing very well.**
>
> **I have told him that if anyone touches him, he must not move. He has agreed.**
>
> **He has also told me, in the strongest terms his echo can manage, that if anyone tries to weigh him, he will make an exception.**

At the edge of the dip, two men sat on upturned crates beside a brazier, rifles across their knees. One of them stood up and held up a hand.

"Business?"

"Selling," said Yuanshu, through the mask. He made his voice flat and bored. "One soldier. Old. Whole. Good bone."

The man lifted the corner of the tarpaulin with the barrel of his rifle. Old Seven lay underneath it with his skull turned slightly to one side, eye sockets empty and dark. No gray flame. It had gone out, or gone somewhere deep, when they left the gate.

The man let the tarp fall.

"Nice," he said. "Row four. Weigher's on the left. Don't sell to the alchemists, they'll grind him. Tan pays better for soldiers." He sat back down. "Welcome to the market."

---

The market was louder than Yuanshu had expected.

He had imagined something furtive. Whispers in the dark. It was nothing like that. It was a market. People shouting prices. People laughing. A man frying skewers of something over a gas burner and selling them on paper plates. Music from a phone somewhere, tinny and cheerful. Under the swaying lanterns, between the broken slabs of the old bunker, stalls stood in uneven rows, and on every stall, under the red and yellow light, were the dead.

Bones by the heap. Rift-beast bones mostly, huge and pale, sold by weight to men in heavy aprons who loaded them onto trucks for grinding. Alchemists, Seed said. Bone powder for law medicine. Then, further in, smaller stalls. Human bones in neat bundles, tied with string, with paper tags. *Settler, female, approx. 40. Clean.* *Soldier, Eastern Line era, partial. Cheap.* *Child.*

Yuanshu stopped walking.

He didn't mean to. His feet stopped on their own. He stood at the edge of the stall with the cart handle in his hands and looked at the small bundle, and at the tag, and for a moment he couldn't see anything else.

Qin Hao's hand closed on his arm. Hard. Through the scarf, very quietly:

"Not here. Keep walking."

> **He is right.**
>
> **I have recorded the stall. The tag. The seller's face.**
>
> **We will come back.**

He kept walking.

---

Row four was the soldiers.

There was a set of old brass scales at the end of the row, as big as a bathtub, hanging from a tripod of welded pipe. Beside it, at a folding table under a white lantern, sat a fat man in a padded vest with a ledger open in front of him and a calculator and a cup of tea.

He was writing. Not on a tablet. In the ledger. By hand. In small, neat, careful characters, the way a bookkeeper writes in a shop that has been in the family for three generations. Every few seconds he looked up, took a number from the weigher, and wrote it down.

> **That is Tan.**
>
> **Grandpa Xu said: "Fat Tan. He writes everything down. He always has. He says it is so nobody can cheat him."**
>
> **That book is the most valuable object in this market.**

They didn't go to Tan yet.

Yuanshu parked the cart in the short queue for the scales, between a heap of tagged bundles and a man asleep on a sack, and they walked on past the table, to the end of row four, where the lanterns were fewer and dimmer, and where the market got quiet.

---

The last row had no bones.

It had benches.

Long wooden benches, the kind you see in a train station, set out in a line under three dim white lanterns. And on the benches, sitting in a row, side by side, were people.

Living people.

Eleven of them. Men, mostly. A few women. Most of them young. Most of them in the remains of uniforms: white Northern Union, gray Huaxian Defense Corps, dark green. They sat with their hands in their laps and their backs straight and their eyes open. Breathing. Slowly, evenly, all at the same rhythm, like sleepers.

Nobody was home.

You could see it in their faces. A face is never really still when someone is inside it: there are a hundred small movements, a twitch at the eye, a swallow, the flicker of a thought. These faces had none of that. They were like houses with the doors open and the lights on and all the furniture gone.

Around each of their necks hung a paper tag on a string.

> **Hollows.**
>
> **That is what they call them here. I heard a man say it as we passed.**
>
> **Their minds were taken. Their bodies were not killed. They are alive, the way a tree is alive.**
>
> **Someone buys them.**

Beside Yuanshu, Qin Hao had stopped breathing.

He was looking at the fourth person from the end. A heavy-set young man in a torn dark-green Western War Zone uniform, with a burn scar on one cheek and a shaved head, sitting very straight with his hands in his lap and his eyes on nothing.

The tag around his neck said: *Military. Young. Good condition. 40,000.*

"Private Liang," said Qin Hao. His voice, through the scarf, was a whisper. "Radio operator. Western Pass. He's nineteen." His hand, at his side, had begun to glow. Faintly. Orange through the fingers. The air around it shimmered. "He's *nineteen.*"

Yuanshu caught his wrist.

It was hot. Very hot. It burned his palm and he held on anyway.

"If you burn this place down," he said, very low, "the ten people on the other benches burn with it. And Tan's book burns. And we never find Sergeant Wu." He held on. "Not here. Not yet."

Qin Hao stared at the boy on the bench for a long time.

The glow in his hand faded. Slowly.

"Then how," he said.

---

The seller of the hollows was a thin woman with a cigarette, sitting on a stool at the end of the bench with a thermos and a tablet. She looked up as they came closer and blew smoke at them.

"Looking or buying?"

"Buying," said Qin Hao.

Yuanshu turned to look at him.

Qin Hao didn't look back. He had pulled the scarf down a little from his mouth, and his voice had changed completely. Lazy. Bored. Rich. The voice of a young man who has bought a great many things in his life without asking what they were for.

"The military one. Fourth from the end." He nodded at Private Liang. "Forty thousand?"

"Forty-five," said the woman. "He's popular. Got a buyer coming at three who'll take the whole bench."

"Who?"

She shrugged. "Same as always. Pays well. Never comes himself. Sends a van." She took a drag. "Forty-five or wait till three."

Qin Hao reached into the satchel, took out a fat envelope, counted out notes without looking at them, and dropped them on her tablet.

"Forty-five," he said. "He walks?"

"They all walk. Just tell them where." She peeled the tag off Private Liang's neck and pocketed it. Then she leaned over and said, in the voice you'd use to a dog: "Up."

The boy in the green uniform stood up.

---

They walked away from the bench with Private Liang between them.

He walked when they walked and stopped when they stopped. His eyes were open. His breathing was slow and even. When Qin Hao put a hand on his shoulder to steer him around a puddle, he turned exactly as far as the hand turned him, and not a centimeter more.

Qin Hao didn't say anything. His jaw was clenched so hard Yuanshu could see the muscle jumping under the scarf.

"You just bought a person," said Yuanshu quietly.

"I bought a soldier of the Western War Zone back from a thief," said Qin Hao. "And I'm going to put it in my report in exactly those words." His voice shook, very slightly. "And then I'm going to come back and buy the other ten, and then I'm going to burn this place to the ground."

> **The buyer at three o'clock.**
>
> **"Same as always. Never comes himself. Sends a van."**
>
> **Someone has been buying hollows from this bench for a long time.**
>
> **Tan's book will say who.**

They turned the corner at the end of the row, back toward the brass scales and the fat man writing under his white lantern.

And stopped.

There was a man standing beside Tan's table with a scanner in his hand. A tall man in an orange coverall with a breathing mask hanging around his neck, and a black-and-silver logo on his chest. *NORTHERN GATE REMAINS RECOVERY.* He was sweeping the scanner slowly back and forth over the handcart, over the blue tarpaulin, over the skeleton lying still underneath it.

The scanner was beeping.

Not the steady, bored beep it had made over every other cart in the row. Higher. Faster. Insistent.

The man in orange looked up from the screen, over the cart, straight at Yuanshu.

"Hey, Tan," he called, without taking his eyes off Yuanshu's face. "Come and look at this one. This one's got something *in* it."
