## Chapter 144: Major Zhuo

Major Zhuo Wenli left a letter on the general's camp table, folded once, under a cup of tea he had made and not drunk.

General Qin found it at two in the morning, when the radio in the gatehouse woke everyone with the sound of a sentry on the summit road shouting that the Ninth Mountain Battalion had come up the switchbacks in the dark, four hundred of them, in full kit, and walked past his post without a word, and gone into the old watch fortress on the saddle above the pass, and shut the gate.

The general read the letter standing up. Then he sat down on the camp bed and read it again. Then he held it out to Yuanshu without looking at him.

It was in a neat, careful, slightly old-fashioned hand. The hand of a man who had written the general's orders for twenty years.

*General,*

*I'm sorry. You will think I have been lying to you. I haven't. Everything I have ever told you was true. I simply never told you this.*

*Eight years ago, in the silver rain, my wife and my two daughters were in the pass. You remember. You were there. You found them with me. They were standing by the roadside with their heads turned north, three degrees, and their eyes were empty. You carried Lili back to the trucks yourself. She was nine.*

*They didn't come back. When the boy went up the stalk and asked everyone in the field what they wanted, the ones with bodies came home. My wife and my daughters had no bodies by then. The hospital had let them go years before. So the boy asked their minds, in the field, and the Ledger says they chose quiet.*

*I don't believe it.*

*I've read the Ledger. I know the boy is honest. I know he wrote down what he heard. But there were columns in that field the keeper didn't hold any more. There were columns that had been taken, before he ever went up. The Abbot told me. The keeper itself admits it. Tall shapes walking through the field, taking the brightest.*

*Lili was the brightest thing I ever knew.*

*I think they were taken before the boy got there. I think they're kept somewhere. I think something is keeping them, and it doesn't ask, and it doesn't let go, and I would give anything I have to be kept with them.*

*So I'm going up. With four hundred who feel the same. We won't harm anyone. We won't fire unless fired on, and we won't fire then, either. We only want to be there when it speaks.*

*Please tell your grandson I'm sorry about the tea.*

*Z.*

---

Nobody in the gatehouse said anything.

The general sat on the camp bed with his one hand on his knee, looking at nothing. Qin Hao stood by the window with his arms folded so tightly that his knuckles had gone white. Lingyue had both swords across her back already.

"I carried her," said General Qin finally. "Lili. Down the pass. To the trucks. She weighed nothing. Her head kept turning back north on my shoulder. I kept turning it back." He looked at the letter in Yuanshu's hands. "He's been at my side every day for eight years. Every day. Making tea."

> **I did ask them.**
>
> **In the field. His wife. His daughters. I remember. Three columns, together, in the old Western Pass harvest. I asked them what they wanted, and they said quiet, and I let them go.**
>
> **He is wrong.**
>
> **But he is also right. There were columns already taken. I saw the empty places. I did not know what they were.**
>
> **I cannot prove to him which is true.**

---

They went up the summit road at dawn, and stopped at the last switchback, below the fortress, where the sentry had been.

The old watch fortress on the saddle had been built two hundred years ago to guard the pass. Low walls of iron-gray stone, a gatehouse, a square tower, a courtyard in the middle. The Western War Zone had used it as a weather station for the last forty years. Now its gate was shut, and on the walls, every two meters, a soldier of the Ninth Mountain Battalion stood in winter whites with a rifle across his chest.

Not pointed. Across his chest. The way the dead held rifles on the north wall of Lingzhou.

Major Zhuo Wenli stood on the gatehouse roof with his tablet under his arm.

"General," he called down. His voice carried easily in the thin cold air. "I'm sorry about the letter. I didn't know how else to say it."

General Qin did not answer.

"Mister Zhou." Zhuo looked at Yuanshu, and his thin, tired face was perfectly calm. "I know what you're going to say. You're going to say we're standing in someone else's window. I read about Lingzhou. The woman, Fang Lili. My Lili had the same name. I took that as a sign, which I know is foolish." He looked down at the courtyard behind him. "But we're not in anyone's window up here. There's no city. There's a mountain of graves and two things growing under it, and four hundred people who've asked to be here when they speak."

"The things growing under it," said Yuanshu, "are eating forty-one thousand of your own soldiers."

"I know." Something moved in Zhuo's face. "I served with half of them. I buried a quarter of them myself." He was quiet for a moment. "They said yes to you. To being raised. To being used. They wanted to be useful. I'm only asking to be useful in a different way."

"And the eight thousand who said quiet?"

Zhuo looked at him for a long time across the cold air.

"You covered them," he said. "Last night. I felt it. The whole mountain went dark. You banked every one of them in the ground without asking them, because you'd decided what they'd want." He did not raise his voice. "So you see, Mister Zhou. You and I aren't so different. We both decided what the dead wanted. You just had a vote first."

---

> **That is not true.**
>
> **It is not entirely false either. I hate that.**

They went back down to the switchback and stood in the lee of a rock out of the wind, the general and Yuanshu and Lingyue and Qin Hao and Dabao and Old Seven and Adjutant Xu with his cane.

"Four hundred," said Qin Hao. His voice was very flat. "The Ninth Mountain. Best mountain troops in the Western War Zone. They know every rock on this saddle. They've got rifles and grenades and two mortars and a year of rations in that fortress, because it's a weather station and nobody ever comes up."

"They won't shoot," said Yuanshu.

"They don't need to shoot. They only need to stand there." Qin Hao looked up at the walls. "If we go in, we hurt them. If we hurt them badly enough, they die. Up here. Four hundred meters above the seeds."

"And everything that dies up here," said Lingyue slowly, "runs downhill. Into the seeds. Not into his Field."

> **Yes.**
>
> **The seeds are directly below. Four hundred meters. My Field's slope cannot beat their pull at that distance. Not for the living, whose embers are bright and close.**
>
> **Every one of them who dies on the saddle feeds the seeds. Four hundred people would be a great deal of fire.**
>
> **They know that. I think that is why they came.**

"And the dead can't touch them," said Dabao.

Everyone looked at him.

"Article Seven," said Dabao. He was holding his shield very tightly. "The dead may defend. Never used against the living except to stop a killing. I read it. I've read it a hundred times, on the wall. Those soldiers aren't killing anyone." He swallowed. "So the Spear Corps can't go up there. Old Seven can't go up there. It has to be us. The living."

"Without killing anyone," said Lingyue.

"Without killing anyone," said Yuanshu. "Without even hurting anyone badly. Four hundred mountain troops in a fortress, who want to die, who know every rock, who won't shoot back."

General Qin had not said a word since the letter. He said one now.

"Then we'd better be very good," he said.

---

The spires came up through the courtyard that night.

Yuanshu felt them before anyone saw them. A long grinding shudder through the mountain, rising out of the rock under the saddle like something very large pushing up through a floor. Then, from the switchback below the fortress, in the dark, in the light of the general's lamps, they watched it happen.

The paving of the courtyard cracked. In two places, a hundred meters apart. And up out of each crack, slowly, came a point of gray crystal. Cloudy. Cracked all over with fine dark lines, like old ice. Lit very faintly from inside, the color of a coal. Rising. A meter. Two. Five.

And around each one, on the cracked paving, the soldiers of the Ninth Mountain Battalion had laid down their rifles and knelt in rings, facing in, with their heads bowed, the way people kneel around a fire on a cold night.

> **They are not only growing.**
>
> **They are listening.**
>
> **Yuanshu. When the scout rang, its sisters heard. All of them. These two have the word. FIELD. EMPTIED. KEEPER TURNED. CAUSE. And the knot at the end that I cannot open.**
>
> **They are carrying it up. To the top. To give it a voice.**
>
> **And four hundred people are kneeling around them, asking to be the first thing it says.**
