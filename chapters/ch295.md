## Chapter 295: A Year

> **It has been a year.**

Seed said it on the ninth morning of the voyage home, on the observation deck of the Eighth Candle, while he was sitting on the floor with his back against the window and Xiaodeng asleep on his chest, because Lingyue had said that if she did not sleep for four hours she was going to throw the baby, the sword, and him into the fold, in that order.

> **More than a year. Four hundred and sixteen days.**
>
> **Since the deep water. Since Nana Ampofo stood at the edge of the cloud and said: we'll stay. We'll keep asking. Ask us again in a year.**
>
> **We did not ask.**
>
> **I am sorry. I should have said. On Anthos. At the Assembly. I was counting other things.**
>
> **I did not forget. I want you to know that I did not forget. I knew the number every morning. I did not say it.**

He sat very still with his daughter asleep on his chest.

*Why not?*

> **Because I was afraid of the answer.**
>
> **They are in the deep water. Eleven hundred and forty-six of the drowned of Kesse. Asking the ones who fell out of the hands of the one who picks, nine by nine, the ones at the edge, the oldest and slowest. In the cold and the dark, where nothing pushes back.**
>
> **I thought: if we ask them, they might say they want to come home. And I do not know how to bring them home from there.**
>
> **Or they might say they want to stay forever. And I did not want to hear that.**
>
> **So I did not ask.**
>
> **That is what the Collector did. For a very long time. It did not ask the thing it was afraid of the answer to.**
>
> **I would like to stop doing that.**

---

He asked them that night.

Not from the Candle. The Candle was a rowed listener ship in the middle of a fold, between the inner worlds of the Federation and the edge of the Shallows, and there was no stone on it, and nothing on board that touched the threads except him. So he asked the way he felt everything now: down into the net, and back along it, a very long way, to the Lens.

Ichiro caught it. He said he had been waiting. He said he had been looking out at the deep water through the root ever since the man in the gray coat put the last piece back, and that he could see the edge of the cloud from here, small, the way you see a fishing fleet's lamps from a headland at night. He said he could see the Watch. He said the woman in the coat had looked up once, a month ago, when he looked at her, the way she had looked up when he rang to her on his way home.

> **He says: give it to me. He'll carry it.**
>
> **He says: he'll ring it. In the fog-code. The way he rang it to her the first time. She'll know it's from you.**

So he said it, on the observation deck of the Eighth Candle, with his daughter asleep on his chest and his wife asleep in the next room and a dead captain with one arm in the doorway, facing in. Out loud. Through Seed. Through the Lens, a very long way behind them now, in the bow of a ship rowed by two hundred and forty thousand people who had chosen to be there. Out of the root. Along the threads. Across the dark between arms. To the edge of a cloud of small dim lights that had fallen out of the hands of the oldest thing anyone had ever made.

*Nana Ampofo. It's me. The boy. It's been a year. More. I'm sorry I'm late.*

*I said I'd ask you again. I'm asking.*

*What do you want?*

---

The answer took four days.

He had not expected it to be fast. The deep water was the deep water. Ichiro said that even through the root, even whole, a mark going out to the edge of the cloud and a mark coming back was like ringing a bell across a bay in a fog: you rang, and you waited, and the fog took the sound, and you did not know if anyone had heard until the answer came back out of the white.

On the fourth night, it came.

> **Ichiro has it.**
>
> **He says it is in the fog-code. She learned it. From the quiet ones. From him, a little, when he went past. She rings it badly. He says that is the first thing he has ever heard rung worse than his boat call, and he is delighted.**
>
> **He says it says:**

> **WATCH OF THE SHELF.**
>
> **WE HEARD.**
>
> **YOU ARE LATE. WE KNEW YOU WOULD BE. YOU ARE ALWAYS LATE. THAT IS ALL RIGHT.**
>
> **WE HAVE ASKED ONE HUNDRED AND NINETY-ONE MILLION.**
>
> **MOST WENT THROUGH THE DOOR. SOME WENT DOWN THEIR THREADS. SOME STAYED. THE ONES WHO STAYED WE SING TO. WE SING THEM THE NAMES. THEIR OWN. WE ASKED THEM WHAT THEY WERE CALLED AND WROTE IT DOWN IN OUR HEADS.**
>
> **THERE ARE STILL MANY AT THE EDGE.**
>
> **YAW WANTS TO COME HOME.**

He sat very still on the floor, with the small weight of his daughter rising and falling on his chest, and read it again.

> **YAW ASKED FIRST. IN THE VOTE ABOUT THE TOP. HE IS TIRED. HE HAS ASKED NINE HUNDRED THOUSAND BY HIMSELF. HE WANTS TO LIE AT THE BOTTOM OF THE SEA OFF KESSE AND HEAR THE BOATS GO OVER.**
>
> **FOUR HUNDRED AND TWO WANT TO COME HOME WITH HIM.**
>
> **SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY-TWO WANT TO STAY. AND KEEP ASKING. UNTIL THE EDGE IS EMPTY. THEN THEY WANT TO BE ASKED AGAIN.**
>
> **I AM STAYING.**
>
> **SOMEBODY HAS TO SING THEM THE NAMES.**
>
> **— NANA. ASK ME AGAIN IN A YEAR. ON TIME.**

---

For a while he did not say anything at all.

Lingyue had woken. She had come to the door of the next room and was leaning in it with her arms folded and her hair loose, the way she leaned in doorways, and she had heard. Seed had let her.

"*How do they get home?*" she said. "*Four hundred and three. From the deep water.*"

> **Ichiro says: the root.**
>
> **He says: everything comes through the root now. He passed the man in the gray coat down into the stone on Anthos. He can pass the drowned of Kesse.**
>
> **He says: they'll come along the threads to the Lens. And he'll send them down. Through the net. To the nearest stone to Kesse.**
>
> **Which is Kesse.**
>
> **The midwife is in it.**

Lingyue was quiet for a moment. "*And then?*"

> **And then they walk into the sea.**
>
> **The midwife says she will meet them. She says she held four hundred children at the first breath and she would like to hold four hundred and three of the drowned at the last.**

---

They came home on the eleventh night of the voyage.

He did not see it. He felt it, the way he felt everything on the net now: through Seed, through the Lens a very long way behind, through the threads under everything. Four hundred and three cold dim embers the color of deep water, coming in out of the edge of a cloud of fallen lights in the dark between arms, slowly, the way the drowned answered anything, along the threads, into the root. Where a fog-ringer caught them, one after another, and said each of their names, because he had asked them on the way in, and passed them down.

Through the net. Down. To a stone on a beach at Kesse, on the north coast of the Southern Compact, where a midwife from the bottom of a well had sat in the knot for a year and asked every child of Kesse who touched her what they wanted to become.

And she held them.

He felt that too. Seed said it was the first time anything had come down a stone's thread and been held at the bottom, instead of read. Four hundred and three of the drowned of the coast off Kesse, coming out of the stone at Kesse one after another into the night, onto a beach, and walking down it, slowly, into the sea.

And at the front, the first to come out, the first to walk into the water: a fisherman who had gone over the side of a boat in a storm more than forty years ago, who had answered first the night the boy asked them, who had asked, in a vote about who should be at the top, *why does there have to be a top*.

> **Yaw says: tell the boy thank you.**
>
> **He says: tell him it was cold out there.**
>
> **He says: tell him the sea is warmer.**

---

> **Death Law: 58.3% → 59.1%.**
>
> **I did not open the door. The door was not part of it. They did not go through. They came home.**
>
> **It went up anyway.**
>
> **I think because the Death Law is not only the door. It is everything that happens to the dead. And four hundred and three of them came home from the deep water and walked into the sea. Nobody has ever done that.**

He sat on the floor of the observation deck with his daughter awake now, on his chest, looking up at him with the cloudy unfocused eyes of a child of four months.

*Fifty-nine,* he thought.

> **Yes.**
>
> **I need to tell you something. I have known it since Corraine. I did not want to tell you on Anthos.**
>
> **It is not going to go to sixty.**

He went still, and the baby on his chest stirred, and settled.

> **Sixty is a Fragment. A piece of a Great Law. Complete enough to stand on its own, without you holding it up.**
>
> **I do not know who holds one. I have never stood close to one. I only know what the line looks like from this side.**
>
> **You are a Lord. Level seventy-five.**
>
> **Your body is a Lord's body. Your soul is a Lord's soul. That is what I am built on. I cannot comprehend more than the thing I am built on can hold.**
>
> **I can feel the edge. It is just past fifty-nine. Fifty-nine point nine, perhaps. And then a wall.**
>
> **Not a wall someone built. The kind a cup has. It can only hold so much water. If I pour more, it does not hold more. It breaks.**

*So it stops.*

> **It stops.**
>
> **Until you are more than a Lord.**
>
> **I am sorry. I know you did not do any of it for the number. But I said I would always tell you the numbers.**
>
> **This is the number. It is the first one I have ever told you that I cannot change by running anything.**

---

He sat on the floor and thought about it. His daughter fell asleep again on his chest.

He did not mind.

That surprised him. He sat on the floor of the observation deck of a rowed ship crossing the Star Sea and turned it over, the way you turn over a stone you have picked up on a beach to see the underside, and found that he did not mind at all. He had never wanted the number. He had wanted the door to be a little open, always, so that anyone who came to it could go through. It was. He had wanted to be able to ask. He could.

"*What do I have to do,*" he said, "*to be more than a Lord?*"

> **I do not know.**
>
> **King, probably. A King is a world's worth. Lingyue's grandfather is one. He is past eighty.**
>
> **I do not know how long he was a Lord. I could ask him.**
>
> **You have been awake for three years.**

"*Then it'll take a while.*"

> **Yes.**
>
> **I think that is good.**
>
> **I think the first time I told you the numbers, in the cemetery, at three in the morning, when you raised Old Seven, I thought the numbers were the point.**
>
> **I do not think that any more.**
>
> **I think the point is the door. And the asking. And coming home.**
>
> **The number can wait.**

In the doorway, one-armed, facing in, the tall dead captain of the Eastern Line had turned his skull, a little, toward the floor where the boy sat with his daughter asleep on his chest.

> **He says: a soldier with one arm is still a soldier.**
>
> **He says: a Lord with fifty-nine is still the boy who asked him to get up.**
>
> **He says: the drowned waited a year. He can wait the rest of his.**

He looked up.

> **He says: he told you. At the long table, with the box. Ask me again in a year.**
>
> **He says: it has not been a year. It has been a season. You are not late for him yet.**
>
> **He says: when it is a year, ask him at stone No. 7. Not here. Not on a ship.**
>
> **He says: he already knows what he's going to say.**
>
> **He says: he would like you to have to wait for it. For once.**
