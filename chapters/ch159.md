## Chapter 159: Ify

The river jetty at Sandu was a hundred meters of old gray planking on wooden piles, running out from the mouth of the river into the sea, with a fish market at the shore end and a rusted crane at the other. Ifeoma Adeyemi was sitting at the far end of it in her wheelchair, beside the crane, facing the sea, with her eyes shut and her thin hands open on the blanket.

Her two household men were sitting on the planks on either side of her chair. One of them was holding an umbrella over her against the sun. The other was weeping and trying not to let her hear it.

She had been sitting like that for eight hours and forty minutes.

It had taken them three hours to cover the twenty kilometers of coast road from Kesse, through the cars the town had abandoned when the buses came, and a bridge the army had closed, and a herd of goats that would not be asked anything by anyone.

Yuanshu came down the jetty at a run, with the staff in one hand and the Field coming with him down the coast like a tide coming in, and stopped beside her chair, and felt it.

The sea off Sandu was flat.

Not calm. Flat. Ten kilometers of it, from the mouth of the river north and south along the coast and out past the edge of the shelf, a great sheet of blue water as flat as a floor, with the afternoon swell rolling in from the open ocean and stopping dead at the edge of it, as if it had hit a wall. And under it, three kilometers down, the whole weight of the deep cold sea pressing upward, wanting to rise, the way it had risen every afternoon and every dawn for ten thousand years, and not rising, because an old woman in a wheelchair was sitting on it.

> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **She is holding the whole column. All ten kilometers. Down.**
>
> **And the trunk is under it. It cannot rise. So it is pushing.**

He felt that too. Through the Field, the moment it reached the heap. The Sandu trunk. The largest of the three: four kilometers of cracked gray crystal standing up out of the thickest end of the smear of sand, capped at the top like the one at Kesse, and pressing up against the held sea like a man pressing up against the lid of a coffin. It had stopped trying to ride the tide. There was no tide to ride. So it had started to eat.

> **It is eating everything. All the drowned at the south end. Fast. The way the Western Pass seeds ate when they knew they were found.**
>
> **It is using what it eats to push.**
>
> **She cannot hold it much longer.**

---

"Ifeoma."

The old King did not open her eyes.

"You're late, child," she said. Her voice was very faint and very dry, like paper. "The machine said three hours."

"The machine was wrong. Kesse came early."

"I know. I felt it go." Her mouth moved. "Twelve. I felt them. I felt the bride."

> **I am sorry.**

"Don't be sorry. You're a kind machine. Be quick." She took a breath. It was shallow. "It knows I'm here. It's been pushing on me since ten o'clock. When it ate the first of my drowned at the south end, I felt them go, one by one, and there was nothing I could do, because if I let go of the sea to go and help them, it rises." Her thin hands trembled on the blanket. "How many are left on the river road?"

> **Four thousand. Most of them in the last trucks, on the narrow part, where the road goes along the river under the hills.**
>
> **They will be clear in two hours.**

"Then I'll sit here for two hours," said Ifeoma Adeyemi.

> **...you will not.**
>
> **I am sorry. I am counting. You have about forty minutes left.**

The old King was quiet.

"Well," she said. "Then count faster."

---

He counted with it.

He sat down on the gray planks at the foot of her wheelchair, with his back against the rusted base of the crane and the staff across his knees, and shut his eyes, and went down into the Field with Seed, through the held flat sea, to the bottom.

The heap. A mound of waking sand the size of a small town, at the south end of the long smear, glowing at every seam. The trunk going up out of the middle of it, four kilometers, capped, pressing. Roots going out from it through the sand in every direction like the roots of an old fig tree, thick as a man's body near the heap, thinning out into gray threads further away, reaching for the drowned of the south coast. Hundreds of them had already been eaten. Their embers had gone up the roots into the heap, into the trunk, into the push.

And at the edge of the heap, coming in out of the north, out of the dark, walking: Emeka.

He was alone. The others were still eight kilometers back, the four thousand drowned of Port Abiri and Kesse, walking south across the seabed as fast as the dead can walk in deep water. Emeka had gone on ahead. A fisherman with a lit knife, walking toward the heap on his own, the way a man walks toward a house where someone he loves is shouting.

> **He is going to the roots. On his own.**
>
> **He will burn.**

*Tell him to wait.*

> **I cannot tell him anything. No pipe. Ifeoma is holding the sea, not a pipe.**

---

*All right*, Yuanshu thought. *What do we know?*

> **The trunk is capped. Lingyue's light cannot get in at the top.**
>
> **The roots are open. But there are no drowned here to catch them. Only Emeka. The others are forty minutes away. That is how long Ifeoma has.**
>
> **The seed is under four hundred meters of sand, at the center of the heap.**
>
> **The trunk cannot rise, because she is holding the sea. If she lets go, it rises. It surfaces in about nine minutes and bites. Four thousand people on the river road. Probably most of them.**

*And if she doesn't let go, she dies holding it, in forty minutes, and then it rises anyway.*

> **...yes.**

He sat on the planks in the sun and held the staff and thought.

Not about the trunk. Seed was thinking about the trunk. Seed had been thinking about the trunk since ten o'clock, through four thousand runs, and every run ended the same way. He thought about what Ifeoma had said in the harbor master's office last night, with her finger on the chart. *You counted me as a pipe.*

And then about what she had said on the white boat, in the dark, on the first night out of Concordia, while Seed got it wrong.

*If I pushed the water behind the boat, I would have to push the whole sea. I ask the water in front of the boat to move out of the way. It is much less work.*

"Seed," he said. "She's doing it backwards."

> **...what?**

"She's holding the whole sea down so the trunk can't come up. That's pushing the water behind the boat. The whole sea." He opened his eyes. "What's in front of the boat?"

Seed was quiet for almost a second.

> **...the seed.**
>
> **The thing she wants to move is not the sea. It is the seed. It is the size of a house. It is under four hundred meters of sand.**
>
> **She does not have to hold three kilometers of water down on top of it.**
>
> **She has to ask four hundred meters of water-soaked sand to get out of the way of one stone.**

---

"Ifeoma," he said.

"I heard, child." Her eyes were still shut. Her voice was a thread. "I'm old. I'm not deaf." She was quiet for a moment. "You want me to let go of the sea."

"Yes."

"And when I let go, it comes up."

"Not if you take the seed out first." He knelt in front of her chair on the planks, so their faces were level. "Let go of the sea. All of it. Use everything you're using to hold it, and put it into the sand around the seed. Ask the sand to move. Just there. Just around the stone. Four hundred meters." He held her hands. They were very cold. "And then ask the water under the seed to rise. Not the whole sea. Just a column. Like the pipe. Straight up. And let it carry the seed up to us."

"Up," said Ifeoma Adeyemi.

"Out of the sand. Out of the heap. Away from its roots. Four kilometers up the outside of its own trunk, in a column of rising water, to the surface. Here." He looked at the flat blue sea off the end of the jetty. "Where we can reach it."

> **Without its roots it cannot eat. Without the heap it cannot hold. It is a stone the size of a house in a column of water, and it is coming up to a jetty where there is a girl with two swords, and Old Seven, and me.**
>
> **And when the seed leaves the heap, the trunk has nothing at its root. It comes apart.**
>
> **Running.**
>
> **Eighty-three percent.**
>
> **Yuanshu. For Ifeoma. I will not tell you that number. She has asked me not to. Just now. She is very polite about it.**

The old King opened her eyes.

They were very bright and very black, the way they had been on the harbor wall at Port Abiri a year ago, looking out at a spire in the bay.

"Pulling a weed," she said. "That's all it is. Pulling a weed out by the root." Her cold hands tightened on his. "My mother used to make me do it, in the garden. I hated it. I was eleven." She let go of his hands, and put her own flat on the blanket. "Stand back, child. Get your girl."

---

She let go of the sea.

All at once. Ten kilometers of held water came loose from under her hands like a breath let out, and the great flat sheet off Sandu broke into the afternoon swell, and the deep cold water at the foot of the shelf, held down for seven hours, surged upward. He felt the trunk feel it. Four kilometers of capped gray crystal, suddenly free, beginning to rise.

And in the same instant, four kilometers down, at the center of the heap, Ifeoma Adeyemi asked four hundred meters of sand to get out of the way.

It did.

He felt it through the Field, the way you feel a door open in a dark house. Not dug. Not blasted. The sand around the seed simply moved aside, grain by grain, a long round shaft of it from the center of the heap to the seabed, opening, the way water opens in front of the bow of a boat. And at the bottom of the shaft, the seed. A stone the size of a house. Gray, cloudy, cracked with fine dark lines, glowing the color of a coal.

Then she asked the water under it to rise.

---

The seed came up out of the heap like a cork coming out of a bottle.

Its roots tore. All of them at once. Thousands of gray branches running out through the sand to the drowned of the south coast, snapping, one after another, as the stone they were rooted in lifted out of the ground on a column of rising water and went up, past the edge of the heap, past the base of its own trunk, up the outside of four kilometers of capped gray crystal that had just begun to rise toward the surface with nothing at its root.

The trunk came apart.

He felt it go. From the bottom. Sand. A tower of gray crystal four kilometers tall, one moment rising toward the light, the next moment pouring away into the dark in long soft clouds, with nothing left to hold it. And through the falling sand, the seed went up, carried on a pillar of water that the oldest King on Blue Star held straight from the bottom of the sea to the end of a jetty in the Southern Compact, faster and faster, toward the light.

> **Two kilometers.**
>
> **One.**
>
> **It knows. It is reaching. It has no roots left. It is reaching with what it has.**
>
> **Lingyue. Old Seven. Thirty seconds.**

Lingyue was at the end of the jetty, beside the crane, with both swords lit. Pale fire. Silver-gray. Old Seven stood beside her with his spear, and she touched her grandfather's blade to its rusted head, and the pale fire ran down the shaft into the one finger and the thumb that held it.

Mo Shi was on the crane, at the top, with his eyes shut. "*Four seconds early,*" he said. "*It's turning. It turns as it comes up. There's a seam on the left side. It comes round every... every one point one seconds.*"

> **I see it. Through his ears. Yes.**
>
> **Old Seven. The seam comes round at the surface at 15:41:07.3. You will have four tenths of a second.**
>
> **He says that is plenty.**

---

At 15:41:06, a hundred meters off the end of the jetty at Sandu, the sea stood up.

A column of water three meters across, rising straight out of the flat blue afternoon into the air, white at the edges, roaring. And riding the top of it, lifted out of the sea into the sunlight for the first time since it had been planted, a stone the size of a house, gray and cracked and glowing, turning slowly in the spray. Every crack in it was the color of a coal. Gray roots trailed from it like hair, torn and dripping, reaching blindly for anything warm.

Mo Shi said: "*Now.*"

Old Seven threw.

Not the way he had thrown at the Bridge-Breaker, or the spire at Port Abiri, or the faceless thing in the round chamber. With one finger and a thumb. He could not throw the old way any more; Hearth One had taken that. He threw the way a man throws who has learned, in a year, with what he had left: from the shoulder, with his whole body turning behind it, and the spear went off the end of the jetty in a long flat line of silver-gray fire across a hundred meters of air, and turned once, and went into the seam on the left side of the seed at 15:41:07.3 as it came round in the spray.

Lingyue's light went in after it. Both swords. Point to point. A single stream of pale fire, falling, not shining. Downhill. Into the crack the spear had opened.

The seed broke.

---

It did not crumble like the trunks. It broke like a stone breaks, in the air, into four pieces and then a hundred, and the hundred went to sand in the sunlight, gray and fine, and fell back into the column of water, and the column fell back into the sea, roaring, white, and spread out across the flat blue afternoon in a long low ring of foam that rolled in toward the jetty and broke against the wooden piles and was gone.

The sea off Sandu was the sea again.

> **[The Sandu trunk is dead.]**
>
> **[The sea seed is dead.]**
>
> **15:41:08.**
>
> **The river road is still being cleared. Nobody on it knows yet. They will hear it on the radio in a few minutes.**

Yuanshu turned around.

Ifeoma Adeyemi was sitting in her wheelchair at the end of the jetty, under the umbrella, with her eyes open. Looking at the sea. Her thin hands were lying open on the blanket. Her household men were on their knees on either side of her.

He went to her. He knelt down in front of her chair on the wet planks so that their faces were level.

"Ifeoma."

The oldest King on Blue Star did not answer. Her very bright black eyes were looking past him, out to sea, at the place past the point where the water turned dark. Her face was perfectly still.

> **Yuanshu.**
>
> **She is gone.**
>
> **She was gone before the seed reached the surface. Somewhere around one kilometer. I did not tell you, because you would have stopped, and she asked me not to let you stop.**
>
> **She held the column up to the surface after she was gone. I do not know how. I do not have a number for it.**
>
> **I think it was the last thing she asked the water for, and the water did not know she had stopped asking.**
