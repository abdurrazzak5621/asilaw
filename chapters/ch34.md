## Chapter 34: Deputy Director Gu

Gu Hanshan's office was full of plants.

That was the first thing anyone noticed about it, and the thing everyone at the Rift Research Institute mentioned when they talked about him. It was on the fourth floor, at the corner of the building, with two walls of windows facing the river, and there was hardly any office left in it. Ferns on the filing cabinets. Orchids on the windowsills, their roots hanging down in the air like gray string. A lemon tree in a ceramic pot in the corner, taller than a man, with three small green lemons on it. Climbing vines trained across the ceiling on wires, so that the light from the windows came down green and soft and moving, like light at the bottom of a pond.

It smelled of wet soil and leaves. The air was warm and damp. After the cold blue of the second sublevel, it was like walking into another country.

"Sit, sit," said Gu Hanshan. "Mind the fern. She bites."

---

He was in his late fifties. Silver hair, neatly cut. A soft gray cardigan with leather patches on the elbows, over a white shirt. Reading glasses on a cord around his neck. A kind, tired, intelligent face, the face of a favorite uncle or a well-loved teacher, with deep laugh lines at the corners of his eyes.

He made tea. He did it himself, in a clay pot, with water from a kettle on a hot plate between two pots of mint, and he talked while he did it, easily, the way people talk when they are used to putting nervous students at ease.

"I knew your mother for nineteen years," he said, pouring. "I was her senior when she joined. She corrected my equations in her first week, in front of the whole department, and she was right, and I've never forgiven her." He smiled, and the laugh lines deepened. "You have her habit of looking at people as if they were a problem on a whiteboard. It's not unpleasant. Just a little alarming."

He set a cup in front of Yuanshu. Another in front of Qin Hao, who did not touch it. A third in front of Director Lu, who sat by the lemon tree with his hands folded and his face very carefully empty.

Old Seven stood by the door. The vines on the ceiling swayed slightly over his skull, though there was no wind.

> **Law signature: Earth Law. 41%. Fragment-adjacent. He is very strong for a researcher.**
>
> **Heart rate: 64. Steady.**
>
> **He is not afraid of you. He is not surprised that you are here.**

---

Yuanshu put the ledger on the desk.

He opened it to the page he had marked with a strip of paper, and turned it around, and slid it across between the teacups so that Gu Hanshan could read it. A neat column of entries. *Halcyon Medical Supply. Deliver to: loading dock B.* And at the bottom of each receipt, in a slanting hand, a single letter.

*G.*

Gu Hanshan put on his reading glasses and looked at it for a long time.

Then he took them off, and let them fall on their cord against his cardigan, and sighed.

"Yes," he said. "That's mine."

Director Lu closed his eyes.

"Halcyon Medical Supply is a procurement company the Institute set up twelve years ago," said Gu, gently, to Lu as much as to anyone. "For buying equipment we didn't want our competitors to know about. It's all on the books. You signed the incorporation papers yourself, Zhengtian. You don't remember, because I put them in a stack of forty."

"You bought people," said Qin Hao.

"I bought people," Gu agreed. "Fourteen of them, over three years. From a market on the wrong side of the wall, where they were sitting on a bench with prices around their necks." He turned his teacup slowly on its saucer. "Would you like to know what would have happened to them if I hadn't?"

Nobody answered.

"Neither would I," said Gu Hanshan quietly. "I've never asked."

---

He took them to see.

The ward was on the third floor, in a wing Yuanshu had walked past twice without noticing. A plain white door with a keypad. Behind it, a long, quiet room with tall windows and the blinds half down, so that the afternoon sun came in in bright stripes across the floor.

Fourteen beds, in two rows. Clean white sheets. Monitors beeping softly. Drip stands. Two nurses in pale green moving between the beds, checking lines, turning pillows, talking to the patients in low ordinary voices, the way you talk to someone you've been told can't hear you and you don't quite believe it.

In every bed, a hollow.

They lay on their backs with their eyes open, looking at the ceiling. Breathing slowly, all at the same rhythm. Some had feeding tubes. One, a young woman with long braided hair, had a small vase of flowers on the table beside her bed, and a hairbrush, and a photograph of a family in front of a farmhouse.

"We feed them," said Gu, very softly, at the door. "We turn them so they don't get sores. We talk to them. We play music. Nothing helps. But nobody else would do even this much." He looked down the room. "I've been trying for three years to find a way to put something back inside them. I've failed every single day."

Qin Hao walked slowly down the row between the beds. He stopped at each one. He looked at each face. He was looking for green uniforms, Yuanshu knew. For burn scars and shaved heads. For a big man who laughed like a donkey.

None of them were from the Western Pass.

> **Fourth bed on the left.**

Yuanshu looked.

A thin boy. Very young. A narrow face, pale against the white pillow. Short fair hair. His hands lay on top of the sheet, long-fingered, very still. On the table beside his bed, somebody had left a folded paper crane.

> **Private Andrei Belov.**
>
> **Grid Nine.**
>
> **He is here.**

---

"That one came in three months ago," said Gu, following his look. "Northern. We don't know his name."

"Andrei Belov," said Yuanshu. "Nineteen. His mother said yes."

Gu Hanshan turned to look at him, and for a moment something moved behind the kind, tired eyes. Surprise, maybe. Or something like it.

"How remarkable," he said. "You'll have to tell me how you know that."

"Grid Nine," said Yuanshu. "The men who took him had a scanner. It beeped differently over him. Higher." He kept his eyes on the boy on the bed. "Why would it do that?"

"I've wondered the same thing." Gu walked down the row, slowly, and stopped at the foot of Belov's bed, and looked down at him with his hands in the pockets of his cardigan. "Most hollows are completely empty. Whatever took their minds took all of it. But a few, very few, one in a thousand perhaps, there's something left. A thread. A tiny residue, right at the bottom, the way there's always a little water left in a bucket you've emptied. Their scans are different. That's what made his reading high." He looked down at the boy. "I've been trying to reach that thread for three months."

> **He is telling the truth about the thread.**
>
> **I can feel it from here. Very faint. At the bottom. Like someone waving.**
>
> **Fat Tan was right. He is still in there.**

"I'd like to try," said Yuanshu.

Gu looked at him for a long moment.

"Of course," he said warmly. "Of course. Anything you can do. Whenever you like." He put his hand on Yuanshu's shoulder for a moment, lightly, kindly. "This is what your mother would have wanted. She always said the two of you would be the ones to solve it."

---

They walked back up through the green, swaying light of his office to say goodbye.

At the door, with Old Seven already in the corridor and Qin Hao waiting stiffly at the elevator, Yuanshu stopped.

"Deputy Director," he said. "One more thing. The weather mast on the roof."

Gu Hanshan had sat back down behind his desk among the ferns. He looked up, reading glasses back on their cord, eyebrows raised mildly.

"The one that melted? Terrible thing. Lu told me." He shook his head. "Eleven years old, that mast. I had it put up myself, actually." He smiled, and the laugh lines came back. "For your mother. She wanted wind data from the north. She was always asking about the north."

He picked up his teacup.

"Is that all?"

"That's all," said Yuanshu. "Thank you for the tea."

He went out. The door clicked shut behind him, and the green light and the smell of soil and the gentle tired face were gone, and he stood for a moment in the plain gray corridor, breathing.

> **I need to tell you something.**
>
> **Everything he said was true. I checked every sentence against everything I could reach. The company. The ward. The mast. Your mother. All true.**
>
> **And there is something in his law signature I cannot classify.**
>
> **Earth Law, 41%. And under it, very thin, a band. One percent. It does not match any of the three thousand Great Laws.**
>
> **It might be noise.**
>
> **It might be an old injury. A scar from a rift.**
>
> **Or it might be the same thing as the transmitter on the roof. The same thing as the scout under the floor.**
>
> **It is too small to be sure.**
>
> **I do not like that it is exactly the right size to be too small to be sure.**

At the elevator, Qin Hao was holding the doors open with his foot.

"Well?" he said.

"He's helping," said Yuanshu. "He bought fourteen people off a bench and put them in clean beds, and he's been trying to save them for three years, and he's the kindest man I've met in this building." He stepped into the elevator. "And I don't trust him at all."

Qin Hao let the doors close.

"Good," he said. "Neither does your skeleton. He growled at the lemon tree."
