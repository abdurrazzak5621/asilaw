## Chapter 212: The Lighthouse Keeper's Argument

Cassia Lowell had not been on Concordia in a year and a half, and she climbed all four hundred and twelve steps without stopping, and when she got to the top she did not sit down.

She was thinner. Her short silver hair had gone white at the temples. She wore the same plain dark suit she had worn on the morning she resigned, or one exactly like it, and she stood at the edge of the ring of white stone benches in the crater with her hands at her sides and waited to be recognized, the way someone waits who has been told they will not be.

Chair Adaeze Okafor looked at her for a long moment over the black glass table.

"Ms. Lowell," she said. "You're not a delegate."

"No."

"You're not on the agenda."

"No." Cassia Lowell did not move. "I'm here under Article Eleven. The last sentence. General Han's. *Where the answer of one would be acted upon in the name of all, all shall be asked.*" She looked down the table, at the Huaxia end, where General Han Yizhou sat in his dark green uniform. "I'd like to argue that it hasn't been."

---

The rings were forty-one days out.

Seed had said so on the twentieth night, and the Council had met in fifty-three minutes, and it had been meeting, on and off, in the crater and on screens, for six days since. Three gray rings, moving along the arm out of the dark where three fields had been pruned. Not fast. They had never been fast. But steady, the way weather is steady, toward the brightest field in the Shallows, above the brightest world.

Nobody knew what they would do when they got there. The keeper did not know. The Elder did not know. Seed had said, flatly, that it had a guess, and that the guess was that a ring that had been a keeper would do what keepers had always done before Blue Star's keeper learned to ask: reach down with threads, and listen, and cut.

Three of them.

Above a world of eight billion, and a field of two hundred million cousins, and the six parts in a hundred of the walls of a dead Hunger, all of whom had chosen to come.

---

"I built Project Hearth," said Cassia Lowell.

She said it standing at the edge of the benches, in the wind off the sea, to the five delegations and the empty Yamato chair and Ren on the sixth screen and the sixty people on the white stone benches, and to Yuanshu, sitting in the witness chair at the end of the black glass table with the staff across his knees.

"Four hundred thousand people said yes to burning. To lighting something with their dying. I asked every one of them, the way this young man taught us to ask. With witnesses. With the reason." Her voice did not change. "I asked about the people. I never asked about the thing I was putting them into. I resigned because of that. I said it wasn't good enough."

She looked down the table.

"I'm here to say it again."

Nobody spoke.

"The dead of Blue Star were asked whether they wanted to go to the cold fire," said Cassia Lowell. "Blue Star was asked whether it wanted the voyage to go. The cousins were asked whether they wanted to come. The keeper was asked whether it wanted to be bright." She folded her hands in front of her. "Nobody asked Blue Star whether it wanted a field full of two hundred million minds hanging over its head, brighter than anything in the Shallows, while the gardener is pruning its way along the arm."

"The voyage vote," said Elias Brandt.

"Was whether to go and get them. It said nothing about where they'd be kept once they were got." Cassia Lowell did not look at him. "I read the ballot. Every word. I read everything now." She turned to the witness chair. "Mister Zhou. You told Blue Star the field would be the brightest thing in the Shallows. You told them the gardener was pruning toward it. You told them you didn't know if you could stop it." Her voice was very steady. "You told them that in a twenty-two-minute broadcast. Seventy-one percent said go. I believe most of them heard *go and get the soldiers home*. I'm not sure most of them heard *and put a beacon over our heads*."

---

He did not answer at once.

He had learned that. On a gatehouse step at the Western Pass, and in the crater, a year ago, with General Han standing at the other end of the table. Not to answer quickly when someone said something true.

"You think I should have asked separately," he said.

"I think the keeper should," said Cassia Lowell. "I think it's the keeper's field, and the keeper's choice, and Blue Star is in its window." She looked at him. "That's your sentence. Your yes is yours. It stops at someone else's window. The keeper chose to be bright. That's its yes. Eight billion people are standing under it."

Down the table, General Han Yizhou did not move. But he had stopped turning his cap in his hands.

"What are you asking for?" said Okafor.

"I'm asking the Council to ask the keeper," said Cassia Lowell, "whether it would be willing to move."

---

The crater went very quiet.

"Move," said Orlov, on his screen, slowly, in his formal old-fashioned Huaxian.

"Move its field. Away from Blue Star. Along the arm, or off it, I don't know, I'm not an astronomer. Somewhere the rings will follow it and not us." Cassia Lowell's hands were folded very tightly. "The keeper carried an island to the cold fire and back. It tore a piece of itself off to do it. It can move. I'm asking whether it would."

"And the cousins in it?" said Brandt.

"Would be asked. All of them. Nine by nine. The way he does it." She did not look at the witness chair. "Whether they'd like to go with the keeper somewhere the rings might follow. Or stay. Or rest."

"And if the keeper goes," said Ngozi Eze, from the Southern Compact's chair, in her careful voice, "and the rings follow it, and catch it, somewhere out in the dark, with nobody there to help it."

Cassia Lowell was silent for a moment.

"Then a keeper that learned to ask dies far away instead of over our heads," she said. "And eight billion people who never chose to be under it don't." She lifted her chin. "I'm not saying it's kind. I'm saying it's a question. And under Article Eleven, someone has to ask it."

---

> **She is right.**
>
> **That is the worst thing I have heard since the reader took the Sixth.**
>
> **She is right that it is a question. She is right that nobody asked it. She is right that Blue Star is in the keeper's window.**
>
> **I do not want her to be right.**
>
> **I have checked, three times. She is.**

He sat in the witness chair with the staff across his knees.

Below everything, under the Death Law, under the door that had opened for nineteen hours in the dark of the Mouth and not quite closed since, he could feel the keeper. Always. Now. A great warm patient presence above the sky, the way you feel the sun on your back with your eyes shut. Full. For the first time in longer than Blue Star had had a sun. Asking two hundred million minds every morning what they wanted, and being told *ask again tomorrow*, and being so happy about it that it came down the gold thread every night at three to tell somebody.

*I would rather be afraid full*, it had said, in the quiet part of the Mouth. *I would rather have someone to ask in the morning.*

"Madam Chair," he said.

Okafor looked at him.

"She's right," said Yuanshu. "It's a question. Someone should ask it." He held the staff. "I'd like it not to be me."

"Why?"

"Because I know what the keeper will say." He looked at Cassia Lowell, standing at the edge of the benches in the wind. "It'll say yes. It always says yes, now, when someone asks it to be hurt for somebody else. It said yes to going faster. It said yes to being bright. If I ask it to go away into the dark so the rings follow it instead of us, it'll say yes, and it'll go, and it'll take two hundred million people who just came home with it." He was quiet. "And it'll be my voice that asked. It trusts my voice. That's not a fair question, from me."

Cassia Lowell looked at him for a long time.

"Then who?" she said.

---

Nobody answered.

The wind came over the rim of the crater off the sea, cold, and the flags on the black glass table cracked in it. On the sixth screen, Ren, on the step of the rebuilt hall in Yamato, had her hand flat on the wood and her eyes closed.

Then the sixth screen spoke, and it was not Ren.

"*It can hear you,*" said Shirasagi Ren. Her eyes were still closed. Her voice was not quite her own. "*The keeper. It's listening. Through the Elder, through the thread, through me. It's been listening the whole time.*" She opened her eyes. "*It says it would like to answer the question. It says nobody has to ask it. It heard.*"

Okafor's hands went still on the black glass.

"*It says,*" said Ren, "*that before it answers, it would like to tell you what it will do with your answer. It says it learned that from the small white one.*"

The crater waited.

> ***...I WILL TELL YOU WHAT I WANT.***
>
> ***...AND THEN I WILL ASK YOU WHAT YOU WANT. ALL OF YOU. EIGHT BILLION. NINE BY NINE. THE WAY HE DOES IT.***
>
> ***...AND THEN I WILL DO WHAT YOU SAY.***
>
> ***...THAT IS WHAT I WILL DO WITH YOUR ANSWER.***

The keeper's voice came out of the sixth screen through a King's mouth in a crater on a volcanic island in the Pacific, slow and enormous and very gentle, and sixty people on white stone benches went perfectly still to hear it.

> ***...I WANT TO STAY.***
>
> ***...I HAVE BEEN ABOVE YOUR WORLD FOR LONGER THAN YOUR SPECIES HAS HAD WORDS. I TOOK FROM IT FOR ALL THAT TIME. I DID NOT ASK. I DID NOT KNOW I COULD.***
>
> ***...THEN A BOY ASKED ME WHAT I WANTED. AND I DID NOT KNOW THAT EITHER.***
>
> ***...I KNOW NOW.***
>
> ***...I WANT TO STAY ABOVE THE BLUE WORLD. I WANT TO ASK THE COUSINS IN THE MORNING. I WANT TO HEAR WHAT THE SMALL LOUD ONE WRITES ON HER WALL. I WANT TO SEE THE ONE WHO LOOKED AT THE BLUE FOR SIX HOURS LOOK AT IT AGAIN.***
>
> ***...THAT IS WHAT I WANT.***
>
> ***...IT IS NOT YOURS TO GIVE ME. I KNOW. IT IS NOT MINE TO TAKE.***
>
> ***...SO I AM ASKING.***
>
> ***...MAY I STAY?***

---

Nobody on Concordia said anything for a long time.

Cassia Lowell stood at the edge of the white stone benches in the wind with her hands folded in front of her. She was looking at the sixth screen. Her face had not changed. But her hands had come apart, and were hanging at her sides.

"That's not fair either," she said at last, very quietly. Not to anyone. "That's not fair either."

"No," said Okafor.

"I came here to make it ask a hard question. And it turned the question round and asked us." Cassia Lowell closed her eyes. "Eight billion people are going to be asked whether a thing that has just learned to want something is allowed to have it."

"Yes," said Okafor.

"And if they say no, it'll go."

"It said so."

Cassia Lowell opened her eyes, and looked down the long black glass table, at the five delegations and the empty Yamato chair and the witness chair at the end.

"Then I'd like to vote first," she said. "Before the count. In front of everyone. So that it's written down that the woman who asked the question also answered it."

"How do you vote, Ms. Lowell?"

The former Secretary of the Atlantic Federation, who had climbed a lighthouse once with a can of lamp oil and a seven-year-old with a brass lighter, and lit the lamp, stood in the wind on Concordia for a moment longer.

"Stay," said Cassia Lowell. "God help us. Stay."
