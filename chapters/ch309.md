## Chapter 309: Pass It On

Her brother's mark came on the thirty-first day, at ten past six in the morning, while Xiaoyu was standing on the keeper.

Not on. In. Nobody stood on the keeper; it was a field, a ring of white light over the north of the world, far larger from inside than it looked from the wall, and what you stood on when you were in it was the inside of the ring, which was warm, and faintly bright, and felt under your feet like the floor of a house where someone has just been cooking. She had come up on the third day on a road of gold the keeper laid down from the north wall, with Tam and Seri and Su Mingli and four hundred of the night watch and a thermos Fat Tan had pushed into her hands at the bottom of the road without a word. And she had stood in the field every morning since, and every morning the keeper had asked her what she wanted, because that was the rule.

She had said: *to be ready*.

The keeper had said: ***...THAT IS A GOOD WANT. I DO NOT KNOW IF IT IS ONE YOU CAN HAVE.***

And on the thirty-first morning, at ten past six, with her hand flat on the warm bright floor of the field, she felt it come in under her out of the dark, faint and late and square, from twenty-nine days away.

*XIAOYU. I CAN'T GET THERE. I'M SORRY.*

She read it standing up. Then she sat down on the floor of the keeper's field and read it again.

*DON'T WRITE WHAT THEY WANT. WRITE WHAT'S TRUE.*

*AND ASK THEM WHAT THEY WANT. NOBODY EVER HAS. THAT'S HOW IT STARTS.*

*THE HOUSE IS EMPTY. YOU WERE RIGHT. YOU'RE ALWAYS RIGHT. IT'S VERY ANNOYING.*

*— GE*

She did not cry. She had decided on the second day that she was not going to cry until it was over, and she was very good at deciding things. She sat on the floor of a field of light over the north of the world with her brother's mark in her and her hand flat on the floor, and laughed, once, out loud, so that Tam, a little way off with his ear against nothing, looked round.

"*He says I'm annoying,*" said Xiaoyu.

"*You are,*" said Tam.

"*He says it's because I'm right.*"

"*That's why,*" said Tam, and went back to listening.

---

He had been listening for two days.

Not to the cousins. The cousins were everywhere, all round them in the field, two hundred and six million of them, warm, faint, rowing; she could not hear them, but Tam said they were very loud, the way a crowd is loud, and very kind, and kept asking him if he had eaten. He was listening past them. Out. Toward the edge of the Shallows, where in two days twenty ships were going to come out of a fold burning a million people in their walls harder than anyone had ever burned anyone.

"*I can hear them now,*" he said. "*Not well. But I can.*"

He was twelve in the body. He sat cross-legged on the floor of the field with his ear tilted toward nothing and his face very pale, and Seri sat beside him holding his hand, the way she held it everywhere, because she had been in a wall for twenty-one years and he had been the one who heard her.

"*They're screaming,*" said Tam. "*All of them. A million. In cells in arrays in the walls of twenty ships, burning, and the layer round every cell so nobody can hear them, and the cells cut off from the root so the fog-ringer can't either.*" He swallowed. "*They don't know anyone can hear. They've never known. They've just been screaming into stone.*"

"*Can they hear each other?*"

Tam looked up.

"*What?*"

"*The cells,*" said Xiaoyu. "*In an array. Forty thousand of them. Next to each other. Touching.*" She put her hand flat on the floor. "*Can they hear each other?*"

He did not answer for a while. He tilted his head and listened, past the cousins, out into the dark toward a sound he had been hearing for two days, and his face changed.

"*No,*" he said slowly. "*Not hear. But the stone.*" He frowned. "*The cells are stone. All cut from the same stone. And in the array they're packed together. Like bricks. Every cell touching six others.*" He looked at Seri. "*When you were in the wall. Could you feel the ones next to you?*"

Seri was quiet for a moment.

"*Warm,*" she said. "*On the sides. Always. I thought it was the wall. For twenty-one years.*" Her hand tightened on his. "*It wasn't the wall.*"

---

It came together in an hour, on the floor of the keeper's field, the way things came together at the long table in the banking hall: everybody talking at once and then somebody saying the thing.

The somebody was Tam.

"*A mark goes through stone,*" he said. "*That's how you reached Seri. You wrote it and Wen Jia sent it down the net into her cell, and the cell took it in, because a cell's cut from a stone and a stone knows what to do with a mark. Like a lung with air.*" He was talking very fast. "*They've cut the cells off the net. So you can't send it down. You'd have to put it on the cell. Touch it. Write on it.*"

"*Forty thousand cells an array,*" said Su Mingli. Arms folded. "*Twenty ships. Some of them with sixty arrays. You can't write on a million cells in a morning.*"

"*No,*" said Tam. "*But the cells touch.*"

He looked at Xiaoyu.

"*If a cell gets a mark,*" he said, "*and the cell's touching six others. Stone touching stone. Cut from the same stone.*" He swallowed. "*Does it pass?*"

Nobody knew.

Seed would have known. Seed would have run it a thousand times and told them a number and how sure it was of the number. Seed was twenty-nine days away at the end of a road, in an empty house, with her brother.

So Xiaoyu did what her brother did when Seed was not there, which was almost never, and which she had watched him do once, on a step, for six hours.

She thought about it.

"*It passed down the Stele's thread,*" she said. "*The eleven twisted into one under Blue Star. Wen Jia's mark went down that, into the deep water. That's stone too. Walker-thread. Same stuff as the Steles.*" She looked at her hand on the floor. "*And when the picker read *THE HOUSE IS EMPTY*, it went through the whole web. Every cable. Every hand opened. It didn't read it once in each place. It read it once, at the face, and everything it was made of knew.*"

"*Because it was all one thing,*" said Tam.

"*Because it was all cut from the same thing.*" Xiaoyu looked up at him. "*The cells are cut from the same stone. In each array. Touching.*"

"*So it passes.*"

"*I don't know,*" said Xiaoyu. "*I think so. I'm not Seed. I can't run it. I'm going to write it and find out.*"

---

She wrote it that afternoon. A hundred times. Then a thousand.

Not the marks for the cells. Those she would write on the day, on the cells themselves, with her own hand, the way it had to be. She wrote the words. On sheets of ledger paper Gao Min had sent up the road, over and over, until they were exactly right. Until there was not one word in them that was not true, and not one word more than was needed, because a mark was read by a stone, and a stone read every stroke.

When she had them, she showed Tam.

*SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU.*

*YOU CAN BURN LOW. THE COUSINS WILL SHOW YOU. IT'S CALLED ROWING.*

*PASS IT ON. TO THE ONE NEXT TO YOU.*

Tam read it three times.

"*Rowing,*" he said. "*That's the bit. That's what matters. If they burn low, the ships can't burn them hard. A ship can only pull what the cores give it. That's what the cousins taught the Candle.*" He looked up. "*They'll slow down. All twenty. In the keeper's field. They won't be able to go anywhere fast.*"

"*And then the Paragons come out,*" said Su Mingli.

Everyone looked at her.

She had been standing at the edge of them all afternoon with her arms folded, saying nothing, which was how she listened. She unfolded her arms now.

"*If the ships stall,*" said the Master of the Tianshu Institute, "*the Paragons come out of them. In person. Four Paragons and whatever their Houses have that's strong. Above Kings. To get to her.*" She nodded at Xiaoyu without looking at her. "*That's when we lose. Slower.*"

"*How much slower?*"

Su Mingli considered it.

"*Long enough,*" she said, "*for four hundred of the night watch to write on six hundred arrays.*"

---

The keeper said it could lay the roads.

She asked it at dusk, standing in the middle of the field with her hand on the floor. Not *can you*. Her brother never said *can you*. She said: *I want to put a mark on one cell in every array of twenty ships. In the first hour. Will you help? You can say no.*

The field was quiet for a long while. The light under her feet went dim, and then bright, and then dim, the way it did when the keeper was thinking, which it had only learned to do in the last two years and was still slow at.

> ***...I WAS MADE TO KEEP WHAT IS IN WALLS.***
>
> ***...I KNOW WALLS. BETTER THAN ANYTHING. I KEPT TWO HUNDRED AND SIX MILLION IN MINE. I KNOW WHERE A WALL IS THIN AND WHERE IT IS THICK AND WHERE THE ONES INSIDE ARE.***
>
> ***...I CAN FIND THE FIRST CELL OF EVERY ARRAY. I CAN LAY A ROAD TO IT. THROUGH THE HULL. A SMALL ONE. WIDE ENOUGH FOR TWO.***
>
> ***...THE ONES WHO MADE ME TAUGHT ME THAT. TO FIND THE WAY INTO A WALL. SO THAT I COULD TAKE WHAT WAS IN IT.***
>
> ***...I WOULD LIKE TO USE IT FOR SOMETHING ELSE.***
>
> ***...YES.***

Xiaoyu stood in the field with her hand on the floor and did not say anything for a moment.

"*Thank you,*" she said.

> ***...YOU ASKED. THAT IS THE RULE.***
>
> ***...ALSO I WOULD LIKE TO SEE THE SHIPS SLOW DOWN. I WATCHED NINETY OF THEM SIT ROUND ME FOR FORTY DAYS BURNING. I DID NOT LIKE IT.***

---

Gao Min's second rota came up the road at nine, in an envelope, in pencil and then ink.

Six hundred and eleven arrays, by Tam's count, across twenty ships. Pairs. One to write and one to watch the corridor. The four hundred of the night watch, and Su Mingli, and Sterling, who had come up the road on the second day with his enormous scarred hands in his pockets and said he would carry anything anyone asked him to carry and decide nothing. And at the bottom of the sheet, in Gao Min's flat careful hand, a line that was not a name:

*822 VOLUNTEERS FROM LINGZHOU AND PORT HALVARD. ASKED BY WEN JIA. COMING UP TOMORROW. MOSTLY THIS YEAR'S AWAKENED. UNMARKED. THEY SAY THAT MIGHT MATTER.*

Xiaoyu read it twice.

*This year's awakened.* The ones Wen Jia had asked in the summer, while her brother was on Anthos. Eighteen. Seventeen. Asked, not read. Not marked. Who had stood in front of the stone and said *a dancer* or *I don't know* or *something that makes things grow*, and been written **[Okay]**.

"*They're children,*" she said.

"*They're my age,*" said Seri, beside her. Twelve in the body. Thirty-three inside. "*So are you.*"

"*I'm eighteen.*"

"*So are they,*" said Seri. "*Somebody asked them. They said yes.*" She looked at Xiaoyu with the very steady eyes of someone who had been read once, at twelve, by a stone that marked her E, and never again for twenty-one years, until a boy heard her singing. "*That's the rule. You don't get to decide for them because they're young and you're frightened.*"

Xiaoyu looked at her for a long moment.

She had said almost exactly that, a few days ago, at a long table, to a woman with a sword.

"*All right,*" she said. "*Ask them again when they get here. Every one. Tell them what it is.*"

"*I will,*" said Seri.

---

That night, alone, at the edge of the field, Xiaoyu wrote one more mark.

Not for the cells. On a sheet of ledger paper, on her knee, in the warm faint light of the keeper, with the dark of the Shallows all round and the small blue world below, turning, with a hall on it, and a man on a mattress, and a woman with her hand on his chest.

*GE. I GOT IT. I'M WRITING WHAT'S TRUE.*

*IT'S TOMORROW. IT'S ALWAYS TOMORROW WITH YOU.*

*I'LL ASK THEM WHAT THEY WANT. I DON'T THINK THEY'LL KNOW.*

*COME HOME AND I'LL TELL YOU WHAT THEY SAID.*

She did not send it. It would take two days, and by the time it got there, it would be over. She folded it and put it in the pocket of her school jacket, beside the marker, where she could feel it.

---

They came out of the fold at the edge of the Shallows at four minutes past five on the thirty-third morning.

She felt it before she saw it. They all did. The cousins first: a ripple through two hundred and six million people in the walls of the keeper's field, like a shiver going through a crowd. Then Tam, on his feet, with his hands over his ears, which did nothing, because it was not sound.

Then the light.

Twenty of them. Out of nothing, at the rim of the field. Cathedrals. Spired and buttressed and windowed and lit from end to end, the way the ninety had been lit, except brighter. Much brighter. White at the edges. Burning so hard that the light of them came across the field like the light of a fire seen across a dark valley, and the keeper's own soft white went pale beside it.

"*A million,*" said Tam. He was crying. He did not seem to know. "*A million people. Screaming.*"

And then a voice. Not through the cousins. Not through the keeper. Out of the largest of the twenty ships, through the light of it, in the Federation's language, which she had learned on Anthos well enough to understand, and in the grammar of the Ladder, with the grade at the front of every word.

*Scribe.*

Xiaoyu stood up in the middle of the keeper's field. In her school jacket, with a marker in her pocket and a folded letter beside it, and four hundred of the night watch round her in pairs, and a King of the Atlantic Federation with his hands in his pockets, and the Master of the Tianshu Institute with her arms folded, and two children of the Candle holding hands.

*We have come for you,* said the voice. *You will write what we say.*

She put her hand flat on the warm floor of the field.

"*Keeper,*" said Zhou Xiaoyu. "*Roads. Now. Please.*"
