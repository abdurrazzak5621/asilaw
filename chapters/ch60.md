## Chapter 60: The Last Page

At midnight, the Concordia Consent Charter came into force, and nobody on the island was asleep to see it.

They had gathered on the rim of the crater without anyone arranging it. Delegates and Kings and Council staff and journalists, in coats and blankets, sitting on the black rock in the cold wind with the sea far below them, silver under a half moon. Nobody made a speech. At one minute to twelve, Chair Adaeze Okafor stood up from the rock where she had been sitting with a thermos of tea, and looked at her watch, and when the minute hand touched the top, she said, quietly, "There."

That was all.

Then three hundred people looked up at the sky, all together, because they couldn't help it.

It was full of stars. Only stars. Ordinary, cold, uncountable, white. No ring. No eye. No black water. The Milky Way spilled across it from horizon to horizon like dust on a dark table.

Somebody let out a breath. Then everybody did.

---

Chair Okafor found him at the eastern end of the rim, sitting beside the small wooden palanquin, where the eight men in white robes had set it down facing the sea.

She sat down beside him on the rock, slowly, with a small grunt, the way old people sit.

"The Atlantic Federation will sign in the morning," she said, without preamble. "All five. Voss told me an hour ago. Then he resigned." She unscrewed her thermos. "He's taking Garrett Hale home. To his family. He said he'll find a Quiet chapter in the Federation capital, if there is one. If there isn't, he'll start one." She poured tea into the cap. "I believed him."

"So did I," said Yuanshu.

"Meridian's board has been arrested. Doctor Kwan has asked for asylum in Huaxia. Your Director Lu has given it to her, in writing, on the back of a Council napkin." She sipped. "Seven nations have asked to send observers to your Ossuary. Four have asked for the Quiet to train their hospital staff. And the Bureau has issued an international warrant for Gu Hanshan, Deputy Director, for the trafficking of persons." She paused. "And for one Julian Mercer, who does not exist."

She held out the cap of tea.

He took it. It was jasmine.

"I've been a judge for thirty-one years," said Adaeze Okafor, looking out at the moon on the sea. "And a Chair for nine. And I've learned that the day a good law passes is the most dangerous day there is. Because everyone goes home thinking it's finished." She took the cap back and drank. "It isn't. It's a piece of paper. It's only real if somebody keeps asking."

"I know," said Yuanshu.

"I know you do." She stood up, slowly, and looked down at him. "That's why I voted for it."

---

The *Long Patience* sailed at dawn.

The nine new hollows went with her, in the old hospital ward, on nine of the forty iron beds. Sister Ning's twelve sat beside them in silence, in shifts, all the way home. Harlan Voss had asked, at the gangway, whether Garrett Hale could come to Lingzhou instead of the Federation. To the teahouse. Where people knew how.

Sister Ning had looked at him for a long time.

"Yes," she'd said.

So ten went west. And on the third night out, Yuanshu went down to the ward and sat on the floor at the end of the row, out of the way, thinking about rice, and watched a former Secretary of the Atlantic Federation sit on a cushion beside his own lawyer's bed with a candle in front of him and his eyes closed, trying, very badly, to think about nothing.

He was not very good at it. Nobody was, at first.

He came back the next night anyway.

---

Seed counted on the fourth night.

> **The pulse.**
>
> **From the north. The big lens. The real one.**
>
> **I have been waiting to be sure.**

Yuanshu was on the afterdeck, in the dark, alone, with the wake hissing white behind the ship.

*And?*

> **It slowed.**
>
> **Before Concordia, the interval was shrinking. 31 hours. 30 hours. 29.**
>
> **Since the eye closed, it has gone back up. 32 hours. 34. 37.**
>
> **It is not hurrying anymore.**
>
> **Something up there is busy.**

He gripped the rail.

*Her.*

> **I think so.**
>
> **She did something very loud, in the middle of its field. And it turned to look at her. And it has not turned back.**
>
> **I cannot draw a straight line through these numbers. They are not straight. But if they hold...**
>
> **Four hundred and ten days was the old number. It is longer now. Perhaps much longer. Perhaps only a little.**
>
> **She bought us time, Yuanshu.**
>
> **I do not know what it is costing her.**

He stood at the rail for a long time, looking north, at the dark.

---

Lingzhou harbor was full of candles.

They came in on a gray afternoon, in the drizzle, and the whole naval pier was lined with them again, ten thousand, twenty thousand, more than when they had left. But this time they were lit. Every one. Thousands of small gold flames under umbrellas and plastic bags and newspapers, flickering in the wet wind along the whole length of the harbor wall, and not a sound from any of the people holding them.

At the foot of the gangway stood Xiaoyu, in his old hoodie, with no candle at all. She had both hands jammed in the front pocket. Her face was set in a scowl so fierce that he knew she had been practicing it for a week.

He came down the gangway with Old Seven behind him.

She took one hand out of the pocket. In it was a green flask, dented, with a crack across its lid, mended with a strip of white tape.

"I found it," she said. "At the back of the cupboard. Where I hid it." She held it out. Her scowl was not holding. "I made you tea. It's probably cold. You skipped breakfast. Don't lie, I can tell."

He took it.

It was cold. It was jasmine.

He drank all of it, standing at the bottom of the gangway in the rain, with twenty thousand candles burning along the harbor wall, while his sister cried into his shoulder and hit him, not very hard, over and over, on the arm.

---

That night, in the vault under the Ossuary, he read the last page.

He did it the way he did everything now. With a witness. Sister Ning sat on the other side of the steel table with her candle lit. Old Seven stood by the round steel door. Nobody else. No phone. No windows. Nobody who might be listening through anybody.

The black leather notebook lay open on the table between them, at the very back. The last page was blank. Cream-colored, slightly foxed at the edges, completely empty.

He went quiet. All the way down. Then he laid his palm flat on the empty page, and opened the Death Law under it, very gently, like opening a door into a cool dark room.

Letters came up out of the paper.

Gray. Faint. Like frost forming on a window. In the small, quick, perfectly neat hand of a man who had been trained to write reports under fire, and had written this one while he was dying.

---

*To whoever comes after me.*

*If you can read this, you are like me. I'm sorry.*

*I have been thinking about the faceless one. About why it fell, when we raised the dead. Not because the dead were strong. Because the dead were quiet. It could not see them. They walked right up to it and it never once turned its head.*

*I think the dead are the only things in the world it cannot hear.*

*I made a staff, at the end. From the bones of the first one I ever raised, who said yes. It is buried with me. It is not a weapon. It is a key. It opens the way the threads come down. From this side.*

*Whoever holds it can walk up the thread into the place where they are kept. Awake. Unseen. As quiet as the dead.*

*And whoever holds it can open that place wider. From this side. All the way.*

*I could not decide which it was for. I was too tired, and too sorry, and there were too many dead I had never asked. So I buried it with me, and I am leaving it to you.*

*Ask it what it wants. Ask the bones. Ask before you touch it.*

*And do not let it be taken by anyone who would not.*

*— L. X.*

---

The letters faded back into the paper, slowly, like frost going off a window in the sun.

The vault was silent. Sister Ning's candle burned straight.

"Gu has a head start," said Yuanshu at last. His voice was very quiet. "Mercer has a head start. They know where the grave is. They don't know what the staff does. That's why he wanted me to read this to him." He closed the notebook. "Now I know. And they don't."

"And now," said Sister Ning, "you have to get there first."

"Yes."

"Into the Wastes."

"Yes."

The old woman looked at him across the steel table for a long time, in the candlelight.

"Then you had better go and tell Su Mingli," she said, "that *after the Charter* is now."

---

Su Mingli was on the wall.

He found her where he had known she would be, on the battlements of the Academy section at midnight, looking north, with the wind off the Wastes tugging at her coat. Lingyue was there too, a little way along, sitting on the parapet with her sword across her knees. And the Mo twins, arguing in whispers. And Bai Lu, with a medical bag that was much too big for her. And Qin Hao, in his dark green uniform, with his grandfather's warrant folded in his breast pocket. And three dead soldiers: a skeleton with a spear, an empty shield, and a shape of ash with an iron arrowhead for a heart.

Nobody had told any of them to come.

Su Mingli did not turn around.

"Thirty days," she said. "Supplies. Maps. Guides who've been past Grid Nine and come back. A route that doesn't go anywhere near anything with a thread in it." She was silent for a moment. "We walk in. On our feet."

"Like Mama would have wanted," said Yuanshu.

"Like your mother would have wanted." Su Mingli turned her head, finally, and looked at him. In the starlight her face was calm and very tired and, for the first time since he had known her, something that might almost have been hopeful. "And then we go and get her back."

Down the wall, Lingyue stood up off the parapet and slid her sword into its sheath with a soft click.

"After the north," she said, to nobody in particular, looking out at the dark, "I'll tell you what I chose."

The wind came down off the Wastes, cold and clean and smelling of snow, and far away in the north, past the black glass and the frozen ridges and the last outpost on the edge of the world, beyond the place where the trucks had gone, a lens hung in the sky over the end of everything, listening.

But it was not listening to them.

Not tonight.
