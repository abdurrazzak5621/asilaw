## Chapter 28: Sister Ning's Terms

The Quiet had no temples.

They met where they could. In the back rooms of noodle shops after closing. In a disused bus depot in the south of the city. And, when there were too many of them for anywhere else, under the Old Ferry Bridge, where the river ran wide and slow and brown and the great concrete arch of the bridge made a roof that kept off the rain.

On the night they decided, there were three thousand of them under the bridge.

From the riverbank above, it looked like a field of stars had come down to rest on the water's edge. Three thousand paper-cup candles on the gravel and the concrete and the steps, in rows, in clusters, in long wavering lines down to the water. The light ran up the underside of the arch and moved there, gold and restless. The traffic on the bridge above passed in a steady muffled roar that nobody below seemed to hear.

Nobody had invited Yuanshu.

He came anyway, and stood at the top of the concrete steps with his hood up, and nobody stopped him.

---

Sister Ning sat on her folding stool at the bottom of the steps, by the water. There was no stage. There was no microphone. When she spoke, the people nearest her went quiet, and then the people behind them, and the quiet rolled back through the crowd like a wave going the wrong way up a beach, until there were three thousand people listening to one small old woman with cropped white hair.

"I've been asked to tell you what I saw," she said.

She told them.

The cracked stone in the named section, and the cold under her candle, and then the warmth. The hangar, and forty frozen mouths closing. The cold room on the second sublevel, and a candle flame leaning toward a crystal the size of a fist, as if toward an open door.

She did not make it sound beautiful. She did not make it sound terrible. She said it the way a person reads out a list of what was in a house after a fire.

When she was done, the river went on running and the traffic went on roaring, and three thousand people said nothing at all.

Then a man near the front stood up.

He was middle-aged and heavy-set, in a delivery driver's uniform, with a candle in each hand.

"My father is in the eastern field," he said. His voice shook. "Number four thousand and something. I don't even know which one. And now you want to tell me there's a *list,* and some boy with a law in his head gets to decide whether my father is awake or asleep, and we're supposed to *help* him?" He turned to the crowd. "We're the Quiet. We let them rest. That's the whole thing. That's all we've ever been."

There was a murmur. Agreement. Not all of them. Some.

"Your father isn't on the list," said Sister Ning.

The man stopped.

"He can't be. He died before there was one. Nobody asked him." She turned her candle in her fingers. "That's why I want us in the room. Not to help the boy. To make sure that every single time, someone who believes in rest is standing there when he reaches for someone. To hear the no, if there's a no."

---

She had three terms. She said them plainly, one after the other, and Yuanshu, standing at the top of the steps, knew she was saying them for him as much as for anyone.

"One. One of us witnesses every time he touches the dead or the taken. Every single time. Not the Bureau. Not Tianshu. Us. Someone who would rather he didn't."

"Two. Quiet means quiet. Forever. Not *unless.*" She glanced up the steps, straight at him, through the crowd. "He's already agreed to that one. In the rain. In front of the Director."

"Three." She held up the candle. "We see it work. Before I ask any of you to put your name on anything, we see it. Tonight."

---

They carried the crystal down the steps in a steel case.

Director Lu carried it himself. Captain Lin walked in front of him and two Bureau officers walked behind, and the crowd of the Quiet parted for them in silence, three thousand faces turning to follow the small steel case down to the edge of the water.

Lu set it on the concrete at Sister Ning's feet, and opened it, and stepped back.

The crystal sat in a bed of gray foam. The size of a fist. Pulsing violet. The ring of tiny screaming mouths on its face.

In the front rows, people leaned away from it without knowing why.

Yuanshu came down the steps.

He knelt on the cold concrete beside the case. Sister Ning shifted her stool closer. The man in the delivery uniform had come forward too, without being asked, and stood at her shoulder with his two candles, glaring.

> **Ten thousand, four hundred and six.**
>
> **I counted them last night. I wanted to know exactly.**
>
> **I am not going to push. I am not going to call.**
>
> **I am going to open a door, and leave it open, and wait.**

*Like Return.*

> **Not like Return. Return is rest offered to the dead. These are not dead.**
>
> **This is a door. On the other side of it is rest. On this side is the crystal, and the dark, and each other.**
>
> **Anyone who wants to go through can go through.**
>
> **Anyone who does not want to can stay. And tell me why.**

He put his hand on the crystal.

It was cold, and it hummed against his palm, and he could hear them very faintly, the low terrible moan of ten thousand voices in the dark.

He didn't reach for them. He reached past them, into the gray place where the river ran home. And instead of pouring rest into the crystal the way he had poured it into the canyon and the mine, he simply opened a door in the dark beside it. A small door. A gray door. Open.

And waited.

---

For a long time, nothing happened.

The river ran. The traffic roared on the bridge above. Three thousand candle flames stood in the still air under the arch.

Then, at the bottom of the steps, by the water, Sister Ning's candle leaned.

Not toward the crystal. Away from it. Toward the dark beside it. Toward the door.

It leaned, and trembled, and straightened.

Then leaned again.

> **One.**
>
> **She was a fox. From the Wastes. She was very old. She went through without looking back.**

Then the candle next to Sister Ning's leaned too. And the two in the hands of the man in the delivery uniform. Both at once, toward the dark, flickering, straightening.

> **Two. Three. Four.**
>
> **A man. A bird. A man.**

And then the leaning spread.

It went out through the crowd under the bridge the way wind goes through a field of grass. Row by row. Candle by candle. Three thousand small flames bending toward the same patch of empty dark beside a steel case on the concrete, trembling, straightening, bending again, over and over, as if a long, slow, invisible procession were walking down the steps through all of them, one at a time, toward the water, and each one that passed made every flame in the crowd bow.

Nobody under the Old Ferry Bridge said a word.

---

It went on until dawn.

The sky over the river went from black to gray to pale gold. The traffic on the bridge thinned and then thickened again. One by one, the candles in the crowd burned down to nothing and went out, and people lit new ones from their neighbors' and went on watching.

> **Seven thousand three hundred and twelve.**
>
> **They have gone through.**
>
> **The rest have stayed. Three thousand and ninety-four.**
>
> **I asked them why.**

*What did they say?*

> **Some of them are waiting for someone. Someone they were taken with. They do not want to go without them.**
>
> **Some of them want to be heard first. They want someone to know their names.**
>
> **Some of them want to send messages to people who are still alive.**
>
> **And some of them are angry. They want to see whatever did this to them stopped.**
>
> **They want to stay and help.**

Yuanshu sat back on his heels on the cold concrete, in the dawn, with the crystal in its case in front of him.

It was pulsing more slowly now. And its color had changed. Not the bruised violet of before. Something paler. Almost gray.

On her stool beside him, Sister Ning had not slept. She looked at the crystal, and at the guttering candle in her hands, and at the man in the delivery uniform, who was sitting on the steps now with his two burned-out candles in his lap, crying quietly into his sleeve.

Then she stood up, slowly, with a grunt of old knees, and turned to face the three thousand people under the bridge.

"I saw it," she said. "Every one of you saw it. Some of them went. Some of them stayed. Nobody was pushed."

She held up her notebook.

"I'm going to witness for him. Every time. For as long as I can walk. Anybody who wants to stand beside me is welcome." She looked out across the crowd. "Anybody who doesn't is still Quiet. Still family. We'll still light your candles."

---

About two thousand stayed.

The rest went up the steps in the dawn light, past Yuanshu, in twos and threes, some of them looking at him and most of them not. The man in the delivery uniform went last. At the top of the steps he stopped, and turned, and looked down at the crystal in its case, gone gray, beating slowly in the morning light.

Then he came back down, sat on the bottom step beside Sister Ning, and lit a new candle.

"Number four thousand and something," he said, to no one. "I'd like to know which one."

> **Three thousand and ninety-four still in the crystal.**
>
> **And one more name for the Ledger.**
>
> **He has not chosen yet. But he is going to ask.**
