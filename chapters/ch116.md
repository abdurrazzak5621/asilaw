## Chapter 116: The Sand

It took Doctor Iris Kwan four days to find where the sphere had come from.

She did it from a hotel room in Port Halvard with three laptops and no sleep, while the rain came down outside and the rolling blackouts came and went, and every four hours the lights in her room went out and she kept typing by the glow of the screens.

On the fourth night, she knocked on Yuanshu's door at two in the morning.

She was holding one of the laptops against her chest with both arms. Not in its supermarket bags this time. Her glasses were crooked. Her face was white.

"Sand," she said.

---

She laid it out on the bed in his hotel room. Lu came in from next door in a coat over his pajamas. Lingyue came in from across the hall with her sword. Old Seven was already there, standing by the window, looking out at the dark lighthouse on the headland.

"The Federation bought the sphere through a trust," said Kwan. "Registered in the capital. Eleven months ago." She turned the laptop around. "The Halcyon Trust."

Nobody spoke.

"It was dissolved," said Lu, very quietly. "After Concordia. After Mercer. The Bureau and the Federation shut it down. I signed the papers myself."

"It was dissolved," agreed Kwan. "And then it was re-registered. Six weeks later. Same name. Same registered agent." She tapped the screen. "Whitcombe Hale."

"Garrett Hale was hollow," said Lu. "On Concordia. In the rain. For ten weeks. He's been in the Listening Pavilion with Harlan Voss sitting beside him..."

"I know. It wasn't him. Hale's firm has four thousand trusts. Somebody in it re-registered Halcyon while he was in a teahouse in Lingzhou with his eyes open." Kwan's voice was shaking very slightly. "And Halcyon paid a salvage company in the Southern Compact. Deepwater Recovery. Eight months ago. To dredge a location off the northern coast." She turned the laptop again. A map. A blue sea. A red mark, kilometers out, past the edge of the continental shelf, in water so deep the map just showed it as black.

"Port Abiri," said Lingyue.

"Where Ifeoma sank the spire," said Kwan. "The sand. The gray sand. Everything that was left of it." She closed the laptop. "They dredged it up. They shipped it to a lab. Somebody sorted it. Grain by grain." She looked at Yuanshu. "And found the seeds. Inside. The parts of the spire that weren't dead. Small. Asleep. Like the core of a peach."

"How many?" said Yuanshu.

Kwan did not answer for a long moment.

"Seven," she said.

---

The hotel room was very quiet.

Outside, the rain. The dark lighthouse. Down the coast road, the hospital, running on rationed diesel, the children's ward lit and everything else dark.

"One went to the Federation," said Kwan. "For Hearth One. Here. That one's on the record. Paid for with Federation money, through Halcyon, all very proper." She swallowed. "The other six were sold privately. Through the trust. In gold." She looked at Lu. "Like Mister Moon."

"To who?"

"I don't know yet. The trust's records were... edited. After." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "Three to buyers I can't trace. One to a mining consortium in the Northern Union. One to a shell company in Yamato." A pause. "And one with a delivery address I can't read. The record's corrupted. Only the first character survives."

"What is it?"

"A city code," said Kwan. "Huaxian." She looked at Yuanshu. "L."

Lingzhou.

---

Yuanshu sat on the edge of the hotel bed for a long time.

> **Six seeds.**
>
> **Six more spheres. Somewhere on Blue Star. Asleep. Hungry.**
>
> **And one of them is home.**

"Does Cassia Lowell know?" he said finally.

Kwan shook her head. "I don't think so. I think she was told it was a lens. Built by Federation scientists from Architect principles. That's what she said to you. I think she believes it." She looked down at the closed laptop. "I think somebody let her believe it. Because a kind woman with a clean record who believes in the Charter is the best face Project Hearth could ever have."

> **Like Gu.**
>
> **Someone who learned how to be loved.**
>
> **Not the thread. The use of it.**

---

He went to see Cassia Lowell at dawn.

She was in a small plain office at the back of Hearth One, with a window over the gray sea, drinking coffee from a paper cup. Roman Sterling stood by the door. She had not slept either. He could see it in her face.

He told her.

All of it. The trust. The dredging. The sand. The seven seeds. The six that had gone somewhere else, in gold.

She listened without interrupting. Her face did not move. When he finished, she put the paper cup down on the desk, very carefully, and looked at it for a long time.

"I reviewed every volunteer's file myself," she said at last. Very quietly. "Every witness statement. Every recording. Four hundred thousand. I didn't sleep for a month. I wanted it to be perfect. I wanted it to be the cleanest thing the Federation had ever done." She looked up at him. "I never asked where the sphere came from. I asked about the people. Not the thing."

"I know."

"That's not an excuse."

"No," said Yuanshu. "It's not."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she stood up. And walked to the window. And stood there with her back to him, looking out at the gray sea and the dark lighthouse on the point.

"Then we stop," said Cassia Lowell. "Today. I'll call the Council. I'll suspend Hearth. I'll..."

"No," said a voice from the doorway.

---

Walter Ashby was in his wheelchair in the corridor. A nurse behind him, looking terrified. The oxygen tube under his nose. His enormous knotted hands gripping the arms of the chair.

He had heard. Yuanshu did not know how much. Enough.

"No," said the old lighthouse keeper again. His voice was a wheeze. "You don't get to stop it. Not mine."

Cassia Lowell turned from the window.

"Walter, the sphere is... it's not what we were told, it's a piece of a..."

"I heard," said Walter Ashby. "I'm old, not deaf." He looked at Yuanshu. "The boy told me what it was a week ago. A piece of the thing that ate that town. Hungry. No lock. Burns forever." He coughed, long and wet and rattling, and when it stopped he did not wipe his mouth. "I said yes anyway. Twice. In front of him."

The office was very quiet.

"You told me it was my choice," said Walter Ashby to the Secretary of the Atlantic Federation. "That was the whole point. That's what you said. On the telly. *Entirely voluntary.*" His pale blue eyes were very bright. "So it's my choice. Not yours. Not his. Mine." He looked out of the window at the dark lighthouse. "I've got two days, the doctor says. Maybe one. I'm not going to spend them watching that light stay dark because somebody found out the lamp came from a bad shop."

---

Nobody said anything.

Cassia Lowell looked at the old man in the wheelchair for a long time. Then at Yuanshu.

He did not look away from her.

"He's right," said Yuanshu quietly.

She stared at him.

"It's his choice," he said. "That's what we built. A world where people choose. Even when they choose something that breaks your heart." He swallowed. "*Quiet means quiet.* That's in Article Three. But so is *yes means yes.* We don't get to take that back from him because we're frightened."

"Then what do we..."

"We do what he asked," said Yuanshu. "I go in with him. After. And I ask him again." He looked at Walter Ashby. "And if he says no, I keep my promise. I get him out. Somehow."

"And the sphere?"

"After he's out," said Yuanshu, "nobody else goes in. Ever. And we find the other six before anybody else does."

---

Cassia Lowell looked at him for a very long time.

Then she nodded. Once.

"One volunteer," she said. "Walter's. Nobody else. I'll freeze the registry today. Everyone else's yes is suspended until we know." She looked at the old man. "Is that acceptable to you, Mister Ashby?"

"It's acceptable," said Walter Ashby. "Long as I'm first."

He coughed again. It went on for a long time. The nurse put a hand on his shoulder. He let her.

When it stopped, he looked out of the window again. At the lighthouse.

"Ellie's in the children's ward," he said, to nobody in particular. "My great-granddaughter. Seven. Asthma. They turn her machine off for two hours every night when the diesel runs low. She's not supposed to know. She knows." He folded his enormous knotted hands in his lap. "Ten years, the Secretary said. Ten years, that ward stays lit."

He looked at Yuanshu.

"You'll ask me properly," he said. "After. Won't you."

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

"Good," said the old man, and closed his eyes.
