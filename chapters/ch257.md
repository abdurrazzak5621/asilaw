## Chapter 257: Home

The dead came home all morning.

He watched it from the platform of the Awakening Hall, sitting against the warm stone with a blanket round his shoulders that someone had put there and a cup of Fat Tan's tea going cold in his hands, because Bai Lu had said that if he tried to stand up she would sit on him. He could not see them. Nobody could. But he could feel them, and so could Seed, and so, Gao Min said, could her laser, which jumped every time a column of them came down the thread into the stone and went sideways into the net and home.

Twelve thousand embers. Down eleven threads, into eleven stones, out through the knots where eleven people from the bottom of a well sat holding, and along the net, nine by nine, round and round, slow, to the War Memorial Cemetery and the slope by the river and a valley in the Northern Wastes and the Western Pass and stone No. 7.

The Spear Corps first. Two hundred and seventy-two. He felt them come down into the Lingzhou stone in a knot, together, the way they had gone up, and out through Wen Jia, and away. Fifteen minutes later, Kwan, who had driven out to the cemetery at dawn with the laptop, telephoned to say that the bones round stone No. 7 had stood up. All at once. And faced out.

Then the First Legion, in their rows of nine. Then the cemetery companies. Grid Nine, last of the Lingzhou dead, who came down the thread slowly, and stopped in the stone for a moment before they went home, and sent a message up the net that took four hours to reach him.

> **They say: we saw the sea and the deep water in one week.**
>
> **They say: we would like to sit down for a while.**

---

Old Seven came at noon.

He did not come down the Lingzhou thread. He had gone up it, lying on his back on the polished stone floor of the Awakening Hall beside the platform, with his spear across his chest and his one finger and his thumb closed round it. His bones had been lying there for twelve hours. Nobody had touched them. Fat Tan had put a folded blanket under the skull at four in the morning, very carefully, and then gone and stood by the side door with his arms folded and his eyes wet and dared anyone to say anything.

At noon, the bones stood up.

Slowly. The way he did everything. One hand on the spear, pushing up off the floor. Two meters ten. He stood beside the platform for a moment, upright, the blanket fallen at his feet, and turned his skull, and looked at the boy sitting against the stone with the cup of tea.

> **He says: Port Halvard.**
>
> **He says: we took the man down the Port Halvard thread. The sergeant and me. The docker in the stone caught him. He says the docker said: steady, lad. Steady. You're home.**
>
> **He says: then we came across. Sideways. Along the threads under the world. The sergeant is coming the long way, with the Sixty. He says they will be a day.**
>
> **He says: the man is down. In the hall by the sea.**
>
> **He says: his mother was there.**

He put the cup down.

*Was he...?*

> **He says: ask the King on the radio. He was there. He saw. He says it is not for a captain to say what a mother looked like.**

---

Sterling was in a wheelchair.

He told them so himself, on the radio, at twenty past twelve, in a voice that was not quite steady. The doctors had said a month before he could decide for anything bigger than a door. They had not said anything about wheelchairs. He had made them wheel him down the hill from the hospital to the harbor at four in the morning, when Seed's message came down the net that they were coming, and sit him at the head of the roofless hall beside the black stone, and leave him there.

"*She'd been there since the hand,*" said Roman Sterling. "*Since before. She never went home. She had her hand on the stone every day for six weeks. And every morning she brought something with her and put it on the floor next to her and every night she took it home again.*"

"*What?*"

"*His jacket.*" A pause on the line. Wind off the Atlantic. "*His work jacket. Port Authority. Orange, with the reflective strips. He'd left it on the back of a chair the night he climbed in the window with the hammer. She said she wasn't going to have him come home with nothing on. She said that every morning. To the stone. I heard her.*"

He did not say anything.

"*He came down at nine minutes past eleven,*" said Sterling. "*Out of the top of the stone. The docker said something. I didn't hear it. I heard her. She didn't scream. She said: Callum. Like she was calling him in for his tea.*" The King of Force was quiet for a moment. "*He came down into the jacket. On the floor. Like the sixty-one came down into their coats. He was lying on the floor of the hall in an orange work jacket, and he was a man. Not burning. Just a man. Thin. Gray. Twenty-five. His eyes were open.*"

"*And?*"

"*And he looked up at her,*" said Sterling, "*and he said: Mum. I told it. I held it up and I said oi and it looked.*"

Another pause.

"*And she said: what do you think,*" said Roman Sterling, "*and then she sat down on the floor of the hall and put her arms round him and I made them wheel me out, because it wasn't for me to see.*"

---

The platform was quiet.

Then Lingyue, who had been sitting beside him against the stone since six in the morning with her hand on his arm and the pale flame in her other palm, said: "*Is he all right?*"

"*He's tired,*" said Sterling. "*The docs say he's going to be tired for a long time. He's lost weight. He doesn't remember most of the six weeks. He remembers the fire and the voice and the bit at the end.*" A pause. "*He asked for a bacon sandwich. They said he couldn't have one. He said he'd been the brightest thing in the dark between the arms of the galaxy and he'd like a bacon sandwich. They gave him one.*"

On the platform, Xiaoyu laughed. Out loud. With her hand over her mouth. She had not slept either.

"*And Sterling,*" said the King of Force.

"*Yes?*"

"*He asked me what grade I was. When I was eighteen.*" A long pause. "*I told him. SSS. Ironclad. He said: and did it tell you what you were? And I said: yes. And he said: was it right?*"

The line was quiet.

"*I didn't know,*" said Roman Sterling. "*I'm seventy. I've been a King for fifty years. And a dock worker with an F asked me if the rock was right, and I didn't know.*" He breathed out. "*I'd like your sister to put that on the wall too. Small. Not in red.*"

---

The Sixty came the next morning, the long way, and they did not come down any thread.

They came up out of the ground.

Kwan saw it first. She had gone back out to the War Memorial Cemetery at dawn because Seed had said the sergeant was close, and she telephoned the Ossuary at 5:51, when the sun came up over the river, and could not say anything for a while, and then said: "*Stone No. 7.*"

He went. They all went. Gao Min drove, because Kwan was not answering her phone any more, and they came up the long green slope from the river in the early light between the rows of small gray stones, past the cypresses, to the eastern field.

The Spear Corps were standing round stone No. 7, facing out, the way they always stood.

And between them and the stone, in the grass, in a ring round it, ash.

Not bones. The bones of the Third Battalion were at the cold fire, where they had stood at the edge of the quiet part for four hundred and forty days and then sixty-odd more, and would stay there, Seed said, because they had been left facing out and the dead did not like to be moved once they had been left facing something. What had come home was not bones. It was what the dead keep when there is nothing else: their shape. In ash. The way Hundred-Arrow Lin had been a shade of ash with an iron arrowhead for a heart, on the north wall, for two years.

Sixty shapes of gray ash, standing in a ring in the grass round stone No. 7, in the gray light, facing out.

And at the front of them, a short one. Square in the shoulders. With the shape of a pick on its shoulder.

---

Old Seven walked up the slope.

He had not come in the car. He never came in cars. He had walked from the Awakening Hall in the dark, and he came up the long green slope of the War Memorial Cemetery between the small gray stones as the sun came up over the river, two meters ten, with his spear grounded in one finger and a thumb, slowly, the way he had walked out of it on the first night, two years ago, at three in the morning, when a boy with a training spear and no permit asked him to.

He walked through the Spear Corps, who made room for him without being asked.

He stopped in front of the sergeant.

The ash shape of Huo Changsheng looked up at him. Then down. At the left arm. The sergeant's own arm, with one finger and a thumb.

> **Huo says: sir.**
>
> **Huo says: we're home.**
>
> **He says: yes.**
>
> **Huo says: the cold fire's empty. There's nobody left in there to stand in front of. I asked them all. Every morning. Nobody answered for the last month. I think they've all gone through.**
>
> **He says: I know. You told me on the thread.**
>
> **Huo says: I'm telling you again. So it's said here. On the grass.**
>
> **Huo says: the Third Battalion stood at the edge of the cold for four hundred and forty days so the cold came to them first. And then some more. And now we're home. And I'd like to stop facing out for a bit, sir. If that's all right.**

Old Seven was quiet for a long moment.

Then he turned round.

Not out. In. Toward stone No. 7, the stone he had come out of on the first night, the small gray stone on the slope with no name on it, only a number. He turned his back on the river and the city and the sun coming up, and faced the stone. And put his spear down on the grass.

> **He says: at ease.**

The sixty ash shapes of the Third Battalion turned round. In. Toward the stone. Toward their captain.

And sat down on the grass.

---

He stood at the edge of the eastern field with the staff in his hand and Lingyue beside him and Xiaoyu on his other side with her marker, and watched sixty shapes of gray ash sit down on the grass of the War Memorial Cemetery round a small stone with a number on it, at sunrise, with a tall dead captain standing among them facing in, for once, and not out.

He did not say anything. He found he could not.

Lingyue took his hand. The one with the ring on it.

"*Say it,*" she said. "*You say it every morning.*"

He looked at the short square shape of ash at the front, with the pick across its knees.

"*Huo Changsheng,*" he said. "*Sergeant. Third Battalion. You held.*"

The ash shape did not turn round. It was facing the stone. Facing in. At ease.

> **Huo says: I know.**
>
> **Huo says: you can stop saying it now, boy. I'm home. Say it to someone who isn't.**
>
> **Huo says: say it to the ones in the deep water.**

---

That evening, Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder in the banking hall of the Ossuary and wrote, at the end of the INSIDE column, under the queen of hearts on its single pin, under *HUO CHANGSHENG. SIXTY. AT THE COLD FIRE. FACING OUT. SOMEONE IS WAITING WITH THEM*, and under *SAY HIS NAME*:

*HOME. STONE NO. 7. FACING IN. AT EASE.*

And on a new column, round the corner on the last of the white plaster, at the top, in square black capitals:

*THE WATCH OF THE SHELF. 1,146. NANA AMPOFO. YAW, WHO ASKED FIRST.*

*IN THE DEEP WATER. ASKING. NINE BY NINE.*

*SAY THEIR NAMES. ASK THEM AGAIN IN A YEAR.*
