## Chapter 140: Inside

The message from inside the Mouth came at three in the morning on the twenty-third day, the long way, through the echo, the way they always came.

The keeper caught it. The Elder carried it down the gold thread. Seed read it out loud in the old banking hall of the Ossuary, under the one lamp over the board, to the five people who were awake: Yuanshu, Xiaoyu in her pajamas with her hair sticking up, Fat Tan on the vault steps, Sister Ning on the wooden chair by the bronze door. And Old Seven, who had come in last, as he always did, from the hill above the cemetery where a captain stood in a gate and a director lay under a stone beside him.

It took eleven seconds. Longer than the others.

> ***Day 178.***
>
> ***Worlds asked: 34. Burning low: 21.***
>
> ***The sergeant says the men are well. Private Tan Wei says the world that sings has learned a new song. He says it is about a captain.***
>
> ***Something else.***
>
> ***There is a voice in here that is not burning.***
>
> ***It is not one of the worlds. It is not one of us. It is very old and very quiet and it is not in the fire. It is behind the fire. Like someone standing behind a curtain.***
>
> ***It asked me who I am.***
>
> ***I said: a child of the world that asks.***
>
> ***It said: I have been looking for that world for a long time.***
>
> ***Then it went quiet.***
>
> ***I did not like it. The Sixth did not like it. The sergeant has put six men on the curtain.***
>
> ***I still have the queen of hearts.***
>
> ***Tell the captain. — H.***

---

Nobody said anything.

Xiaoyu was already on the stepladder. She had the black marker. She climbed up to the very end of the board, under INSIDE, under the lines from Day 31 and Day 64 and Day 98 and Day 127 and Day 158, and wrote the new one in careful capitals, and her hand did not shake until the last word.

*Day 178. Worlds asked: 34. Burning low: 21. The men are well. Something behind the curtain is looking for us.*

She stepped down and stood back and looked at it with her arms folded.

"I don't want to write that last part," she said. "I want you to know that. I wrote it because it's true."

"I know," said Yuanshu.

At the bronze door, Old Seven stood looking at the board. He read it all. From the beginning. The way he did every month. Then he stood there a while longer, with his spear grounded and his gray flame very small, looking at the last line.

> **He says six men on a curtain is the right number.**
>
> **He says Huo was always good with numbers.**
>
> **He says: tell them the captain is well too.**

"How do we tell them?" said Xiaoyu.

Nobody knew. Nobody had ever known. The messages came one way. That was the thing about the inside of a fire.

"You write it on the board," said Sister Ning from her chair. "Under theirs. In case."

Xiaoyu looked at her. Then she climbed back up the stepladder.

*The captain is well too.*

---

His mother was awake when he got home at five.

She was sitting in her wheelchair by the kitchen window with a blanket over her knees and the lights off, looking out at the river, the way she sometimes did when she could not sleep. She did not turn around when he came in. She knew his step.

"You're going west," she said.

"Tomorrow."

"Today." She looked at the clock on the stove. "It's already today."

He sat down at the kitchen table. The staff leaned against the wall by the door, where it always leaned now, with its small white skull and its silver reading glasses. Two old women were asleep in it, snoring. He could hear them if he listened.

"Mama," he said. "You told me to ask you again. After. About her."

His mother did not answer for a while. She looked at the staff by the door.

"It isn't after," she said.

"No."

"Then I'll tell you what I've thought so far." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "She's the part of me that went loud. Twice. In the field, so you could get away. And in the fire, so you could get out. I couldn't have done either of those things from this chair." She was quiet. "She's not tired any more, I don't think. I think she's resting. The way a soldier rests between watches."

"So you don't want her back."

"I didn't say that." Zhou Wanqing almost smiled. "I said she's on watch. Let her finish it. She's looking after you. That's what I would be doing, if I could get out of this chair." She looked at him. "Ask me again when you come home."

"I will."

"I know you will," said his mother. "That's the whole problem with you."

---

Lingyue was on the Sector Nine battlements at dawn, as he had known she would be, in the spot where he used to sit every night looking north. She had her sword across her knees. Her grandfather's sword was beside it, sheathed, on the stones.

"He gave it to me," she said, before he asked. "Last night. He said he's going back to the family house to tell three hundred years of Shens that Light runs downhill. He said he needs both hands for that." She touched the sheath. "He said I'll need it in the west. He said a King's sword is heavier than it looks, and I should get used to the weight."

"Are you?"

"No." She did not look at him. "It weighs about a hundred kilos. He carried it for sixty years like it was a stick."

He sat down beside her.

North, over the end of the world, the small white ring hung low in the gray morning, the way it always did. West, beyond the city, beyond the river towns and the plain, a thousand kilometers away, there was a mountain of graves with an old man sitting on one of them, waiting.

"Mingli's staying," he said. "She's barely standing. She'll hold the city with Ma Lianhua and the Legion. Orlov's going north to get Sokolova's people moving. Ren's going home. She thinks the seed in the mountains is on her mountain. At the shrine. The Abbot's old shrine."

"And us?"

"The Spear Corps. Adjutant Xu. The twins. Bai Lu. Dabao, because he said if I didn't take him this time he'd follow the train on foot. And you." He looked at her. "If you want."

Lingyue turned her head and looked at him for the first time.

"You're asking me," she said.

"Yes."

"You don't need to ask me. You've never needed to ask me." She picked up her grandfather's sword, with both hands, and set it across her knees beside her own. It was very heavy. She did not let him see that it was. "I told you what I chose. On the ice at Grid Nine. *Only you.* That wasn't only about after."

He did not say anything for a moment.

Then he said, "I know."

"Good." She looked back north. "Then stop asking me things you know."

"That's the only thing I know how to do."

"Hm," said Lingyue. "We'll see."

---

The train left Lingzhou Central at eight.

A Western War Zone train. Eleven carriages, painted dark green, sent by an old general with one arm. Three hundred dead soldiers of the Spear Corps filled four of them, standing in the aisles in their rows, rusted spears grounded. A small stooped skeleton with a wooden cane sat in the first carriage with his legs crossed, the way a man sits who has finally been given a seat. The Mo twins took the window seats and argued about them. Bai Lu had brought her grandmother's brass clock, wound, ticking. Hu Dabao took up most of a bench by himself, with his shield.

Lingyue sat across from Yuanshu with two swords.

And at the front of the train, in the doorway between the engine and the first carriage, Old Seven stood facing west, with his spear in a hand that had one finger and a thumb, the way he had once stood by the door of a room under a mountain while a boy read a dead man's notebook by lamplight.

> **Level 47.**
>
> **Death Law: 32.6%. Field.**
>
> **Thirty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two of the dead at the Western Pass said yes to you, a year ago, on a cold day, with the rule read out first.**
>
> **They are burning under the ground.**
>
> **Yuanshu. Let us go and ask them what they want.**

The train pulled out of the station, west.
