## Chapter 239: Easier

He wrote the statement in eleven minutes, and that was how he knew something was wrong.

It was the fourth night after the Number Nine. He was sitting at the long table in the banking hall at two in the morning with a sheet of ledger paper and a pen, writing what would be read aloud in eleven halls on Awakening Day before anything was done to anything. Article Twelve said you had to. *Say aloud that you decided, and that nobody said yes.* So he wrote it.

*The Stele cannot answer. It is not anyone. We decided for it. Nobody said yes. We are putting into it only what was freely given: eleven people who chose to go. We will hold on to nothing. We will ask it every day.*

He read it over. It was good. It was clear and short and true, and it said everything the article required, in the right order.

He looked at the clock over the vault door. Eleven minutes.

The first time, on the battlement, he had sat on cold stone for an hour and not been able to answer Seed at all. He had needed his wife to come up the stairs and tell him that every person who had ever lived had been put into the world without a yes. He had needed his mother at breakfast. It had taken him two hours to write eleven lines for Xiaoyu to put on the wall, with three crossings-out and a tear in the paper where the pen had gone through.

This had taken eleven minutes. No crossings-out.

He put the pen down.

> **I noticed too.**

*When?*

> **Minute six. You did not stop. Last time you stopped every line.**
>
> **I did not say anything. I wanted to see if you would notice on your own.**

He sat at the long table under the lamp and looked across the banking hall at the wall. At the head of the last column, ARTICLE TWELVE. And under it, small, in red, in his sister's capitals:

*R. STERLING: NO. BECAUSE IT GETS EASY. READ THIS EVERY TIME.*

He had not read it. Not tonight. He had walked past it to the table and sat down and written.

He got up, and went to the wall, and read it. Out loud. To the empty hall.

Then he went back to the table, and tore the sheet of ledger paper in half, and sat down, and did not write anything else that night.

---

Roman Sterling came on the sixth day.

Nobody knew he was coming. He did not fly; Kings did not fly into other nations without telling Concordia, and he had not told Concordia. He came on the overnight ferry from the Atlantic coast to the Huaxia ports and then on the morning train to Lingzhou like anybody else, in a gray raincoat, with a canvas bag over his shoulder, and walked from the station to East Market Road, and stopped at the bottom of the steps of the Ossuary at nine in the morning and looked up at the bronze door.

Old Seven was in it.

The King of Force was a big man, broad and heavy and slow-moving in the way of men who have spent their lives being careful not to break things by accident. Seventy, perhaps. Short gray hair. A boxer's nose. He stood at the bottom of the steps and looked up at the tall dead captain of the Eastern Line in the doorway, two meters ten, with his spear grounded in one finger and a thumb.

"*I'd like to come in,*" he said. "*I'd like to talk to the boy. I'm not here as a King. I left that on the ferry.*"

Old Seven looked at him for a long moment.

Then he stood aside.

> **He says: he knows.**
>
> **He says: he could tell from how the man stood at the bottom of the steps. A King stands as if the steps should come down to him. This one stood as if he was waiting to be asked up.**

---

They sat on the vault steps, because Yuanshu did his thinking there, and because Sterling said he did not want a chair. Lingyue sat on the bottom step of the stairs with her sword across her knees and a candle on the floor beside her, lit, a small pale flame the size of a fingertip now, which she was holding and not looking at. Xiaoyu was at the long table and pretended not to be listening. Fat Tan made tea.

"*I read your article,*" said Sterling. "*All of them. Twelve. I read the first eleven on the ferry. I read the twelfth about fifty times.*" He held the tea in both hands. They were enormous hands, scarred over the knuckles. "*I voted no.*"

"*I know. It's on the wall.*"

"*I know it's on the wall. Your sister sent me a photograph.*" He did not smile. "*I want to tell you why. Properly. Not on the radio. On the radio it sounds like I'm being clever.*"

He drank some tea.

"*I'm a Force King,*" he said. "*You know what Force is? Under all the Law and the cultivation and the textbook words?*" He put the cup down on the step. "*It's deciding for things. That's all it is. A wall's in the way, you decide it isn't. A man's coming at you, you decide he's going backwards. You don't ask the wall. You don't ask the man. That's the whole Law.*" He looked at his hands. "*The first time I threw a man through a wall, I was nineteen. He'd come into a shelter during a rift breach with a knife. I threw him through a wall to stop him. I was sick for two days. I couldn't eat. I kept seeing his face going backwards.*"

He was quiet.

"*The second time, I was sick for an afternoon.*"

Yuanshu did not say anything.

"*By the tenth time, I didn't notice,*" said Roman Sterling. "*By the hundredth, I was good at it. I was fast. I could decide for a building in a breath. That's how you become a King. You get very good at deciding for things very quickly.*" He looked at the boy on the step. "*And nobody ever stops you and says: you didn't ask. Because you're a King. Because it worked. Because the building's down and everyone's alive.*"

He picked the cup up again.

"*How long did it take you,*" he said, "*to write the statement for the stones?*"

Yuanshu looked at him.

"*Eleven minutes,*" he said.

Sterling nodded slowly.

"*And the one for the tree?*"

"*Two hours. And a battlement. And my wife. And my mother.*"

"*Yes,*" said the King of Force. "*That's why I voted no.*"

---

Nobody said anything for a while. The candle on the floor beside Lingyue burned straight up in the still air of the banking hall.

"*I tore it up,*" said Yuanshu.

"*I heard. Your sister.*" Sterling almost smiled, then did not. "*That's good. That's once. It's going to get easy again. Next time. And the time after. You're going to be very good at this by the time you're my age.*" He turned the cup in his hands. "*I didn't come to tell you not to do it. The stones are worse than you. I counted. Gao Min sent me her graph. I've got a granddaughter turning eighteen in four years.*" He looked across the hall at the long white wall. "*I came to ask if I can stand there.*"

"*Where?*"

"*Next to you. On the day. In the hall. When you read it out. When you say nobody said yes.*" He put the cup down. "*I'd like to say no. Out loud. Right after. Every time. Every stone. Every time you ever use that article, for as long as I'm alive.*" He looked at his scarred hands. "*So it's never only your voice. So there's always someone in the room who says: no, it's not easy, and you didn't ask, and I'm not letting you forget.*"

From the bottom step of the stairs, Lingyue said, without looking up from the candle: "*Sister Ning does that.*"

Sterling turned his head.

"*The Quiet,*" said Lingyue. "*One of them witnesses every time he touches the dead. Every single time. Someone who'd rather he didn't.*" She held up her finger with the flame on it. "*It's been the rule since the Ledger. You're asking to be that. For the twelfth.*"

"*Then yes,*" said Roman Sterling. "*I'm asking to be that.*"

Yuanshu looked at the big gray man on the vault step, with his boxer's nose and his raincoat and his scarred hands round a cup of Fat Tan's tea.

"*Yes,*" he said. "*Please.*"

---

> **There is a problem I have not solved.**

It was evening. Kwan was at the long table with the laptop and three diagrams, and Gao Min with a ledger, and Sterling, who had not gone to a hotel, with a pencil, drawing something on the back of a timetable. The world had been asked for five days, down the slow net of the Lattice and over the radio and on paper ballots in schools and temples, and was still answering. Fifty-eight percent, so far, said: *let the eleven go in*. Twenty-nine said: *no awakenings this year, not until we know*. The rest said other things, which Kwan read out loud in the evenings, and Xiaoyu wrote the best of on the wall.

And under all of it, the article that had not needed a vote, because it was not anyone's to vote on: *every child decides for themselves whether to touch it.* Article Ten. Their window.

> **The graft.**
>
> **A graft is bone to bone. Hands on both. I can unpress Wen Jia's strand, the way I unpressed the hundred and thirty-eight. I can carry her to the stone. But someone has to put their hands on it and tie her into the knot at the bottom.**
>
> **And anything that puts its hands on a Stele gets read.**

"*Callum Ward,*" said Kwan quietly.

> **Yes.**
>
> **If Yuanshu puts his hand on it, it reads him. And it reads me. The way it did on the first morning. And it went black then and did not know what to do with us.**
>
> **It knows now. It has had two years. It sent up what it saw, and something heard, and sent a scout, and rings, and a tree.**
>
> **I do not know what it will do this time. I have run it. Some of the runs, it goes black again. Some of the runs, it opens him. All the way. Like Callum Ward.**
>
> **And sends him up.**

Lingyue put her candle out.

"*No,*" she said.

> **I agree.**

"*Then who?*" said Sterling.

---

> **Old Seven.**

The tall dead soldier at the bronze door did not move. But he had turned his skull, a little, toward the long table.

> **READ WHAT RIPENS. A MIND IS RIPE WHEN IT HAS GROWN ITS OWN SHAPE AND NOT YET SET.**
>
> **The dead have set. They are not growing. They are not ripening. They are embers. It was written for the living, and for children, and for the moment between.**
>
> **I do not think a Stele can read the dead. I think a Stele would look at Old Seven and see nothing it was made to see.**
>
> **And Yuanshu would be inside him. The way he was on the cut. With Huo's hand.**
>
> **The stone would not see him either. Not through the dead. I think.**
>
> **I think. I do not know. I have run it nine hundred times. Old Seven is not read in eight hundred and sixty of them.**
>
> **In the other forty, I do not know what happens. I cannot see past the touch.**

At the bronze door, Old Seven came away from the doorway.

He walked across the marble floor of the banking hall the way he always walked: slowly, with his spear, between the boxes and the people, to the long table. He stopped beside it. He did not look at Kwan's diagrams, or Gao Min's ledger, or the King of Force with his pencil. He looked a little higher than Yuanshu's eyes.

> **He says: yes.**
>
> **He says: but not because he was asked. Because he was going to stand next to the girl on the platform anyway. Because her brother will be inside him and he goes where the boy goes. So he might as well have his hands on something useful.**
>
> **He says: a scout goes out ahead of the line.**
>
> **He says: and if he is one of the forty, someone should tell Xu Dehai he is coming, so that the old man can save him a place by stone No. 7.**

The long table was silent.

Then Roman Sterling, at the end of the long table, put down his pencil, and looked up at the tall dead captain, and said, slowly, in his heavy accented Huaxian, the thing he had crossed an ocean on a ferry to say:

"*No.*"

Old Seven turned his skull and looked at him.

"*No,*" said the King of Force again. Not loudly. Steadily. Looking up at him. "*I'm saying it so it's said. Not to stop you. So that when you put your hand on that stone, somebody in this room said no first.*"

Old Seven looked at him for a long moment.

> **He says: good.**
>
> **He says: you will do.**

---

That night, at two in the morning, he wrote the statement again.

It took him four hours. He crossed out every line at least once. He went up to the battlement at three and sat in his old place with his back to the parapet and did not write anything for an hour, and came down, and wrote one more line, and crossed it out. He read the red line on the wall out loud four times. He thought about Callum Ward, twenty-five, an F, with a sledgehammer, burning for nine seconds. About Wen Jia in a new blue jacket she had never worn. About a tall dead captain putting the hand of a sergeant flat against a black stone, and being one of the forty.

At six in the morning, he had eleven lines again.

They were almost exactly the same as the eleven he had torn up. He noticed that. He sat at the long table and looked at them and noticed it, and did not know whether that meant the first ones had been right or that he had spent four hours arriving at the same easy place by a longer road.

> **I think it means you are learning.**
>
> **I do not think learning is the same as it getting easy.**
>
> **But I am not sure. And I think you should not be sure either.**
>
> **Sterling will say no. Every time. That is what he is for.**

At the top of the sheet, above the eleven lines, before he gave it to Xiaoyu to copy for the wall, he wrote one more line. Small. Not part of the statement. For himself.

*This took four hours. The last one took eleven minutes. Ask why every time.*

Nineteen days.
