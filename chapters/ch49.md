## Chapter 49: Mister Moon

The Bureau kept Fat Tan in a safe apartment on the ninth floor of an ordinary building in an ordinary district, and he had turned it, in seven weeks, into an office.

There were ledgers everywhere. On the kitchen table. On the sofa. Stacked on the windowsill in neat towers, each one tied with string and labeled in his small careful hand. A new ledger lay open on the coffee table beside a cup of cold tea, and in it, Yuanshu saw when he came in, Tan had been writing down everything that had happened to him since the night market. What he ate. What the guards said. What was on television. How many times the lift had broken.

"Habit," said Tan, a little embarrassed, closing it. "If I don't write things down, I can't sleep."

He was thinner. The great round softness of him had gone a little loose and gray. But his small eyes were sharp, and when Yuanshu sat down across from him and said, "Tell me about Mister Moon," something in them went very still.

"I wondered when you'd ask," said Fat Tan.

---

"He came once a year," said Tan. "Always on the night of the autumn full moon. That's why I called him that. He never gave a name." He folded his hands on his stomach. "Always the same. Midnight. The back of the night market, by the pier, where the trucks come in. A black car. He'd get out alone and walk up to my table and say, 'One, please. The best you have.' Like he was buying a melon."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Forties. Very good suit. Very good shoes. Spoke Huaxian like he'd learned it from a teacher, not a mother." Tan touched his own mouth. "And very good teeth. Very white. Very even. The kind of teeth you only get if someone pays for them."

> **The man with the megaphone.**
>
> **Outside the Ossuary. The Eternal Rest Foundation protest. He had very good teeth.**

"How did he pay?"

Tan didn't answer. Instead he heaved himself up off the sofa, crossed the small room to the bookshelf, and took down a heavy old ledger with a cracked brown leather cover. He opened it on the coffee table. The middle of the book had been hollowed out with a knife. In the hollow, wrapped in a yellow cloth, lay seven small bars of gold, each one the size of a domino.

"In these," said Fat Tan. "Twenty bars a core. I kept one bar from each year." He looked at them. "My father's rule. Keep one of everything. Never spend the evidence."

---

Yuanshu picked up the first bar.

It was heavier than it looked. Warm, oddly, from the room. On its face, very small, was a stamp. A refinery mark: a circle with a wave through it. A purity number. And a serial: eleven digits.

> **I can read the serial.**
>
> **Lu's office can trace it. All seven.**
>
> **It may take a day.**

"Seven bars," said Yuanshu. "Seven years."

"Seven autumns. Seven moons." Tan sat down heavily. "The first year, he wasn't alone."

Yuanshu looked up.

"There was another man in the car," said Fat Tan. "In the back. He never got out. I never saw his face; the windows were dark. But he had the window down a crack, and it was a cold night, and he was drinking something hot. I could see the steam." He shrugged helplessly. "That's all. Steam from a cup, through a window. I wrote it down because I write everything down. *Second man in vehicle, did not disembark, hot drink.*" He looked at Yuanshu. "Is that useful?"

> **The autumn seven years ago.**
>
> **Your mother went down to the basement in the winter.**
>
> **I do not know whether it is useful.**
>
> **I would like it very much if it were not.**

---

It took Lu's office twenty-six hours.

At the end of them, Director Lu Zhengtian came to the Ossuary at nine o'clock at night with a folder under his arm and his tie pulled loose and his silver-rimmed glasses pushed up on his forehead, and went straight down to the vault without speaking to anyone, and spread seven sheets of paper on the steel table under the lamp.

"The refinery is in the Atlantic Federation," he said. "All seven bars were sold, new, in two lots, to a single bullion account at a private bank in the Federation capital. Seven and a half years ago." He tapped the first sheet. "The account belongs to a trust. The Halcyon Trust."

"Halcyon," said Yuanshu. "Halcyon Medical Supply. Loading dock B."

"The same Halcyon." Lu tapped the second sheet. "The trust's registered agent is a law firm called Whitcombe Hale."

Yuanshu looked up.

"*Garrett* Hale," said Lu. "The silver-haired man at the Concordia office. Senior partner." He tapped the third sheet. "Whitcombe Hale is also the registered agent of the Eternal Rest Foundation. The one that printed the signs." He paused. "And of nine other trusts and companies and charities, all set up in the last eight years, all with names that sound like nothing at all."

"And the gold came from where?"

Lu's mouth went thin.

He laid the fourth sheet on the table. It was a bank record. A single transfer, seven and a half years ago, of an enormous sum, from one account into the Halcyon Trust's bullion account. The paying account's name was printed at the top in plain black letters.

*Meridian Dynamics. Advanced Systems Division. Payment for services: reference architecture, Project WARDEN.*

---

They called Doctor Kwan from a Bureau phone, at midnight, on a line that went through three countries.

She answered on the first ring. She had been waiting by the phone, she said, every night, since the rain. Her voice was small and tight across the distance.

Yuanshu told her.

The gold. The trust. The serials. Seven bars. Seven cores. Twenty bars each, one a year, carried by a man with very good teeth to a night market in Lingzhou and paid to a bone broker for the best he had. The money that bought the cores had come from Meridian Dynamics, as payment for the thing at the center of WARDEN.

For a long time, there was no sound on the line at all.

"The reference model," said Doctor Kwan at last. "The prediction layer. It remembers people." Her voice cracked. "I said that. I said that to you, at your door. *It's as if it had known a great many people, a long time ago, very well.*"

"Doctor Kwan..."

"Wait." A sound of typing. Fast. Frantic. "Wait. I have... I kept a copy of the diagnostic logs. Of the first layer. Nobody's allowed to open the first layer. It's sealed. It came sealed. We were told it was proprietary." More typing. "I've never run a deep read on it. I never thought..."

The typing stopped.

On the other end of the line, very faintly, through three countries and a thousand kilometers of sea, Yuanshu heard her begin to cry.

"There's a core," said Doctor Kwan. "At the bottom of it. Not a simulation. Not a model. A real one. A law core, wired into the base layer, feeding it." She was crying openly now, and talking through it, the way a scientist does, reading out the numbers because the numbers are the only thing that will hold still. "Full of voices. Thousands. I can see the signature. WARDEN doesn't predict what people will do. It *asks* them. It asks the people in the core what someone like that would do, and they answer, and it calls it a prediction." She drew a breath that shook. "Every prediction we've ever made. Every awakener we've ever profiled. Every insurance risk. Every border flag. Every demonstration. We've been asking stolen people questions for seven years, and calling it math."

---

Nobody in the vault spoke.

Lu had taken his glasses off and was holding them in his hand without cleaning them. Old Seven stood by the round steel door with his flame gone white.

> **Seven years.**
>
> **WARDEN is not a machine that watches the dead.**
>
> **It is made of them.**

"Doctor Kwan," said Yuanshu quietly. "In forty days, on Concordia, Meridian is going to stand on a stage and show WARDEN predicting what I'll do. In real time. In front of the whole world. As proof the Data Act works."

"I know."

"I said I wouldn't use your satellite data. Because the people in it never said yes." He looked at the seven small gold bars on the steel table, each with its tiny stamp, its circle and its wave. "This is different. This is your work. Your diagnostic. Your logs. Your own system, that you built, and what you found inside it." He paused. "If you stand up on that island and say it yourself, with your own name, then it's yours to say."

The line was quiet for a long time.

"They'll destroy me," said Doctor Kwan. "Meridian. Hale. They'll say I'm unstable. They'll say I falsified it. They'll take my house and my career and my name."

"Yes. They'll try."

"And you can't stop them."

"No." He didn't soften it. "I can stand next to you while they try. So can a King. So can a general with one arm. So can a million people with candles." He paused. "But you'd be the one standing up. So you're the one who decides."

A long pause. A breath.

"Put me in your book," said Doctor Iris Kwan. Her voice was still shaking, but under the shaking something had set, like water turning to ice. "Under whatever the column is for *yes, I'll speak.*"

---

Fat Tan looked at the photograph for less than a second.

It was a still from the Ossuary's front-door camera, the night of the protest. A crowd of matching jackets, a sound truck, and in the middle, half turned toward the camera with a megaphone at his hip, a tall man in his forties in a very good coat, smiling at someone off screen with very white, very even teeth.

"That's him," said Tan. "That's Mister Moon."

"We ran his face," said Lu, very tired, from the window. "Bureau records. Border records. Concordia records. Interpol. Nothing. No name, no passport, no birth, nothing. He doesn't exist." He paused. "So I asked Doctor Kwan to run it through WARDEN."

Yuanshu turned.

"WARDEN has watched the entire planet for eleven years," said Lu. "Every phone. Every camera. Every face in every crowd. Every flare of every awakener. Kwan says it's never once failed to match a face." He looked at the photograph. "It has no record of him. None. Not one frame. In eleven years of watching everything, WARDEN has never seen this man."

The vault was very quiet.

"That's not possible," said Fat Tan.

> **It is possible.**
>
> **A thread can only find a mind it has learned. A mind it has not learned, it cannot find.**
>
> **WARDEN is built on a core. It sees the world the way the core sees it.**
>
> **And there is one kind of mind that a harvested core would never, ever learn to see.**

*The one holding the scythe,* thought Yuanshu.

He looked down at the face in the photograph. The very good coat. The very white teeth. The smile, aimed at someone just off the edge of the frame.

He did not know who the smile was for. Some protester. Some passerby. A man in a sound truck.

But he found that he could not stop wondering whether, just out of shot, somebody had been standing at the edge of that crowd in a gray cardigan, holding a cup of tea.
