## Chapter 248: The Hand

Port Halvard smelled of diesel and fish and the cold Atlantic, and at four in the morning on the day the hand came, fifteen thousand people were standing round its harbor in the dark, and not one of them was talking.

He came down out of the sky onto the end of the north pier with Lingyue and Qin Hao behind him and the gold road going out over the water to the west, empty now, rolling itself up. The dead had come down it an hour before. He could not see them. Nobody could. That was the point. Nineteen thousand of the dead of Blue Star, banked, cold, dim, standing on the roofs of the warehouses and the cranes and the fish market and in the narrow streets behind the harbor, among the living, and the living did not know they were there unless they looked very carefully at a shadow that should not have been that shape.

The Awakening Hall was at the head of the harbor. Square. Granite. Built by the Federation forty years ago to look like a bank, Kwan had said, the way the Ossuary had been. Its roof was gone. Sterling had taken it off two days ago, with Force, in one piece, and set it down in the car park, so that nothing would be between the stone and whatever came down the thread.

And the line. Out of the doors, down the steps, along the quay, round the harbor, up past the fish market and onto the coast road and out of sight. Fifteen thousand people in coats, in the dark, one behind the other, each with a hand on the shoulder of the person in front. Sterling had arranged it. He said a stone could only be touched by so many at once, but a line could be as long as a line liked, and if everyone in it was holding on to the one in front, and the one at the front was holding the stone, then everyone in it was holding the stone.

> **It works.**
>
> **I did not think it would. It is like the Lattice. Each holds the one in front. The holding goes down the line into the stone.**
>
> **It is very bright.**

---

Sterling was at the stone.

Not holding it. Standing beside it, in the roofless hall, under the open sky, with his scarred hands in the pockets of his gray raincoat and his feet apart on the granite floor. The black stone stood beside him with a crack round its base you could have put your arm in. Twenty-six centimeters out of the ground before the holders came. Zero since.

At the front of the line, with her bare hand flat on the stone, a woman of fifty in a dock worker's jacket. Short gray hair. A face like the faces of all the dockers on the radio from that city: flat, hard, used to shouting over engines.

She looked at him when he came in. She did not take her hand off the stone.

"*You're him,*" said Callum Ward's mother. "*You talked to my son.*"

"*Yes. This morning. And yesterday.*"

"*What did he say?*"

"*He asked if you were still holding.*"

Her face did not move. But her hand on the stone pressed a little flatter.

"*Tell him yes,*" she said. "*Next time. Tell him I said: what do you think.*"

---

> **There.**

It was 4:41.

He looked up. Everyone in the roofless hall looked up, and then everyone on the quay, and then the whole line, all the way round the harbor, fifteen thousand faces turning up into the dark.

At first there was nothing. The sky over the Atlantic, black, cloudless, full of stars. And the thread, which nobody could see but Seed and Kwan's laptop: going up out of the crown of the stone, through the roofless hall, into the dark. Taut. Humming.

Then a star went out.

And another. Not falling. Covered. In a line, straight up from the hall, one after another, the stars over Port Halvard going out as something came down in front of them along a thread they could not see. Something they could only see by what it hid.

Something with fingers.

---

It was the size of the harbor.

When it came low enough that the lamps along the quay began to light its underside, people screamed. Not many. A few. And then stopped, because the person behind them had a hand on their shoulder and the person in front had a hand on the stone, and nobody let go.

A hand. There was no other word for it, the same way there had been no other word for the tree. Gray. Silver-gray, the color of walker-thread, the color of the rings on two people's hands. Pressed thread on pressed thread, thick as cables at the wrist where it slid down the single humming line from the dark, and then opening, below, into a palm the width of the harbor mouth, and from the palm, fingers. Not five. Hundreds. Long, jointed, slowly uncurling, the way a fern uncurls, reaching down over the hall and the quay and the warehouses and the fish market and the long line of people in coats.

And from every finger, finer, millions of threads, like the roots of a willow over a river.

Reaching for the bright.

---

> **Now.**

The dead struck.

He gave them the fire. Down through the staff, into the Death Law, all forty-eight point eight percent of it, and out through the bone of the staff and the bones of nineteen thousand dead the way water goes through a riverbed. Pale fire. Lingyue's light and his Law wound together. She put her hand on the staff beside his as he did it, and it went pale, the way it had on the island and over Lingzhou and a hundred times before.

On the roofs of the warehouses and the cranes and the fish market, nineteen thousand rusted weapons went pale at once.

The hand could not see them. It was reaching for the bright, for fifteen thousand people in a line, and between the people and the fingers, on every roof and every crane, stood the dead, banked, invisible, ash-gray, and as the fine threads came down past them toward the living, the dead cut them. One each. Spears and swords and shovels and lengths of anchor chain and one pick, burning pale, cutting thread.

The first ten thousand threads fell. Silver-gray, coming apart, drifting down over the harbor like ash.

> **Kills.**
>
> **They count. Every finger the dead take apart is thread with a weave in it. Like walkers.**
>
> **Level 69 → 70.**

The hand did not stop.

It did not flinch, the way the branch had groaned and pulled back. It did not feel the cuts. He understood that with a cold, clear certainty: it had no more feeling in its fingers than a glove. The threads that were cut fell. The threads beside them came on. Millions of them. And the dead could cut one each.

And it was pulling.

---

He felt it in the stone before Seed said anything. Everyone did. The whole line, all fifteen thousand, felt it at once, in the shoulder of the person in front: a heave. Up. As if the black stone in the roofless hall at the head of the harbor had been grabbed from above by a hand the size of the harbor and lifted.

The crack round its base opened. Granite ground on granite.

Callum Ward's mother set her feet.

Behind her, all the way round the harbor, fifteen thousand people set their feet. He heard it. A sound like a wave going back down a shingle beach.

The stone stopped.

Did not go down. Did not go up. Hung there, held, in a crack in the floor, between a hand the size of a harbor pulling up and fifteen thousand people with a little holding opened in each of them pulling down.

> **They are holding.**
>
> **They cannot hold long. The pull is ten times what it was last night. It is pulling with everything it has, because it is here.**
>
> **Six minutes. Perhaps seven.**

Roman Sterling took his hands out of his pockets.

---

He had said, on the vault steps in Lingzhou, that Force was deciding for things. That you did not ask the wall. That a King of Force was a man who had become very good at deciding for things very quickly.

He stood in the roofless hall beside a black stone that was being pulled out of the ground by a hand from the deep water, with fifteen thousand people holding it down behind him and a woman whose son was burning at the front of the line, and put one enormous scarred hand flat on the top of the stone. Not to hold it. To decide.

"*No,*" said Roman Sterling.

He said it to the stone. He said it the way he had said it on the platform in Lingzhou, after Yuanshu read the statement: quietly, clearly, so that it was said.

"*I decide you stay,*" said the King of Force. "*Nobody asked you. I know. I'm saying it out loud. I'm deciding for you, and I don't like it, and I'm doing it.*"

The air in the hall went heavy. Heavier than it had ever been in Lingzhou with forty-one children holding. It went heavy the way the air goes heavy when a King who has spent fifty years deciding for walls and buildings and men with knives decides, with everything he has, that one thing will not move.

The stone went down a centimeter.

> **...he is holding it.**
>
> **Not like the holders. They are pulling down. He is deciding it does not go up. It is not the same thing.**
>
> **It will cost him.**

"*I know,*" said Sterling, through his teeth. Blood was coming out of his nose. "*Put it on the wall.*"

---

The hand let go of the stone.

All at once. The pull went out of the thread and the stone dropped back the centimeter into its crack and the whole line, fifteen thousand people, staggered forward with the release, and caught each other, and held.

It had not given up.

It had felt what was holding the stone down. And it was reaching for that instead.

The fingers came down. All of them. Hundreds of vast jointed fingers the width of streets, slowly, out of the dark over the harbor, toward the line, and from every finger the millions of fine threads, falling on the people like rain.

And the dead could only cut one each.

He saw the first ones taken. Down the quay, by the fish market, where the line bent: a man in a fisherman's sweater, and a girl behind him, and the woman behind her. The fine threads came down on them past the roofs and the dead cut what they could and it was not enough, and the threads went into them. Into their hair, their shoulders, their chests. The way the tip had gone into the people on the wall at Lingzhou.

Not tasting. Taking.

The man in the fisherman's sweater went bright. For a moment. The best thing he had ever felt; he saw it on his face. And then the threads went taut, and the man went up. Out of the line. Out of the coat, which fell, empty, onto the quay. Up, burning, into the palm of the hand, the way Callum Ward had gone up the thread at Port Halvard six weeks ago.

The girl behind him had had her hand on his shoulder. She was holding his empty coat.

> **Forty.**
>
> **Sixty.**
>
> **It is taking the holders. It is going down the line.**
