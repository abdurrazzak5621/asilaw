## Chapter 115: The Lighthouse Keeper

Port Halvard was gray, and it was raining, and the lights were going out.

That was the first thing Yuanshu noticed, from the window of the Council car as it came down the long hill into the city in the late afternoon. Not all at once. Here and there. A street of narrow brick houses, and halfway along it, a block of windows going dark, then flickering back. A traffic light at a crossroads, dead, with a policeman in a yellow raincoat standing under it waving cars through by hand. A row of shops by the harbor with candles in the windows.

"Rolling blackouts," said Lu, beside him, reading from his tablet. "Every night. Four hours. The coal plant up the coast was damaged in a Rift surge two years ago. The Federation never rebuilt it. There wasn't money." He looked out at the dark street. "The hospital runs on diesel. They ration it. The children's ward first."

In the front seat, Lingyue had not taken her eyes off the window since they crossed the city line.

Old Seven sat in the back of the car behind them, folded nearly double, his skull touching the roof. He was looking at the candles in the shop windows.

---

Hearth One stood on a headland at the edge of the harbor, beside a lighthouse.

It was beautiful. That was the worst thing about it. A low curved building of white stone and glass, half sunk into the cliff, with a garden of salt grass around it and a path of pale gravel leading up to the door. It looked like a museum. Or a chapel. Or a very expensive hospital for very rich people.

Beside it, the lighthouse. Old. Red and white, the paint peeling. Its great lamp dark.

Cassia Lowell was waiting at the door.

She came down the gravel path in the rain without an umbrella, in her plain dark suit, with her short silver hair going flat against her skull, and held out her hand.

"Mister Zhou," she said. "Thank you for coming. I know what you think of this. I'd like you to see it anyway."

Her hand was warm. Her grip was firm. Her face, up close, was exactly as it was on the screen. Calm. Intelligent. Kind.

> **Her heart rate is normal.**
>
> **She means it.**
>
> **She means every word.**
>
> **Yuanshu, she is like Gu. Not the thread. The kindness.**
>
> **Real. All the way through.**

---

Behind her, in the doorway, filling it, stood a man.

Huge. Heavy-shouldered. A shaved head and a silver beard. Yuanshu had seen him on Concordia, standing behind the Atlantic Federation's chair with his aura sitting on the crater floor like a weight on a drum. On the night of the rain, sweeping his arm across the air and blowing a hundred threads to tatters.

The Atlantic Federation's King.

"Roman Sterling," said Cassia Lowell. "Force Law. He guards the facility." She smiled slightly. "He insisted on meeting you himself."

Roman Sterling looked down at Yuanshu from the doorway for a long time. Then at Lingyue. Then, longest, at the skeleton unfolding itself out of the back of the Council car, two meters ten of white bone, with a spear in a three-fingered hand.

"I was on the crater," he said. His voice was a low rumble, like a truck engine idling. "When it rained. I saw what you did. On the table."

"I remember."

"I've never seen anyone do anything that stupid." He stepped aside to let them through. "Or that brave. I still don't know which."

---

The sphere was in the center of the building.

They came down a long curved corridor of white stone, with windows looking out at the gray sea and the dark lighthouse, and the corridor opened into a round room. High ceiling. Glass dome. Rain running down it. And in the center of the room, on a pedestal of polished steel, inside a ring of steel taller than a man, the sphere.

It was smaller than he had expected. Perhaps a meter across. Gray. Cloudy. Cracked all over with fine dark lines, like old ice, like the crystal of the cores.

It was not glowing. It sat there on its pedestal inside its steel ring, perfectly still, perfectly dark.

And Yuanshu felt it the moment he walked into the room.

> **...Yuanshu.**
>
> **It is a seed.**
>
> **Not like a seed. A seed. From a Hunger. The same as the one that fell into the bay at Port Abiri.**
>
> **It is asleep.**
>
> **It is very, very hungry.**

He stopped in the doorway.

Behind him, Old Seven stopped too. The gray flame in his chest had gone small and hard and very bright, the way it went before a fight.

Lingyue's hand was on her sword.

---

"It's inert," said Cassia Lowell. She had come to stand beside him, looking at the sphere with an expression he could not read. "Completely. Our scientists have run every test. It has no will. No hunger. It's a vessel. A lens. It takes what's given to it, and turns it into light."

"Where did you get it?"

She hesitated. Just for a second.

"A private supplier," she said. "Through a trust. The Federation acquired it through proper channels. Everything was documented."

"Which trust?"

Another hesitation. Longer.

"I'd have to check," said Cassia Lowell. "I didn't handle the procurement. I handled the Charter compliance." She looked at him. "Which has been flawless. I promise you. Every volunteer asked. Witnessed. Recorded. I personally reviewed..."

"I believe you," said Yuanshu.

She stopped.

"I believe you," he said again. "That's what frightens me."

---

The volunteer's name was Walter Ashby, and he was eighty-one years old, and he had kept the Port Halvard lighthouse for forty-three years.

They brought him to the round room in a wheelchair, an hour later. A small thin old man with a face like a dried apple and enormous knotted hands and an oxygen tube under his nose. Lungs, the nurse said quietly. Forty years of salt air and lamp oil and cigarettes. A few days now. Perhaps less.

He looked at Yuanshu with very bright, very pale blue eyes.

"You're the one," he said. His voice was a wheeze. "The one who asks. I saw you on the telly. On the table, on that island."

"Yes."

"Good." Walter Ashby nodded. "Wanted you here. Don't trust anyone else to do it properly."

---

They left them alone. Yuanshu asked for that. Cassia Lowell agreed at once. Roman Sterling stood outside the door of the round room. Lingyue stood beside him. Old Seven stood inside, by the door, very still.

And Yuanshu sat on a plain chair beside the old man's wheelchair, under the glass dome, with the rain running down it and the dark sphere on its pedestal behind them, and asked.

"Why?"

The old man looked up through the glass at the sky. Gray. Raining.

"Forty-three years I kept that light," he said. "Every night. Up the stairs, a hundred and twelve of them, trim the lamp, wind the clockwork, watch it turn. So the boats could find their way in." He coughed, a long wet rattling cough that bent him double in the chair. When it stopped, he wiped his mouth with a handkerchief and folded it away. "Federation shut it down nine years ago. Said ships have satellites now. Said it cost too much." He looked out through the glass at the dark lighthouse beside the building. "Last spring, a fishing boat came in at night in a storm. Satellite went down. Couldn't see the harbor mouth. Hit the rocks under the point. Three men." He was quiet. "I watched it from my window. Couldn't do a thing. Light's been dark nine years."

He looked at Yuanshu.

"The Secretary says if I burn, it'll light the hospital. The children's ward. Ten years." He nodded at the dark lighthouse. "And I asked her: and that? And she said yes. That too. Every night." His pale blue eyes were very bright. "I kept a light forty-three years so people could get home. I'd like to keep one more."

---

Yuanshu did not answer at once.

Then he told him.

All of it. Carefully. Slowly. The way you'd explain something to someone who had asked you to tell them the truth.

About the fire inside a Hunger. About billions of minds burning, screaming, for longer than the sun had been lit. About a child of the Asking World who had sat at the bottom of a crack for four billion years. About the Sixth, who learned to burn low. About a lock that some Hungers had and some did not.

About the word *before.*

"Revocable before death," said Yuanshu. "That's what the Secretary said. Before. After you're in the sphere, nobody can ask you if you've changed your mind. There's no lock. There's no door. Once you're burning, you burn." He kept his voice level. "Forever. Or until the sphere breaks. Whichever comes first. And nobody knows what it feels like from inside. Not really."

Walter Ashby listened without interrupting. He did not cough once. When Yuanshu finished, he sat looking at the dark sphere on its pedestal.

"Does it hurt?" he said finally.

> **Yes.**

It was Seed. Out loud. Through the air. In its quiet white voice. It had never spoken out loud to a stranger before, except the dead.

The old man turned his head, slowly, looking for where the voice had come from.

> **I am sorry. I am the voice in his head. My name is Seed.**
>
> **I was built out of four thousand one hundred and thirteen people who were burning. In a core. Before a woman found them and asked them if they wanted to stop.**
>
> **I remember it. I did not know I remembered it until this week.**
>
> **Yes. It hurts.**
>
> **Not like a fire on your skin. Like being a fire. Like everything you are being pulled out of you, a thread at a time, and turned into light for someone else, and you cannot stop it, and you cannot ask anyone to stop it, and there is no one to ask.**
>
> **The first thing that burns away is the yes.**
>
> **You remember that you chose. But you cannot remember why.**

---

The round room was very quiet.

Rain ran down the glass dome. The dark lighthouse stood beside the building in the gray. The sphere sat on its pedestal, cracked and cloudy and still, and somewhere inside it, very deep, so deep that only Seed could feel it, something turned over in its sleep.

Walter Ashby sat in his wheelchair with his enormous knotted hands folded in his lap, and looked at the sphere. Rain ran down the dome over him.

Then he looked at Yuanshu.

"I still want to," he said quietly.

Yuanshu closed his eyes.

"I've thought about it," said the old man. "Every night for three weeks. Since I signed. I knew it would hurt. I didn't know how much." He looked out at the lighthouse. "I still want to. I've been useless nine years. I'd like to be a light again." A pause. A long rattling breath. "But I'll tell you what."

Yuanshu opened his eyes.

"You come in with me," said Walter Ashby. "After. Into that thing. However you do it. And you ask me again." His pale blue eyes did not waver. "If I say yes, I meant it. And if I say no..." He looked at the sphere. "Then you get me out. Lock or no lock. You find a way. You promise me that, and I'll sign the paper a second time, here, in front of you."

---

Yuanshu looked at him.

"I don't know if I can get you out," he said. "I don't know if anyone can. That's the truth."

"I know," said the old lighthouse keeper. "I'm asking if you'll try."

> **He means it.**
>
> **Yuanshu. He means every word.**

"Yes," said Yuanshu. "I'll try."

Walter Ashby nodded once. Then he held out one enormous knotted hand.

Yuanshu took it.

"Good," said the old man. "That's how you do it properly."
