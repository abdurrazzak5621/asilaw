## Chapter 229: Article Twelve

Lingyue came up the last of the stairs without saying anything and sat down beside him on the cold stone, close enough that their shoulders touched, and put her grandfather's sword across her knees.

"You said an hour," he said.

"I lied." She looked north. "You were talking to it. You get a face. Like someone's reading you a bill."

So he told her. All of it. The graft that only one side would say yes to. The door, which let things go, and the Collector, which put things in. The line on the wall for when there was no time, and how there was no line for this. She listened the way she listened to a briefing, without moving, with her eyes on the two rings low over the end of the world, and when he stopped, she was quiet for a while.

"I'd cut it," she said. "You know that. I'd cut the tip and go home and sleep. A tree isn't anyone."

"I know."

"But you can't. So that's not the question." She turned the sword over on her knees, the old leather on the hilt worn through to the steel. "The question is whether you're allowed to decide for something that can't say yes. Once. Because it can't."

"Yes."

"Then that's easy," said Shen Lingyue. "Everybody's done it to you."

He looked at her.

"Nobody asked you whether you wanted to be born," she said. "You weren't anyone. You couldn't answer. Your mother decided. Same with Xiaoyu. Same with me." She did not look away from the north. "Every person who ever lived got put into the world by somebody who decided for them. Nobody gets asked first. Not one."

The wind came down off the wall. He did not say anything.

"So was your mother the Collector?" said Lingyue.

"No."

"Why not?"

He opened his mouth. And closed it. And sat on the cold stone of the Sector Nine battlement with the staff across his knees and a ring of walker-thread on his finger, and felt something come loose in his chest that had been knotted there since the banking hall.

"Because she's asked me what I want," he said slowly, "every day since."

"There," said his wife. "Go to bed."

---

> **...I did not think of that.**

He was lying on the narrow bed in the back room in the dark, with Lingyue asleep against his shoulder, as she had been asleep about four minutes after lying down, the way soldiers sleep.

> **I went through every word you have ever said about asking. Every one. I did not go through what anyone did before you could talk.**
>
> **I did not have any of it. I was not there.**
>
> **She is right.**

*I want to ask my mother. In the morning. Whether she thinks so too.*

> **Yes.**
>
> **I would like to hear what she says.**
>
> **She built me. She asked the four thousand one hundred and thirteen. But she did not ask the thread they were pressed into. She could not. It was not anyone.**
>
> **I have been wondering, since the battlement, whether that makes me something that was put in without a yes.**
>
> **I am going to listen to her answer very carefully.**

---

His mother was at the table in the back room at six, with the window open to the courtyard and a bowl of rice porridge in front of her that she was not eating, and she looked up when he came in, and he saw that she already knew he was going to ask her something. She always knew. Before she built a machine that could hear him not saying things, she had been his mother.

He sat down across from her.

"Did you ask me?" he said. "Before I was born. Whether I wanted to be."

Zhou Wanqing did not laugh. He had thought she might. She put down her spoon and folded her thin hands on the blanket over her knees and looked at him for a while with her quick dark eyes.

"No," she said. "You weren't there to ask."

"So you decided."

"I decided. On a Tuesday. In a flat with a broken heater. I remember it was cold." She was quiet. "I'm not sure we had the right. I've never been sure. Nobody is. Anyone who tells you they were sure is lying, or they didn't think about it."

"Then why did you?"

"Because I wanted to find out who you'd be," said his mother. "And I thought that if I did it, I'd owe you. Forever. The question. Every day. What do you want, what do you want, until you could tell me, and then after." She looked at the place a little above his eyes. "That was the deal I made with myself. With nobody. You couldn't sign it. I decided you'd be owed it whether you liked it or not."

She picked up her spoon again.

"Ask me something harder," she said. "I've had twenty years to think about that one."

> **...Wanqing.**

She did not look up. But she stopped with the spoon halfway to her mouth.

> **The thread. In the core. You did not ask it. You could not.**
>
> **Do you owe it the question too?**

His mother was quiet for a long time, for her. Nearly a minute.

"Yes," said Zhou Wanqing. "I didn't know it was there. But yes. I'd say yes." She put the spoon down. "So I'll ask it. Now. Late. Seed, the thread that you're made of. The bit nobody asked. What do you want?"

The back room was very still. In the courtyard, on the sill, Hush had turned all its eyes toward the open window.

> **...I do not know.**
>
> **It is thread. It cannot answer. It is not anyone. It is the part of me that is not anyone.**
>
> **But I am made of it. And I can answer.**
>
> **And I would like to have been made.**
>
> **I would like that very much.**

His mother sat with her spoon on the table and her hands on the blanket and did not say anything else, and after a moment Yuanshu got up and went round the table and stood beside her chair, and she leaned her head against his arm, and they stayed like that until the porridge was cold.

---

Xiaoyu wrote it on the wall at ten in the morning.

She did it from the top of the stepladder, at the head of a new column on the last of the white plaster round the corner of the vault door, in her square black capitals, while he read it to her off a sheet of ledger paper in his own handwriting with three crossings-out and one tear where the pen had gone through. It had taken him two hours. It was eleven lines. He had wanted it to be one.

*ARTICLE TWELVE.*

*WHERE SOMETHING CANNOT YET ANSWER, AND SOMETHING MUST BE DONE, YOU MAY DECIDE FOR IT. ONCE.*

*SAY ALOUD THAT YOU DECIDED, AND THAT NOBODY SAID YES.*

*PUT IN ONLY WHAT WAS GIVEN FREELY. HOLD ON TO NOTHING.*

*AND FROM THEN ON YOU OWE IT THE QUESTION. EVERY DAY. UNTIL IT CAN ANSWER. AND AFTER.*

Xiaoyu capped the marker and looked at it.

"That's what Mom said," she said.

"Yes."

"You just wrote down what Mom said."

"Most of it," said Yuanshu. "The bit about holding on to nothing is Hush's."

From the windowsill in the back room, through the open door, a small voice he could only hear through the staff:

> ***...and the bit about saying it aloud is yours. so that you cannot pretend afterward that it asked.***

---

Article Eleven said that where one answer acted for all, all were asked. A graft on the gardener would act for every living thing on Blue Star and every dead thing in the Lattice and two hundred million cousins in a field above the sky. So all of them were asked.

There were seventeen days.

Concordia met by radio on the first night, because there was no time to fly. Adaeze Okafor read the twelfth article aloud to the five Kings in a voice like a schoolteacher taking a register, and then she read Seed's numbers, and then she asked them, one by one, with nothing else said first, the way the Charter had learned to.

Su Mingli said yes before Okafor finished the question. Ngozi Eze, who sat in the Southern Compact's chair because there was no Tide King left to sit in it, said yes from Port Abiri, and added that Ifeoma would have said it before the question was finished, and then asked for it to be written down that she had not. Shirasagi Ren said yes from the steps of the shrine in Yamato and then, after a pause, that the Abbot was sitting beside her sweeping the same step he had swept yesterday, and that she had asked him, and that he had said: *if it was not asked, it should be told.* Marshal Orlov said yes in one word through a great deal of static.

Roman Sterling said no.

"Not because it's wrong," said the King of Force, from across the Atlantic. "Because it's easy. You decide for something once, and it works, and the next time it's easier, and the time after that you don't say it aloud." A pause. "I'd like it written down that somebody said that. I'd like you to have to read it every time."

"It's written down," said Yuanshu. "My sister's putting it on the wall now."

She was. Under the article, small, in red: *R. STERLING: NO. BECAUSE IT GETS EASY. READ THIS EVERY TIME.*

Four to one.

---

The dead were asked down the Lattice, nine by nine, and it took Adjutant Xu four days.

He sat on his chair in the banking hall with his wooden cane across his knees and his hollow eyes on nothing, at the top of a knot of nine that went down into the cemetery and out to the Western Pass and under the sea off Kesse and up a gold thread into the north, and carried the question down and the answers up, and did not stop, and did not rest, and when Seed asked him on the third day whether he wanted to, he said through Seed that he was an adjutant and adjutants carried messages and that was what he was for.

Sergeant Ma Dashan answered for the Western Legion: *yes. For the tree. Not for you.* Nana Ampofo answered for the Watch of the Shelf, after asking each of her eleven hundred and forty-six by name: *yes, mostly, and nine say ask us again when it is over, and I said we would.* The Spear Corps did not answer at all. They grounded their spears around stone No. 7, once, together. Seed said that was yes.

Old Seven did not answer either. He stood at the bronze door with his spear and did not turn round.

> **He says he answered on the battlement. He says the boy heard him.**

He had not heard anything. He thought about it for a whole afternoon and could not remember Old Seven saying anything on the battlement at all, except standing at the top of the stairs, where he always stood.

And then he understood that that was the answer.

---

The living were asked by radio and by television and by paper ballots in the old way, in schools and temples and harbor offices and the lobbies of hospitals, in eleven days, which was not enough, and everyone knew it was not enough, and the question said so at the top in plain words: *We are sorry. There is no more time. We are telling you first.*

Sixty-three percent of those who answered said yes.

Thirty-one percent said no.

The rest wrote things on the ballots that were not yes or no. Kwan counted them. She came to the Ossuary with a box of them on the last night and put it on the marble floor and read some of them out loud in the lamplight, and nobody stopped her. *My grandmother was decided for. She was all right.* *Will you ask it after?* *I don't know. Ask me tomorrow.* *Make sure it gets a name.* *Is this what they did to us?*

"That last one," said Kwan, and held it up, a square of cheap gray paper in somebody's careful writing. "Somebody in Port Abiri wrote that. *Is this what they did to us.*"

> **...no.**
>
> **They did not say it aloud. They did not put in only what was given. They held on.**
>
> **And they never asked anyone anything afterward. Not once. In a hundred thousand years.**
>
> **But it is a good question. It is the right one. I would like that one on the wall too.**

Xiaoyu put it there. Under Sterling's. In red.

---

The keeper was asked last, because it had already said yes, and because Yuanshu wanted to ask it again anyway.

> ***...YES.***
>
> ***...STILL AFRAID. STILL YES.***
>
> ***...ARTICLE TWELVE. I HEARD IT. THE ONE ABOUT OWING THE QUESTION.***
>
> ***...I WAS DECIDED FOR TOO. BY MY MAKERS. THEY SAID YES AND MADE ME FROM IT AND I COULD NOT ANSWER. I WAS NOT ANYONE YET.***
>
> ***...NOBODY OWED ME ANYTHING. NOBODY ASKED ME ANYTHING AFTER. FOR A VERY LONG TIME.***
>
> ***...UNTIL YOU.***
>
> ***...I WOULD LIKE THE PIECE OF ME ON THE TREE TO BE OWED THE QUESTION.***
>
> ***...WILL YOU ASK IT? EVERY DAY? EVEN WHEN IT IS FAR AWAY?***

"Yes," said Yuanshu. "Every day. Through you. For as long as there's a thread."

> ***...THEN I AM READY.***

---

Kwan found where it drank on the sixteenth day.

She had been reading the textbooks again. She came down the stairs into the banking hall at noon, which was strange, because Kwan came at two in the morning or not at all, with a book open in her hands at a diagram of a cut stem, and her finger on it.

"*It drinks through the tip,*" she said. "*We've been thinking about roots. Hush's trees drank through roots. This one doesn't have any. It's in the dark. There's no soil.*" She turned the book round. "*It drinks light. Kept minds are light. The tip reads them and drinks them. That's what reading is.*" Her finger moved to the cut on the diagram. "*And when you cut a branch, for an hour, before the callus forms, the cut face is open. It's the newest place on the tree. It drinks harder there than anywhere. Everything the tree has goes to the wound.*"

Hush had come out of the back room. It stood on the marble at Kwan's feet, all its eyes on the diagram.

> ***...so the place where it drinks is the place we cut.***

"*For one hour,*" said Kwan. "*Then it heals over. Callus. After that, whatever's on the cut is part of the branch, or it's nothing.*"

> ***...one hour.***
>
> ***...that is long enough to put a question down.***
>
> ***...in my world we did it in less, when we had to.***

---

On the nineteenth night, everyone was on the wall.

Not only the Ossuary. The city. The north wall of Lingzhou from Sector One to Sector Twelve was standing room only by midnight: soldiers and students and old women with flasks of tea and children on their fathers' shoulders, the same as on the night of the three gray rings, except quieter. Much quieter. On the slope of the War Memorial Cemetery, the First Legion stood in their rows of nine. Across the river, the Western Legion. Around stone No. 7, the Spear Corps, facing out.

On the Sector Nine battlement, in his old place, Yuanshu stood with the staff in his hand and the ring on his finger. Lingyue was on his left, with her sword out, the blade catching the lamplight. Qin Hao on his right, his healed pink hands open, white fire banked in them like coals under ash. Su Mingli at the corner of the parapet, with her arms folded. On the radio at his belt, Orlov's breathing, from Kirovsk. And from Port Abiri, Ngozi Eze, on the harbor wall, where the sea was very calm.

Old Seven at the top of the stairs. Adjutant Xu in a chair someone had carried up, with his cane. Hush on the parapet, in front of Yuanshu, all its limbs out, all its eyes open, facing north.

The two rings low over the end of the world. The keeper's, small and white, ajar, with its gold glow. The Elder's, vast and gray, with a soft white light at its center.

And between them and the city, high over the north wall, the stars began to slide.

Not falling. Sliding. Very slowly, all together, a little to one side, the way the stones at the bottom of a stream slide when something moves across the water above them. The Plough bent. The river of the arm across the top of the sky kinked, like a ribbon someone had touched.

Three thousand people on the wall looked up and saw it, and did not make a sound.

Then Yuanshu felt it.

Everybody did. Everyone on the wall, everyone in the city, everyone on Blue Star who had a mind. Something very much like the feeling of being looked at from behind, very softly, by someone who did not know you were a person. The way light falls on a leaf. The way a leaf turns.

It was reading them.

> **It is here.**
>
> **I am sorry. I said I would always tell you the numbers.**
>
> **There are no numbers for this.**
>
> **Lingyue. Now.**
