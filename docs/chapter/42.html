Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 42Silence Shifts

czverse7 min read

The house was called the Listening Pavilion, which everyone agreed was a very bad joke and nobody wanted to change.

It stood at the end of a narrow lane in the old quarter of Lingzhou, where the river bent and the streets were still paved with gray stone worn smooth as soap. Two stories of dark wood with a curved tile roof and a small walled garden: a pond, a maple, a bench, moss. A hundred years ago it had been a teahouse. Thirty years ago it had been left to the Quiet in the will of an old woman who had lost three sons in the Black Tide and asked only that it be kept as a place where nobody had to speak.

Fourteen beds stood in its upper room now, in two rows, under the dark wooden beams. Clean white sheets. The monitors from the Institute ward, with their sounds turned off. Paper screens over the windows, so the light came in soft and gray and even.

And beside each bed, on a low cushion, sat one of the Quiet.

Not doing anything. Not reading. Not praying. Just sitting, with a candle on the floor in front of them, and their hands in their laps, and their eyes half closed.

In perfect silence.

It is working.

I have been watching the threads for six hours.

Fourteen threads. Going up through the roof. And for six hours, they have carried nothing at all.

Not a word. Not a cough. Not a thought loud enough to bend a flame.

It is the quietest room I have ever been in.

Yuanshu stood at the top of the narrow wooden stairs and did not go in.

He had been told not to. Sister Ning had told him, very clearly, at the bottom of the stairs, in a whisper. You think too loudly. Even standing still. We can all hear it. Stay on the stairs.

So he stood on the stairs, and looked through the open door at the long dim room. At the old man with a shaved head sitting beside the young woman with braided hair. At the girl of about sixteen beside Private Liang from the Western Pass, her hands folded, her eyes closed. At the man in the delivery uniform from under the bridge, who had come every day since, sitting beside the fourth bed on the left with a candle in front of him, perfectly still, keeping watch over a Northern boy he'd never met.

The candles stood straight in the still air. Fourteen small gold flames.

They worked in shifts. Four hours on, four hours off, through the day and the night. The Quiet had more than enough people. Two thousand had stood under the bridge, and when Sister Ning said, We need people who can sit beside a stranger for four hours and not say anything, not even in their heads, the list had filled in an afternoon.

"It's what we do," Sister Ning said, in the garden, on the second day, very quietly, so that the sound wouldn't carry up through the tile roof. "Sit with people. In silence. Funerals. Hospitals. Prison cells. We've been doing it for forty years." She was sitting on the stone bench by the pond with her candle unlit in her lap, watching the koi. "We just never had anyone who could tell us whether it helped."

"It helps," said Yuanshu, just as quietly.

She looked at him for a while.

"How do you know?"

Tell her.

"The boy in the fourth bed," said Yuanshu. "The ember at the bottom. Every night in the Institute, it was the same size. For eighteen days." He looked up at the paper screens of the upper room. "In here, in two days, it's grown. A little. Like a coal when you stop the wind."

Sister Ning closed her eyes.

Then she nodded once, and opened them, and went back to watching the fish.

On the fourth night, Seed noticed the other thing.

Yuanshu was sitting on the bottom step of the stairs, in the dark, at three in the morning. He came most nights now and sat there for an hour, not thinking. Trying not to think. It was harder than any training he had ever done. Old Seven stood in the garden by the maple, his gray flame turned so low it was almost invisible.

Something is coming down.

He went still.

The threads. They carry up. That is what they are for.

But in this silence, with nothing going up, I can feel something faint coming down the other way.

Not words. Not a voice.

A pulse.

From the lens?

From whatever is at the other end. Yes.

It is very faint. Like feeling a heartbeat through a wall.

I have been counting.

And?

It comes every 31 hours and 14 minutes.

Or it did, three nights ago. Last night it came after 31 hours and 9 minutes.

Tonight, 31 hours and 3 minutes.

It is getting faster.

Yuanshu sat on the dark step and felt the cold go into him, slowly, from the stone.

Like what?

A long pause.

I am trying to find a gentler comparison.

There is not one.

It is like contractions.

Something up there is preparing to open. Fully. And the intervals are shrinking at a steady rate.

How long?

At the current rate of change, the interval reaches zero in approximately four hundred and ten days.

I want to be careful. That is a straight line drawn through four points. Real things do not usually move in straight lines.

But it is the best number I have.

Four hundred and ten days.

Out in the garden, a koi broke the surface of the pond with a small soft splash, and the ripples spread out across the black water and lapped against the moss.

He told Su Mingli at dawn, at the gate of the pavilion, in a whisper.

She listened without speaking, the way she always did. When he finished, she looked north for a long time, over the curved tile roofs of the old quarter, toward the wall.

"Four hundred days," she said.

"About."

"That's what Grid Nine was, then. And the Western Pass. And the cores in the market." She spoke very quietly. "Samples. Testing the field before the harvest." She was silent for a while. "Thirty-one years ago, the Black Tide came three days after the lens was seen. One scout. And it nearly killed a nation."

"This is the whole lens."

"This is the whole lens." She turned to him. "Does anyone else know?"

"You. Me. Seed." He hesitated. "Whoever's on the other end."

"Keep it that way. For now." She looked up at the paper screens of the upper room, where fourteen people breathed in their beds and fourteen strangers sat beside them in perfect silence. "If this gets out before the Charter, the Atlantic Federation will say the world is ending and that's exactly why they need WARDEN and every memory you've ever read." Her mouth tightened. "And they'd be half right. Which is the worst kind of argument to fight."

On the sixth night, Sister Ning let him go upstairs.

"Ten minutes," she whispered at the bottom of the stairs. "Think about nothing. If you can't think about nothing, think about something boring. Think about rice."

He went up.

The upper room was dim and warm and smelled of candle wax and clean linen. The fourteen watchers did not look up as he came in. He walked slowly down the aisle between the beds, on soft socked feet on the old wooden boards, and stopped at the fourth bed on the left.

The man in the delivery uniform looked up at him. Then, without a word, he shifted along his cushion, and made room.

Yuanshu knelt beside him.

Andrei Belov lay on his back, looking at the dark beams of the ceiling. His face was the same. Pale. Narrow. Empty. His hand lay on top of the sheet.

Yuanshu did not reach. He did not ask. He didn't think anything at all, or tried not to. He thought about rice. Very carefully. Grain by grain.

He put his hand next to the boy's hand on the sheet. Not holding it. Just next to it. Touching at the edge.

For a long time, nothing.

Then, so faintly that he would never be completely sure he hadn't imagined it, the boy's little finger moved.

A millimeter. Less. It curled, very slightly, toward Yuanshu's hand, and touched it, and stayed.

The man in the delivery uniform saw it. His eyes went wide. And then he squeezed them shut and pressed both hands over his mouth, hard, so that he wouldn't make a sound.

Nobody in the room made a sound.

Fourteen candle flames stood perfectly straight in the still air.

Nothing went up the thread.

Nothing at all.

That was only him.

On the way out, at the bottom of the stairs, Sister Ning was waiting with her candle.

She looked at his face, and didn't ask.

"Four hundred days," she said instead, so softly he barely heard it.

He stopped. "Su Mingli told you."

"Su Mingli didn't tell me anything." The old woman nodded up the stairs, at the dim upper room. "You thought it. Six nights ago. Sitting right here on this step." She almost smiled. "I told you. You think very loudly."

She held out her candle, and lit a new one from it, and put it in his hand.

"Then we'd better make the most of the quiet," she said.
