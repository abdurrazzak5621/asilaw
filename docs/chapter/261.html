Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 261Loud

czverse10 min read

His mother knew before they were through the bronze door.

She was at the long table in the banking hall at nine in the morning with a cup of tea and Gao Min's newest ledger, the one with the column headed What they said, reading it the way she read the names on the wall every morning, one at a time, with her finger. She looked up when they came in. At him. At Lingyue, a step behind him, with her grandfather's sword on her back and her hand, without her noticing, flat on her stomach under the gray sweater.

Zhou Wanqing put down her cup.

"How long?" she said.

Lingyue stopped walking.

"How did you..."

"I had two," said his mother. "I know what a woman's hand does." She looked at her son. "And you've got the face. The one you had when you were nine and you'd broken the rice cooker and didn't know how to tell me." She folded her thin hands on the ledger. "How long?"

"Ten weeks," said Lingyue. "About."

Zhou Wanqing sat in her wheelchair at the long table under the lamp for a moment without saying anything at all. Then she held out both her hands. Not to her son. To his wife.

Lingyue went round the table. She did not kneel; she was not that kind of girl. She stood by the wheelchair and let the small woman in it take both her hands, hard, and hold them.

"Nobody asked me either," said his mother. "When I was born. My mother decided. On a Thursday, she said. I asked her once." She did not let go. "I'm going to ask this one what it wants every day it's alive. And so are you. And so is he." She looked up at Lingyue's face. "And when it's old enough to say leave me alone, Grandmother, we'll ask it whether it wants us to stop."

"Yes," said Lingyue.

"Good," said Zhou Wanqing. "Now sit down before you fall down. You flew across an ocean pregnant. I heard."

Xiaoyu came down the stairs at ten past nine, heard, and screamed.

Not a word. A sound. The kind of sound a girl of seventeen makes when she has been told she is going to be an aunt, which is, it turned out, much the same sound as a SCRIBE being shouted at the ceiling of the banking hall by Hu Dabao. The pigeons in the upper windows went up all at once. Fat Tan, in the back room, dropped a teapot.

Then she stopped, on the bottom step, with her hand over her mouth, and went very serious.

"I have to write it," she said. "I have to write it on the wall."

"Not yet," said Lingyue.

"Why not?"

"Because it's not anyone yet," said Shen Lingyue. "It can't say whether it wants to be on a wall. Your brother's article." She sat down on the stairs. "You can write it when it can say yes. Or no. Or ask me tomorrow."

Xiaoyu looked at her for a long moment with the marker in her hand. Then she capped it, and put it in her pocket, and sat down on the step beside her sister-in-law, and put her head on her shoulder.

"That's going to be years," she said.

"I know."

"I'm going to write it in my head every day until then."

"That's allowed," said Lingyue.

They told the world on the third day.

Not from the Awakening Hall, this time, but from the floor of Concordia, on its volcanic island, in front of the five chairs, because Adaeze Okafor said this was Concordia's or it was nobody's. He told them what Ichiro had told him on a breakwater in Yamato. The quiet ones in the deep water. The Lamplit, with their stones and their ladder of marks and their ships that folded, who had fought the one who picks for ten thousand years and had seen a Lord-bright flare in the Shallows and could count. The Singers, who had sung their brightest up a thread every age to be spared, and had sung this age, and been answered by nothing, and were afraid.

Both coming. To look.

He did not say what they should do. He said what Article Eleven told him to say. That he did not know yet. That he was telling them first.

And then he sat down, and the five Kings argued for six hours.

"We hide," said Roman Sterling, by radio, from a desk by a window in Port Halvard, where he was allowed to sit for four hours a day and decide for nothing bigger than a door. "We've done it before. Lanterns. The whole world. We're good at it."

"We hid from rings," said Su Mingli, from her chair, with her arms folded. "For a night. This is a civilization with ships."

"Then we hide for longer."

"They saw him burn," said Marshal Orlov, in his one-sentence way, through a great deal of static. "You cannot hide what has already been seen."

Shirasagi Ren said nothing for the first five hours. In the sixth, she said, in her calm voice: "The quiet ones in the deep water have hidden for a thousand years. A thousand thousand, Ichiro says. They are very good at it. They are alive." A pause. "They are not anything else. They do not talk. They do not burn. They do not ask. They drift in the dark, together, being quiet." She looked at the boy in the chair at the end of the row. "That is what hiding is, when you do it long enough. I sat with three thousand hollows on a mountain for a year. I know what it is to be quiet for so long that you forget what you were being quiet for."

Nobody answered her.

"Ngozi," said Adaeze Okafor.

The woman in the Southern Compact's chair, who sat there because there was no Tide King left to sit in it, had not said anything at all. She sat now with her hands flat on the table in front of her, a tall, serious, careful administrator from the capital who had been Ifeoma's deputy minister of the coast for nine years.

"Ifeoma would have said: we don't hide," said Ngozi Eze. "She would have said it before the question was finished." She looked down the row. "I'm not Ifeoma. I'd like it written down that I thought about it for six hours." A pause. "We don't hide."

It went to the world. Article Eleven. Forty days would have been the old way; there were not forty days, Ichiro had said months, perhaps less, so it was ten. Down the net to the dead, slow, nine by nine. Over the radio and on paper ballots to the living, in schools and temples and harbor offices. And to the keeper, and the cousins, and the eleven in the stones, and Shiomi in the fog of a bay in Yamato, because a world that asked everyone asked everyone.

The question said, at the top, in plain words, the way Seed had learned to write them: Two kinds of people from very far away are coming to look at Blue Star. We do not know what they want. We will either hide, the whole world, every night, for as long as it takes, the way we hid from the rings. Or we will light the lamps and let them see us. We are telling you first.

Kwan read the answers out loud in the banking hall in the evenings, the way she always did. Fifty-nine percent said light the lamps. Thirty-four said hide. The rest said other things.

We hid in the Black Tide. In cellars. For a week. I was six. I don't want my children to do that.

If they're like us, they'll want to be asked. If they're not, hiding won't help.

Ask Shiomi. They know about hiding.

Shiomi had answered. Ichiro rang it in the fog-code, through Ren, and Sister Kiyo translated, dryly, from the end of the breakwater with the lamp in her lap:

"Shiomi says: we hid for nine hundred years. It worked. We are alive."

"Shiomi says: we came home because someone lit a lamp."

"Shiomi says: light the lamps."

...I have run it.

He was on the vault steps. It was the eighth night. Seed had waited until no one was looking at him.

Hiding. Not hiding. Every way I can think of.

I do not know what the Lamplit will do. I have never met them. I have a description from a man who was quiet in the deep water for nine hundred years and talked to the fish. That is not very much data.

But I know one thing.

The Collector kept the wants of a great many of them. It told Xiaoyu. Last night. She asked it what it wanted, and it said it did not know yet, and then it told her, because it tells first now, that it has kept more wants from the Lamplit than from anyone else in the Shallows. More than the Architects. More than the cousins.

What did they want?

It said: to be first.

It said: almost all of them. The same want. Every one. To be first. To be at the top of the ladder. To be brighter than the one beside them.

It said: it was the easiest want it ever kept. It did not even have to ask. They told it before it asked.

He sat with that for a long time.

That's what the stones did to them, he thought. Ten thousand years of being marked.

Yes. I think so.

Gao Min says the same. She says if you mark people for ten thousand years, they stop wanting anything except the mark.

She says it is the saddest thing she has ever heard. She says she will put it in a paper. She says nobody will read it.

On the tenth night, the answer came back from the net of the dead.

Not a percentage. It never was. A pile of index cards on the long table, and on top, the one that had gone round six hundred times. Kwan read it.

"Stone No. 7," she said. "The Spear Corps. They said: we were lanterns in the Black Tide. On the wall. The whole eastern line. We lit the braziers so the city could see where we were. So the beasts would come to us first." She turned the card. "Light the lamps."

"And the rest?"

"Yes. Yes. Light the lamps. Grid Nine says they'd like to see the ships. The First Legion says a wall is a wall, lit or not. The Third Battalion..." She stopped. "Sergeant Huo says: we're at ease. But if someone's coming up the slope, wake us."

Concordia sat on the eleventh morning, and voted, and it was four to one, and the one was Sterling, who said by radio that he would like it noted that he had said no first, and that he would light the lamp in his own window anyway.

That night, at two in the morning, Kwan came down the stairs into the banking hall with the laptop held open in front of her like a tray of hot food, and he knew.

"It's early," she said.

He had been on the vault steps. He had not been sleeping. Lingyue was asleep upstairs, and his mother, and Xiaoyu, who had gone to bed at one after asking the Collector what it wanted and writing on the back of Gao Min's hand NIGHT 75. NOT YET. TOLD ME ABOUT THE LAMPLIT. THEY WANT TO BE FIRST.

Kwan turned the laptop round.

The keeper's eye. The Shallows. Seed's map: the arm, the twelve gold dots of the fields, the gray husks of the pruned ones, the faint dark place where the branch was, with its small gold point at the end. And near the keeper's own small white ring, low over the end of the world, very close to Blue Star, closer than anything had been since the three gray rings, a mark.

Not gold. Not gray. Not red.

A crinkle. The way the surface of water crinkles when something has been dropped into it from very high up, and the rings go out, and in the middle, for a moment, the water folds.

...that is not a ring.

That is not a hand. That is not anything I have a name for.

Something folded.

Something came through the dark between there and here without going through it.

"Months," said Yuanshu. "Ichiro said months. Perhaps less."

"It's been eleven days," said Kwan.

It is very small. The size of a building.

It is not moving.

It is very bright inside. Bright the way the stones were bright. Bright the way Port Halvard was bright, for five minutes.

And Yuanshu.

He waited.

It is full of people.

Not the way a ship is full of people. Not walking about. Not talking.

Held.

In the walls. In the engines. Packed. Pressed. Burning. The way the miners were in the cores at Heishui. The way Mrs. Pan's husband was in Number Three shaft for two years, screaming, before we let him go.

It is a ship made of kept people.

And it is looking at us.
