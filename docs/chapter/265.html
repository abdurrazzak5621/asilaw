Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 265Again

czverse10 min read

The boy who came off the road first was called Tam, and on the third day, he asked to be taken to the stone.

He asked Gao Min. Everyone asked Gao Min; she had made herself the person the Candle's children asked, the way she had made herself the person holders asked, by sitting at a table at the bottom of the War Memorial Cemetery with a ledger for three days and writing down every name and every age-inside and every place they had been taken from, and not once writing down a grade. When a child tried to give her one, she put her pencil down and said, in her flat careful voice, we don't write that here, and waited until they gave her something else.

Tam had given her his name, and the name of a world, Corraine, one of the fuel orchards on the inner worlds, and the number nineteen, which was how many years he had been in the wall.

And on the third day, he came back to her table and said, in the Federation's language, which Seed was teaching Gao Min a word at a time:

He says: the stone. The one that asks. Can I see it.

He says: on Corraine, the stone read me when I was twelve and said F. And then they took me to the ship. He says the stone is the last thing he saw before the wall.

He says: he would like to see one that asks. Before he decides whether he wants to touch it.

They went on the fourth morning. Not all nine thousand five hundred and fifty-nine. Gao Min said that would frighten the stone, and Wen Jia, when asked, said through the staff that it would not frighten her, but it might frighten the children, to see so many of each other in one place again, all in a line. So they went in groups. Tam's first. Forty of them. Barefoot still, most of them, because shoes were strange; in clothes from the city, too big; holding each other's hands, because that was how they had come down the road.

The Number Nine bus took them. Wen Hao would not let anyone else. He stopped outside the Ossuary at 7:12 and opened the doors and counted to ten, and forty children of twelve who were nineteen and thirty and forty-four inside got on his bus and sat down very carefully on the seats, the way people sit who have not sat on anything but the inside of a wall for years. And he drove them up East Ring Road in the morning light, slowly, past the trees, and stopped outside the Awakening Hall, and opened the doors, and counted to ten again, out loud, so that the children could hear him.

Tam asked him why. Seed translated.

"My sister's in there," said the driver of the Number Nine bus. "She asks the kids what they want to be. I count to ten so she knows it's me."

Tam looked at him in the mirror.

He says: is your sister a core?

"She was in a sack," said Wen Hao. "Thirty years. Then a basement. Then a boy's head. Now a stone." He looked at the boy in the mirror. "She chose the stone. Nobody put her there."

The hall was quiet.

It was never empty now. The holders had stopped needing to hold, but some of them still came, in the mornings, and stood in a short line up the aisle with their hands on each other's shoulders, because they had got used to it. They made room when the forty came in. Nobody told them to.

The three lines of light still hung in the air over the platform. [Class: Scribe] [Chosen.] [Talent: — not marked —]. The black stone stood under them with its crack and its runes pointing down and a bright blue jacket laid over its shoulder.

The children stopped in the aisle.

He watched them stop. Forty twelve-year-olds in clothes too big for them, holding hands, at the bottom of the aisle of the Awakening Hall, looking up at a black stone ten meters high. The last thing they had seen before the walls. Seed said that every one of them was shaking. Seed said that most of them did not know they were.

Tam let go of the hand he was holding, and walked up the aisle alone.

He stopped at the foot of the platform steps. He did not go up. He stood there and looked at the stone. At the runes, turned down. At the jacket. At the crack.

He says: it's the same.

He says: the stone on Corraine. It's the same stone. The same black. The same runes. He says he would know it anywhere.

He says: it's warm. The one on Corraine was warm too.

He says: but the runes were pointing at me. These are pointing down.

"There's someone in it," said Yuanshu. He had come up the side aisle and stopped a little way off. "Her name's Wen Jia. She was seventeen when she was taken. She never got to touch one. Now she's in it. She asks."

Tam looked at the runes pointing down into the stone.

He says: can she hear me?

...yes, said Wen Jia, through the staff, from the knot, to the hall. ...I can hear you. Hello. You're the boy who came off the road first. The bus driver told me. He tells me everything.

Tam did not move. Seed said he had stopped breathing.

...you don't have to touch me. Lots of people don't. There's a woman in a stone on the other side of the world who counts the ones who don't, and she says it's more every month, and she says that's good.

...but if you want to, I'll ask you something. I'll tell you first: whatever you say, I'll open that. Not what I read. What you say. And if you don't know, I'll say come back when you do.

...that's all. That's all I do.

He stood at the foot of the steps. The other thirty-nine at the bottom of the aisle did not move. The holders up the aisle did not move. Gao Min, on the bottom tier with her ledger, did not move.

Then Tam went up the three steps of the platform.

Slowly. The way the children walked who had come down the road. He stopped in front of the stone, under the three lines of light, and looked at it from very close.

And then he said, in the Federation's language, quietly, to the stone, the thing Xiaoyu had said on Awakening Day, which he could not have known, because nobody had told him:

He says: hello. I'm Tam. From Corraine. I'm going to put my hand on you.

He says: I'm telling you first.

And he did.

[What do you want to become?]

The letters formed in the air above the platform, under Xiaoyu's three lines, in the hand that was not the stone's. Seed had taught Wen Jia the Federation's script in a night. She wrote it in that.

Tam looked up at it.

He did not say anything for a very long time. His hand stayed flat on the stone. The letters hung in the air.

He says: on Corraine it said F.

He says: it didn't ask. It said F. And then a man in gold came and said the honor, and my mother cried and took the money, and they put me in the ship.

He says: I was in the wall nineteen years. In the first array. Next to a girl from Halloran who sang. Not out loud. You can't, in the wall. Inside. She sang inside for nineteen years and I listened.

He says: she's still up there. In the Candle. She said yes, to carry it home. She wants to see her mother.

He stood with his hand on the stone.

He says: I don't know what I want to become.

He says: I was going to be a fisherman. On Corraine. Before. That's what my father was. I don't know if I want that now. I don't know what anything is here.

He says: I've only ever been one thing.

[Then come back when you know. I will be here.]

Tam looked at the letters for a moment.

He says: no. Wait.

The letters waited.

He says: I know one thing.

He says: I want to be able to hear the ones in the walls. The way the boy did. The way the thing in his head did. Not to take them out. I don't know how. Just to hear them. So that someone does. So nobody's ever in a wall again with nobody listening.

He says: is that a becoming?

The stone was quiet for a moment.

...yes, said Wen Jia. ...that's a becoming.

[Talent: — not marked —]

He felt it open. Very small. A little, the way the old knot said, OPEN IT THAT WAY, A LITTLE. Not a Law on the tree inside the stone. Something the boy had said. Wen Jia opened what he said.

...it is like mine, said Seed, very quietly. A little. The listening part. Not the counting.

She has given him a little of what I do. The part that hears the ones nobody hears.

I did not know that was a thing that could be given.

Tam took his hand off the stone.

He stood there for a moment. Then he turned his head, slowly, as if he had heard something a long way off, and looked up. Not at the letters. Past them. At the roof of the hall, and past the roof, at the sky, and past the sky, the north, the small white ring, and beside it, a crinkle, two hundred and twenty meters long, with thirty-two thousand and forty people in its walls, burning low, in turns, breathing.

His face did something.

He says: I can hear her.

He says: the girl from Halloran. In the first array. She's singing. She's still singing.

He says: she's singing because she's going home.

At the bottom of the aisle, the other thirty-nine had let go of each other's hands.

Not all at once. One. Then another. A girl, then two boys, then more, walking up the aisle of the Awakening Hall one at a time toward the platform, in their clothes too big for them, barefoot on the polished stone floor, to stand in a line at the foot of the three steps, one behind the other, the way the holders stood, with their hands on the shoulders in front.

Not because they had to. Because, a girl told Gao Min afterward, through Seed, that was how you stood when you wanted to do something and were frightened. You held on to the one in front.

Gao Min wrote that down. In her new ledger. In the column that said What they said.

They went up one at a time. All forty. Some touched it and some did not. Some said I don't know and the stone said come back when you do. Some said things nobody had ever said to a stone before. I want to swim. I want to grow things. I want to be a mother. I want to be bright on purpose, just once, for something good, and then stop. A girl of twelve who was forty-four inside put her hand on it and said, through Seed, very clearly: I want to be graded again, so I can see it's wrong. And the stone was quiet for a long time, and then wrote:

[I do not grade. But I will tell you what you are, if you want. You are someone who came down a road of light and asked.]

And the girl stood there with her hand on the stone and cried, and said, yes, that, that's what I wanted, and the stone wrote [Chosen] and nothing else, and opened nothing, because she had not asked for anything to be opened. She had asked to be told.

That night, in the banking hall, Xiaoyu wrote at the top of a new column, on the last of the white plaster round the corner of the vault door:

THE CANDLE'S CHILDREN. 9,559. CAME DOWN THE ROAD.

TAM, FROM CORRAINE. 19 YEARS IN THE WALL. CAME OFF THE ROAD FIRST. WENT UP THE STEPS FIRST. CHOSE: TO HEAR THE ONES IN THE WALLS.

And under it, smaller, because Gao Min had read it to her out of the ledger and she had said it had to go on the wall:

THAT IS HOW YOU STAND WHEN YOU WANT TO DO SOMETHING AND ARE FRIGHTENED. YOU HOLD ON TO THE ONE IN FRONT.
