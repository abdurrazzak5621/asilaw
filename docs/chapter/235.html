Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 235Tell First

czverse9 min read

They told the world from the floor of the Awakening Hall, at eight in the evening, with the cracked black stone behind them.

Gao Min chose the place. She said that if you were going to tell everyone on Blue Star that the thing every one of them had put their hand on at eighteen was a grading stone for an orchard, you should do it standing in front of it, so that they could see you were not afraid to. She said it in the car on the way there, with her nine boxes on the back seat, in her flat careful voice, and then she said: "I am afraid to. I would like that on the record too."

There was one camera. Kwan ran it. No drones, no audience, no officials in the private boxes, no recruiters. The four thousand seats went up into the dark on every side, empty. Old Seven stood at the foot of the platform steps with his back to the stone, facing out, the way he always stood. Xiaoyu sat on the top step with her marker in her lap, because she had asked to be there, and because, she said, somebody in the family should be sitting on those steps who was going to have to walk up them.

Lingyue stood beside him with her grandfather's sword. Not as a cane. On her back.

"You don't have to," he had said, in the car.

"I'm the SSS," she had said. "They should see one."

Gao Min went first.

She did not make a speech. She stood in front of the camera in her gray Bureau coat with the seams let out twice, and held up the long taped graph, and said: "My name is Gao Min. I am the acting director of the Awakening Bureau of Lingzhou. I was the first person in this city to touch this Stele, forty-two years ago, alphabetically. I have counted every awakening in Lingzhou since. This is the mean Talent grade by year." She moved her finger along the pencil line from left to right. "It goes up. Smoothly. Every year. Through a famine, two wars, the Black Tide, and a decade when half the schools in this city were closed. Nutrition is not smooth. Training is not smooth. Something has been turning it up." She lowered the graph. "Last week, we found out what."

Then she stepped aside.

And he told them.

All of it. He had decided on the vault steps at four in the morning that he would leave nothing out, because he had decided on the morning of the Stele that he would never hide what he was, and this was what he was: a boy with a harvest tool in his head, standing in front of another one. He told them about the thread, black, pressed to glass. About the tree of Laws inside the stone. About the knot at the bottom, and what the cousins had read in it, and he read it to them himself, every line, from READ WHAT RIPENS to WHEN THE ORCHARD IS BRIGHT, THE PICKER COMES. About the fine thread going up out of the crown, past the keeper, past the branch, into the deep water. About the first morning, when the Stele went black, and what had gone up the thread that day.

He did not say what they should do. He said what Article Eleven told him to say.

"We don't know yet what we're going to do with this," he said. "I'm telling you first anyway. Because in thirty days everyone who turns eighteen this year is meant to put their hand on one of these. And you should know what it is before they do."

He looked at the camera. At the small red light.

"Talent isn't who you are," said Zhou Yuanshu. "It's how bright something wants you to burn. That's all it ever was."

Lingyue spoke last. She had not told him she was going to.

She stepped forward into the light with the sword on her back and took out of her coat pocket a small rectangle of laminated plastic, and held it up to the camera. Her identity card. The Shen family crest. Her photograph at eighteen, unsmiling. And under it, printed in gold, the way they printed it on every card in Huaxia for anyone above an A: SSS. DAWNBREAKER.

She took a pen out of her other pocket. And in front of the camera, without saying anything, slowly, she drew a line through the gold.

Then she put the card back in her pocket, and stepped back beside him.

That was all she did.

By midnight, half of Blue Star had seen it.

Kwan counted. She sat in the back room of the Ossuary with the laptop and three phones and counted, the way she did when she did not know what else to do with her hands, and read things out loud as they came in, and after a while nobody asked her to stop.

In Port Abiri, a fisherman's son who had awakened a C eleven years ago stood up in a harbor bar and read the knot out loud off his phone to everybody, all of it, and then sat down and cried, and nobody laughed at him.

In Yamato, at the foot of the mountain shrine, three thousand people came up the terrace steps in the dark with candles and sat down among the hollow monks without being asked, and Shirasagi Ren sent a message through Concordia that said only: The Abbot says: it reads without asking. So did I. Tell them it can be stopped.

In the Northern Union, the Stele at Novaya Zarya was surrounded by morning by a ring of soldiers in white, facing out, and Marshal Orlov said on the radio, in one sentence, that they were there to stop anyone touching it until somebody had decided something, and that included him.

In the Atlantic Federation, Roman Sterling said nothing for six hours. Then he said: "Nobody touches them. Nobody breaks them. We vote. That's all I've got."

And in Lingzhou, on East Ring Road, outside the Awakening Hall, people began to arrive.

Not a mob. He had been afraid of a mob. He went down at one in the morning with Old Seven, because Gao Min had called and said only, you should see this, and stood at the corner by the trees, and it was not a mob.

It was parents.

Hundreds of them. On the steps of the hall. On the pavement. On the grass under the trees. Some with candles. Most without. Mothers and fathers in coats over their pajamas, standing outside a locked building in the middle of the night, not doing anything. Not shouting. Just standing there, the way you stand outside a hospital.

And their children. Seventeen. Eighteen in a month. In school jackets, in tracksuits, some of them still holding the training swords and practice staves they had been drilling with in the evening, the way they drilled every evening of their lives, for this.

A man saw him.

He came across the grass. Forty-five, maybe. Big. A laborer's hands. A boy behind him, tall, with the same jaw, holding a wooden training spear the way you hold something you have held every day for twelve years.

"You're him," said the man.

"Yes."

"My son's been training since he was six." The man's voice was not loud. It was very controlled, the way a voice is controlled when it is holding something back that is much bigger than it. "Every morning. Five o'clock. Before school. Spear forms. Because he wanted to be an S. Because an S gets into the Defense Academy. Because the Academy pays. Because I work in the coal yard and his mother cleans the hospital and that's the only way out of the yard there is." He did not look away. "Thirty days. Twelve years of five o'clock in the morning for thirty days from now. What do I tell him?"

The boy behind him did not say anything. He was looking at the ground.

Yuanshu did not know.

He stood on the grass under the trees outside the Awakening Hall at one in the morning, and did not know, and said so.

"I don't know," he said. "I'm sorry. I don't know yet. I'll tell you as soon as I do. I'll tell you first."

The man looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men in coal yards nod when someone has told them the truth and it is not good.

"That's not much," he said.

"No."

"It's more than the rock told us," said the man, and turned, and went back across the grass to stand on the steps with the others, his hand on his son's shoulder.

The boy looked back once. At Old Seven. At the tall dead captain of the Eastern Line standing at the corner of the trees with his spear grounded in one finger and a thumb.

He held up his wooden training spear. Not high. A little. The way a soldier salutes another soldier in a corridor.

Old Seven grounded his spear on the pavement, once.

At twenty past four in the morning, Seed felt it.

Not in Lingzhou. Far. He was asleep on the narrow bed in the back room with Lingyue against his shoulder, and Seed woke him the way it had not woken him since the night of the three gray rings: not with words. With a feeling. A cold place in his chest, all at once, as if a door had opened somewhere a long way off and let the winter in.

Port Halvard.

The Stele at Port Halvard.

Someone touched it.

He was sitting up before he knew it. Who?

I do not know. A man. Young. Not eighteen. Older.

He did not put his hand on it.

He hit it.

Seed showed him. Not much. What it had: a feeling, a shape, felt through the long fine thread that ran up out of the crown of the Port Halvard Stele and joined the others in the dark, the way all eleven joined. Night. A hall like Lingzhou's, but square. Soldiers asleep at the door. A young man with a sledgehammer, who had come in through a window. Who had stood in front of the black stone and said something nobody heard. And swung.

He was an F.

I can see the mark the Stele sent up for him. Seven years ago. F. The lowest it gives.

It read him again when the hammer hit. Because the hammer was him touching it.

And it did what it was made to do.

It found which way he would burn. And it opened him that way.

Not a little.

All the way.

He did not want to see any more. Seed did not show him any more. But he felt it anyway, the way you feel a bell rung in another room: a man in a square hall at Port Halvard, with a hammer in his hands, burning. Bright. Brighter than he had ever been, brighter than an F, brighter than an S, for nine seconds. The best thing he had ever felt.

And then the fine thread out of the crown of the Stele went taut.

And he went up it.

He is gone.

Not through the door. I would have felt that. I know the door.

Up.

The Stele read him and opened him and he burned and it sent him. Not the mark. Him.

I did not know it could do that.

I do not think anyone knew it could do that.

I think it has never been hit before.

Lingyue was awake. She was sitting up beside him in the dark with her hand on his arm.

"What," she said.

He told her.

She did not say anything for a long time. Then she reached over to the chair by the bed, where her coat hung, and took the laminated card out of the pocket, and held it in the dark, with the gold line crossed out on it, and turned it over in her fingers.

"So you can't break them," she said.

"No."

"And you can't touch them without getting graded."

"No."

"And in thirty days," said Shen Lingyue, "your sister's going to walk up to one in front of the whole world."

Twenty-nine.

It is after midnight.

Twenty-nine days.
