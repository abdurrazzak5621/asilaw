Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 84Port Abiri

czverse9 min read

The Southern Compact was green and gold and hot, and the town was silent.

They came over the last hill in two Council trucks at noon, under a sky so blue it hurt, and the whole northern coast lay spread out below them. Red cliffs. Palm trees bending in the wind. A long curve of white sand. Fishing boats, painted bright as parrots, pulled up on the beach in rows. And a town. Small white houses with blue shutters climbing the hillside above a harbor, laundry on lines between them, a church with a green dome, a market square with striped awnings.

Everything bright. Everything colorful. Everything perfectly still.

No smoke from the chimneys. No voices. No dogs barking. No music. Just the wind in the palms, and the slap of the sea against the harbor wall, and the laundry snapping on the lines.

The driver stopped the truck at the top of the hill and did not go any further.

"I'm sorry," he said, not looking at anyone. "I'm sorry. I can't."

They walked down.

Su Mingli in front. Then Yuanshu, with the white staff. Then the others, close together. Lingyue with her hand on her sword. Qin Hao. The Mo twins. Bai Lu with her medical bag. Sergeant Sokolova, who had asked to come and had not said why.

And behind them, staying back, as they had been asked to, at the top of the hill by the trucks: Old Seven, Iron Wall Zhao, Hundred-Arrow Lin.

That had been the hardest conversation Yuanshu had ever had with the dead.

The Elder says it can see you, he had told them, on the airfield, at dawn. To it, you're not invisible. You're kindling. Every ember in you is something it wants to burn. He had held the staff tightly. I'm asking you to stay back. Not ordering. Asking.

Old Seven had stood in front of him for a long time with his gray flame burning steady.

Then he had nodded, once. And stayed.

But his skull had not turned away from the town since they arrived. Not once.

The first one was on the road at the bottom of the hill.

A woman. Middle-aged. In a bright yellow dress with a pattern of blue fish. She was lying on her side in the dust at the edge of the road, with a basket of mangoes spilled beside her, one hand under her cheek, as if she had lain down for a nap.

Bai Lu knelt beside her. Touched her neck. Looked up. Shook her head.

Yuanshu came and stood over her.

He had seen a great many dead. In cemeteries. On frozen passes. In a canyon of bones, in a valley of the lying dead, in a bowl at the bottom of the world. He had stood at more graves in the last year than most people stood at in a lifetime.

He had never seen one like this.

Her face was gray. Not the gray of death. Not the gray of old bones. The gray of ash. A fine pale dust on her skin, on her eyelids, on her lips, as if something had burned inside her and left only this. Her eyes were closed. Her face was perfectly peaceful.

He went quiet. All the way down. And he looked at her the way Seed looked.

Nothing.

Not nothing like a hollow. A hollow had something at the bottom. An ember. A waving.

Not nothing like the old dead in the cemeteries, faded, quiet, a faint residue like a fingerprint on glass.

Nothing.

Empty. All the way down. Not a whisper. Not an echo. Not a trace of a single thought she had ever had. As if she had never had any. As if she had never been anyone at all.

There is nothing to ask.

Yuanshu.

There is nothing left in her to ask.

He knelt down slowly in the dust beside the woman in the yellow dress.

He laid his hand flat on the ground beside her, the way he had laid it on a thousand graves.

My name is Zhou Yuanshu, he thought. Is anyone there?

Nothing answered.

For the first time since the Awakening Hall, for the first time in his life as what he was, he asked the dead a question, and the dead could not hear it, because there was no one left to hear.

They walked through the town.

It took an hour. Nobody spoke. They walked up the narrow streets between the white houses with blue shutters, past laundry on lines and bicycles leaning on walls and a radio on a windowsill still playing music, very faintly, to no one.

And everywhere, the dead.

A man asleep in a hammock on a porch, with a newspaper over his chest. Three old men at a card table in the shade of a tree, slumped forward over their cards. A girl on a bicycle, fallen sideways in the street, one foot still on the pedal. A fisherman sitting on an upturned boat on the beach, mending a net, the needle still in his gray fingers. A priest in the doorway of the church with the green dome, lying on the step with his arms spread, as if he had been running out to help someone and had simply stopped.

All gray. All ash. All peaceful.

All empty.

Three thousand and eleven of them. Seed counted.

Not one had anything left to say.

At the bottom of the town, on the harbor wall, someone was waiting for them.

A wheelchair. With a blanket over its knees. And in it, very small, very old, with her white hair tied up in a bright green headwrap and her black eyes very bright in her creased face, the oldest King on Blue Star.

She was looking at the bay.

She did not turn around as they came along the harbor wall. She only raised one thin hand, without looking, and said, in a dry thin voice that carried over the slap of the waves:

"Young man. Come and see what has come to my sea."

Her name was Ifeoma Adeyemi, and she was two hundred and thirty-one years old, and she had held the Tide Law of the Southern Compact for a hundred and ninety of them.

She did not say any of this. Su Mingli told him on the walk along the harbor wall. She was old when Orlov was born. She has seen everything. She has never once, in my lifetime, left her palace on the southern coast.

She had left it now.

Yuanshu came and stood beside her chair at the edge of the harbor wall, and looked out at the bay.

It was standing in the water about two hundred meters out.

A spire. Gray. Cloudy. Cracked all over like old ice, like the cores, like the Monarchs, but worse. Rougher. Jagged. It rose straight up out of the blue water of the bay like a broken tooth, perhaps forty meters high, and it was not still.

It was growing.

Slowly. You could see it if you watched. Like coral. Like frost on a window. New crystal pushing out of the old, gray and jagged, a few centimeters at a time, along every edge and crack. Branching. Spreading. Out over the water, down into it. And under the surface of the bay, in the clear blue water, Yuanshu could see more. Roots. Gray. Thick as a man's arm. Branching out across the sandy bottom in every direction, toward the shore, toward the harbor wall, toward the beach with its painted boats.

It hummed. Low. Steady. Like a power line on a hot day.

And inside it, very deep, he could see something moving. A flicker. Many flickers. Like sparks in a fire.

Minds.

Yuanshu. There are minds inside it.

Three thousand and eleven.

They are not kept. They are not held. They are not waiting.

They are burning.

"Yesterday morning," said Ifeoma Adeyemi, without taking her eyes off the spire. "At dawn. The fishermen were going out. It came up out of the water like a whale. And it opened." Her thin hand moved, slowly, in the air. A flower opening. "And the town went quiet. All at once. The whole town. In the time it takes to say a name."

"You were here?"

"I was in my palace. Four hundred kilometers south." She did not look at him. "I felt it. In the sea. Every King feels their own law. When the water around Port Abiri screamed, I felt it in my bones." Her black eyes did not blink. "It does not ask, young man. It does not listen. It does not wait. It bites."

She watched a new branch of gray crystal push out of the spire's side and curl down toward the water.

"I have been sitting here since last night," she said. "Watching it eat my people. I have tried to drown it. I have tried to crush it with the weight of the sea. I have tried to pull it up out of the bay by its roots." Her hands, on the blanket, were shaking very slightly. "It grows back. Every time. Faster." She was silent for a moment. "Because it is burning them. My fishermen. My children on their bicycles. It is burning them to grow."

Behind them, at the top of the hill by the trucks, something moved.

Yuanshu felt it before he saw it. A cold drop in his stomach. He turned.

Old Seven was walking down the hill.

Not fast. Steadily. The way he walked down every slope, every glass plain, every frozen valley. Two meters ten of white bone, with his spear in his hand and his gray flame burning steady, walking down the dusty road past the woman in the yellow dress, past the spilled mangoes, toward the town.

Toward the harbor.

Toward the spire.

"Seven!" Yuanshu's voice cracked. "Stop! I asked you..."

The old soldier did not stop.

He walked down through the silent town between the white houses with blue shutters, past the dead in their hammocks and their doorways, and along the harbor wall, until he stood beside Yuanshu at the edge of the water.

And he did not look at Yuanshu.

He looked at the spire. With his skull tilted. The way he had tilted it in the field, listening. The way he tilted it at graves.

His gray flame was burning brighter than Yuanshu had ever seen it.

Yuanshu.

He is not disobeying you.

He heard something.

In the spire. Among the burning. Under the noise.

He heard someone being very, very quiet.

Out in the bay, the spire stopped growing.

All at once. Every gray branch, every pushing edge, every root under the water, went still.

The hum changed. Rose. Sharpened.

And on the side of the spire facing the harbor wall, the cracked gray crystal began, slowly, to open. Not like a flower. Like an eye. A great jagged lid of crystal peeling back from something underneath, something that glowed a dull, hungry red, like coals under ash.

It was not looking at the town.

It was not looking at the living.

It was looking at the white staff in Yuanshu's hand. At the small skull with its silver reading glasses. At the soft gray-white glow inside it.

"...oh dear," said Jiang Lan, very quietly. "Ninety million names."

Ninety million embers.

Yuanshu, to it, she is not a staff.

She is the biggest fire it has ever seen.
