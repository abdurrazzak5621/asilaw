Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 70Lay Down

czverse10 min read

Gu Hanshan woke at dawn and asked for tea, and when Yan Shuang handed him a tin mug, he held it in both hands for a long time without drinking, and watched the steam.

"I keep waiting for it," he said. "The voice. The little pull. Like a tooth you keep touching with your tongue." He turned the mug. "There's nothing there."

He looked older in the daylight. Thinner. His hands shook a little on the mug. But when he looked up at the white stone, and the open shaft at its foot, and the boy sitting beside it with a white staff across his knees, his face was quiet in a way Yuanshu had never seen it. Not calm. Not kind. Just quiet. Like a house with all the windows open after a long winter.

"What happens to me now?" he said.

"What do you want to happen?"

Gu almost laughed. "You really do ask everyone."

"Yes."

He thought about it. The steam rose from the mug.

"I'd like to go home," said Gu Hanshan. "And stand trial. For all of it. The cores, the hollows, the Western Pass, the mast. Every receipt with my initial on it, whether I remember signing it or not." He looked down. "I'd like to tell the truth to a room full of people, out loud, and know that the only ones listening are the ones in the room." He paused. "And I'd like to water the lemon tree. Lu's been doing it wrong. I can tell from his face."

He went south that afternoon.

Not alone. Twelve soldiers of Third Battalion stepped forward when Yuanshu asked who would walk him home. Sergeant Huo chose them. They formed up around the old man in his filthy gray cardigan, a square of gray bones and rags of greatcoats, and Yan Shuang drew him a map on the back of one of Sokolova's notebook pages, every ridge and seam between here and the wall.

At the edge of the plateau, Gu stopped and turned around.

He looked back at the white stone. At the expedition. At Su Mingli, standing with her arms folded, who had not said one word to him since the night.

"Mingli," he said.

She didn't answer.

"I did bring you soup," said Gu Hanshan. "Every day. For a month. When your back was broken." His voice did not shake. "That was me. Nothing else. Just me. I wanted you to know that."

Su Mingli looked at him for a long time across the frozen glass.

Then she nodded. Once.

Gu turned, and walked south, slowly, with twelve dead soldiers around him, until he was a small gray dot on the shining plain, and then nothing.

It took them two days to walk back to the valley of the dead.

They came over the eastern lip of it at noon on the second day, and there it was below them, exactly as they had left it. The gray river. Two hundred thousand shapes, shoulder to shoulder, filling the valley from side to side, flowing northeast across the black glass at the pace of a slow walk. Scrape. Scrape. Scrape. A long, dry, endless exhalation, rolling across the Wastes in the thin winter sun, as it had for thirty-one years.

Yuanshu stood on the lip of the valley with the white staff in his hand.

He did not go down.

"You don't need to," said Jiang Lan, from the small skull at the top of the staff. "They're all in here. Every one. I remember them. Just plant me."

He planted the staff in the frozen ground at the top of the slope, the way you'd plant a flag.

Behind him, everyone was watching. Su Mingli. Lingyue. The twins. Bai Lu. Qin Hao. Yan Shuang and Sokolova. Old Seven and seventy-two soldiers of Third Battalion in their rows. Iron Wall Zhao. Hundred-Arrow Lin.

He went quiet. All the way down.

And he asked.

It went out from the staff like a ring of light across water.

Moon-white. Soft. Pouring down the slope from the small skull, across the crushed glass of the road, into the gray river. And everywhere it touched, the dead stopped.

Not one in a thousand, this time. Not one sergeant with three fingers.

All of them.

The front of the march stopped first, kilometers to the northeast. Then the middle. Then the back, far to the southwest. The whole vast gray tide of bones coming to a halt across the valley in a long, slow wave, like a train braking, carriage after carriage after carriage. Two hundred thousand feet coming down. And then, for the first time in thirty-one years, the scrape of bone on glass stopped.

Silence.

The wind moved over the Wastes, and there was no other sound at all.

They stopped.

Yuanshu, they all stopped.

And slowly, with a sound like a forest bending in a gale, two hundred thousand skulls turned.

Not north.

Toward the lip of the valley. Toward the white staff, glowing in the frozen ground. Toward the boy standing beside it.

My name is Zhou Yuanshu, he thought, into the light, into all of them at once. The man who raised you is resting. He was sorry he didn't ask. I'm asking for him.

You've walked for thirty-one years. Nobody ever told you that you could stop.

You can stop.

Or you can keep walking. With us. North. To the place where the thing in the sky keeps the ones it took. Not to fight it. To bring someone home.

Or you can say something. To someone. Anyone. And then stop.

It's your choice. Not mine. Not his. Yours.

What do you want?

The answers came up through the staff like rain falling upward.

Not words. Most of them had no words left. Just a feeling, rising out of each gray ember, faint and tired and very, very old. Two hundred thousand feelings, all at once, pouring into the small white skull and through it into him, so many that he swayed on his feet and Lingyue caught his elbow.

I am counting.

Jiang Lan is counting with me. She is very fast. She taught arithmetic.

"Of course I'm fast," said the dry voice from the staff, very softly. "Hush. I'm working."

QUIET: 178,611.

ONLY TO SPEAK: 2,304.

YES: 19,402.

ASK MY FAMILY: 0.

There is nobody left to ask.

The two thousand who wanted to speak went first.

They did not have voices. But the staff gave them one, the way Yuanshu gave the dead a voice when he raised them, and for one long minute, the whole valley spoke.

Names. That was almost all of it. Two thousand three hundred names, said aloud into the cold wind, by two thousand three hundred voices, all at once and one after another, overlapping, rising and falling across the valley like the sound of a crowd at a railway station. Wang Lihua. Chen Bo. Little Mei. Grandma Shu. Corporal Deng of the Fourth. Zhao Ping, from the bakery on Salt Street. Not their own names, mostly. Other people's. Wives. Sons. Mothers. Dogs. The names of the people they had been walking away from for thirty-one years.

Sergeant Sokolova sat on a rock with her notebook and wrote as fast as she could, and filled eleven pages, and got perhaps a tenth of them.

"I have them," said Jiang Lan gently. "All of them. I'll give them to your Ossuary. Someone will look them up."

Then the voices stopped.

And then a hundred and eighty thousand of the dead lay down.

It began at the front of the march, far to the northeast, and came down the valley toward them like a wave going out. A long, slow, gray ripple. Skeletons folding at the knees, sinking, lying down on the crushed glass of the road they had worn into the Wastes with their own feet. Some on their backs, looking up at the sky. Some on their sides, curled, like people asleep. Some reaching out, as they went down, for the hand of the one beside them.

The sound of it was the softest thing Yuanshu had ever heard. Like snow sliding off a roof. Like a long breath out.

It came down the whole twelve kilometers of the valley. And when it reached the slope below him and stopped, the river was gone.

In its place, filling the valley from side to side, from horizon to horizon, lay a field.

A field of bones. Lying in long uneven rows across the black glass in the thin winter sun, still and pale and quiet. The largest cemetery on Blue Star. Made in a single minute, by a hundred and eighty thousand people who had finally been told they could stop.

Su Mingli walked down into it.

Nobody followed her. She walked down the slope alone, in her long gray coat, onto the crushed glass among the lying dead, and stopped beside a small skeleton lying curled on its side, holding the hand of a larger one.

She knelt.

She stayed there a long time with her head bowed, and her shoulders moved, and nobody on the ridge said anything, or looked away, or looked at her either.

When she came back up, her face was dry and calm and very tired.

"I was nineteen," she said to no one in particular. "I cheered."

Then she turned, and looked at what was left standing in the valley.

Nineteen thousand four hundred and two.

They stood scattered across the field of the lying dead like trees left standing after a fire. Gray bones in gray rags. Soldiers, mostly. But not only. A farmer with no scythe. A grandmother with a red scarf. A man in a postman's jacket. The woman in the gray wedding dress.

And slowly, without an order, they began to walk.

Not north. Not anymore. Toward the ridge. Toward the white staff. Picking their way carefully between the rows of the lying dead, the way you'd walk through a hospital ward at night, so as not to wake anyone.

They came up the slope and stopped below the ridge, and stood there, in no order at all, looking up at the boy with the staff.

Old Seven stepped forward to the edge of the slope.

He raised his spear.

And nineteen thousand four hundred and two of the dead who had said yes, who had chosen to walk with someone rather than after something, raised what they had. Arms. Hands. A rusted bayonet. A gray bridal bouquet that had been only stems for thirty-one years.

Article Seven.

The dead may defend. They may never conquer.

I told them. All of them. Through the staff. Before they said yes.

They said that was the only kind of war they had ever wanted to be in.

Sokolova's radio crackled that night.

Static. Relays. Lu's voice, thin and far away and, for the first time since they'd left, laughing. Not much. A little. The laugh of a man who hadn't slept in two weeks.

"It came," he said. "The Monarch. Last night. It came down the valley to within twenty kilometers of the wall. We could see it from the battlements. Forty meters of glass, screaming." The static rose and fell. "Sister Ning put four thousand people on the wall with candles. Sixty kilometers. Shoulder to shoulder. Thinking about nothing. Principal Yue in the middle of them in his tracksuit, eyes closed, perfectly still. I've never seen him so quiet."

"And?" said Su Mingli.

"It stood there for an hour. Facing the wall. Its threads out, feeling. Sweeping back and forth." Lu's voice was very soft. "And it couldn't hear anything. Not one thing. Sixty kilometers of people and not one thought it could find." A pause. "Then it turned around and walked back north."

Nobody on the plateau spoke.

"And Mingli," said Lu. "One more thing. At the exact moment your Mercer came apart, two nights ago. Sister Ning's people in the Listening Pavilion said Belov sat up in his bed."

Yuanshu went very still.

"He sat up and looked at the window," said Lu. "North. And he spoke. In Northern. Clear. Ma Jun recorded it." The static hissed. "Kwan translated it. It says: It has stopped looking at her. It is looking at the gate."

I counted the pulse tonight.

The big lens. In the north.

It was slowing for forty days. Since Concordia. 37 hours. 40. 43.

Tonight it was 29.

It is hurrying again, Yuanshu. Faster than before.

Mercer was right. It has seen the staff. It has seen the door.

And it is coming to look.

He sat on the ridge in the dark, above the largest cemetery on Blue Star, with the white staff across his knees and nineteen thousand four hundred and two of the dead standing quietly on the slope below him under the stars, and looked north.

On the far horizon, very faint, so faint that you could only see it if you knew where to look, a pale ring hung in the night sky over the end of the world.

"Well," said Jiang Lan softly, from the small white skull. "Then we'd better go and meet it."
