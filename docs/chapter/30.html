Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 30Data Rights

czverse7 min read

The Concordia Council kept an office in Lingzhou on the thirtieth floor of the Harbor Trade Tower, and it looked like what it was: a room built for arguments.

A long glass table. Twelve black chairs. Floor-to-ceiling windows on two sides showing the harbor, gray water, cranes, a container ship sliding out toward the sea under a low ceiling of cloud. At the far end of the table, a wall-sized screen. On it, at 9 a.m. sharp, five tired faces in five different time zones lit up in five small boxes, and a sixth box showed a crater on a volcanic island where it was the middle of the night.

The chair of the Concordia Council looked as if she hadn't slept.

"This is a preliminary hearing," she said. "Not a trial. The Atlantic Federation has filed a petition under the Provisional Accord. The Council will hear it, and hear the response, and decide whether it goes further." She rubbed her eyes. "Please be brief. All of you."

The Atlantic Federation had sent two people in person.

One of them was a lawyer named Garrett Hale, tall and silver-haired, in a suit that cost more than the Ossuary's monthly rent, with the easy, warm voice of a man who had never in his life lost an argument he had been paid to win. Yuanshu noticed the surname and saw from the way Hale sat that he had noticed Yuanshu noticing.

No relation to Dorian Hale, the assassin. I checked. It is a common name.

He knows you will wonder. He is letting you.

The other was a woman in her thirties with short dark hair and wire-rimmed glasses, who sat very straight with a closed laptop in front of her and had not looked up since she arrived. The badge clipped to her lapel said MERIDIAN DYNAMICS and, under it, Project WARDEN.

On Yuanshu's side of the table sat Su Mingli, Director Lu, Sister Ning with her notebook, and, unexpectedly, Catherine Moore. She had arrived at 8:55 in her gray suit, shaken his hand, and said, "I've left the Office of Strategic Talent. I advise the Council now. I'm not on anyone's side. I'm on the side of the Accord being read correctly." Then she had sat down.

Garrett Hale stood.

"Thank you, Madam Chair. I'll be brief, as asked." He smiled at the screen. "The Provisional Accord says no remains may be raised without the consent of the deceased, recorded by an independent witness. Recorded. That's the key word." He turned toward Yuanshu, pleasantly. "Mister Zhou has been very admirably careful about this. He's had witnesses at every event. Cameras. Notebooks. The Northern Union's embassy filmed the entire Grid Nine reading, and has kindly made the footage public." He spread his hands. "So there are recordings. Of the dead's consent. And, as it happens, of the dead's memories."

He let that settle.

"And then there is the Ossuary. Two hundred and ninety-one thousand living persons, and climbing, registering what they want done with their remains. Held, we're told, by a law that lives in a teenager's head." He shook his head gently. "The Federation's position is simple. A record created under an international Accord belongs to the Accord. Not to the person who happens to hold it. We petition the Council to require that all consent recordings, all memory readings, and the full Ossuary registry be deposited with the Concordia Council and made available to member states for safety review."

"Safety review," said Su Mingli flatly.

"Of course." Hale turned to the woman in the wire-rimmed glasses. "Doctor Iris Kwan. Meridian Dynamics. She can explain better than I can."

Doctor Kwan opened her laptop.

She did not stand. She spoke quietly and quickly, as if she would rather be anywhere else, and every word was precise.

"Meridian builds artificial intelligence. Not laws. Machines. Code running on hardware." She turned the laptop so the screen faced the room: a gray diagram, a lot of boxes and arrows. "Project WARDEN is a Federation defense contract. Its purpose is to model high-rank awakeners. To predict what they'll do. To recommend containment before a situation becomes an emergency."

"To predict me," said Yuanshu.

She met his eyes for the first time. "Yes."

"And it needs the recordings."

"It needs data." She didn't look away. "We have never had data like this. A real record of the inside of a human mind at the moment of death. Thirty-eight soldiers. Their last night. Their fear. Their decisions. What they chose to remember." Her voice did not change, but her hands had gone still on the keyboard. "WARDEN could learn more about how people think from one hour of your recordings than from ten years of anything we have. And the Ossuary is three hundred thousand people telling you, in their own words, what they want most when they're gone." She paused. "Do you understand how valuable that is?"

"Yes," said Yuanshu. "That's why you can't have it."

The room was quiet. Outside the windows, the container ship had reached the harbor mouth and was turning toward the open sea.

"Doctor Kwan," Yuanshu said. "When I read those thirty-eight soldiers, I asked them first. One at a time. They said yes. Do you know what they said yes to?"

She didn't answer.

"To telling their families what happened. Only that. I told them, 'only what happened that night, only to tell them, nothing else.'" He kept his voice level. "Thirty-eight young men who had their minds torn out of them by something that never once asked. And the first thing anyone ever asked them, after, was whether they'd like their mothers to know it only hurt for one second." He looked at the gray diagram on her screen. "If I hand their memories to a machine so it can learn to predict people like me, I've done to them what was done to them at Grid Nine. Taken the inside of them, without asking, because it was useful."

That is the thing I learned in the cold room.

The thing that harvests minds is doing what I do, at scale, without asking.

What she is asking for is the same thing. Smaller. Politer. With a contract.

"The dead are not a dataset," said Yuanshu.

Garrett Hale smiled, unbothered. "Very moving. But the law—"

"The law," said Catherine Moore.

Everyone turned. She had not moved. Her hands were folded on the glass table in front of her.

"The Accord says consent must be recorded by an independent witness," she said. "It does not say the recording becomes public. It does not say it belongs to the Council. I was in the room when it was drafted. The word recorded was chosen specifically so that there would be proof of consent. Proof." She looked at Hale. "Not a product."

Hale's smile thinned very slightly. "With respect, Ms. Moore, your personal recollection of the drafting—"

"Is in the minutes," said Catherine Moore. "Page four. I brought copies."

She slid a stack of paper across the table.

On the screen, the chair of the Concordia Council looked, for the first time that morning, almost awake.

The hearing ended forty minutes later.

The petition was not granted. It was not refused either. It was referred, which Catherine Moore explained in the elevator meant we'll fight about it for a year, at a full hearing, on Concordia, in front of everybody.

"That's not a win," she said. "That's a date."

"It's enough," said Yuanshu.

In the lobby, as they were leaving, Doctor Iris Kwan caught up with him by the revolving doors.

She stood there for a moment, holding her closed laptop against her chest with both arms, like a shield.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I asked them not to send me. I said WARDEN shouldn't be trained on anything the subjects didn't agree to." She pushed her glasses up her nose. "They said that's not how data works."

"It's how it works now," said Yuanshu.

She looked at him for a long second. Then she went out through the revolving door into the gray morning, and was gone.

At 3:47 that night, Seed woke him.

Something is wrong.

He was awake before he knew it, sitting up in the dark, the false window showing rain on the mountain.

What?

The Ossuary registry. Someone has read it.

Not all of it. 3.1%. Nine thousand names, and their choices. A single copy, taken at 3:41.

It was not taken from Tianshu. Tianshu's walls held. It was taken from the Ossuary building. From a desk terminal on the ground floor. Someone used a volunteer's access.

The Atlantic Federation.

That is what I assumed.

It is not what happened.

A pause.

I followed the copy. I wanted to know where it went.

It did not go to the Federation. It did not go across the sea. It did not go out over any network I can find.

It went up.

Yuanshu sat very still in the dark.

Up.

To a transmitter. A small one. Very old. Hidden inside a weather mast on a rooftop in the north of the city.

It sent the nine thousand names straight up into the sky. Toward the north. Toward where the lens was.

And then it shut itself off.

He was already reaching for his jacket.

Which rooftop?

I am checking. One moment.

The mast is on the roof of the Rift Research Institute.
