Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 193Look at Me

czverse11 min read

At 01:40, Xiaoyu's voice broke.

Not all at once. It had been going for an hour. It had gone from clear to hoarse to a scrape, calling eleven hundred streets every three seconds into every speaker in Lingzhou, and at 01:40, in the middle of River Gate, now, it cracked down the middle like a dropped plate and nothing came out.

She tried again. Nothing. A breath. A whisper.

Seventy-one percent.

Sixty-four.

The city is waiting for her voice. It cannot hear it.

On the marble floor of the old banking hall, in the middle of the chalk city, Zhou Xiaoyu sat with the microphone in her hand and tears running down her face and her mouth open and no sound coming out of it, and Mo Shi beside her still calling steps three seconds ahead in his flat fast voice, into a silence.

Then the wheels of a chair squeaked on the marble behind her.

Zhou Wanqing had been in the back room all night. In her chair by the window, with the blanket over her knees and a cup of tea going cold, listening to her daughter's voice on the speaker in the wall. She had not come out. Nobody had asked her to. She had said, at the start, that this was Xiaoyu's, and that she would not take it.

She came out now.

She wheeled herself across the marble floor of the banking hall without anyone pushing her, past Fat Tan, past Kwan, past thirty-one people with headphones who had stopped calling when Xiaoyu did, to the middle of the chalk city. She stopped beside her daughter. And held out one thin hand.

Xiaoyu gave her the microphone.

Wanqing did not take the headphones. She did not need Mo Shi's calls in her ears. She put her other hand on her daughter's shoulder, lightly, and Xiaoyu understood at once, the way she understood everything at once, and put her own hand over her mother's, and listened to Mo Shi, and squeezed.

Once for a step. Twice for breathe. And where, with her fingers, tapping out the street the way you tap out a message on a table. Lantern Lane. River Gate. Tram Yards. The streets she had drawn in chalk.

And Zhou Wanqing, who had once said one word into a microphone on the battlements of Sector Nine and turned three million people dark in nine seconds, lifted the microphone and said, in her quiet, sharp, slightly hoarse voice, on every speaker in the city:

"Tram Yards. Now."

Seventy-two.

Seventy-nine.

Eighty-four.

They know that voice. Everyone in this city knows that voice. They heard it say Lanterns.

On the wall, the six walkers that were left stopped stepping.

All of them. At once. He felt it the way you feel a room go silent when someone has said your name.

They did not hold. They did not turn toward the city. They turned along the wall. Toward each other. And then, one by one, the way the eleven had stepped off their threads onto the black glass that morning, they began to walk.

Not over the wall. Along it. Toward Sector Nine.

They have stopped trying to read the city.

They have learned that the city hides from them. A wave. A voice. Streets going quiet before the beam arrives.

So they are not looking at the city.

They are coming to the place where the one who asks is standing.

They know where you are. One of them read your name in the field. Three degrees. One slice. It told what sent it. And what sent it told these.

They are coming to look at the cause.

He stood on the battlements of Sector Nine with the staff in his hands and watched them come.

Six of them. Along the outside of the north wall, from the east and the west, nine meters of silver-white woven thread each, stepping over the rubble at the foot of the wall, past the silk of the ones that had already come apart, unhurried, with their blank faces turned toward him. Not stepping. Not sweeping. Just walking. Coming to stand in front of him.

Su Mingli was on her knees on the stone beside him with her face gray and blood at the corner of her mouth. She had held five faces down in one night. She could not hold another.

Orlov was at Crossing Four with his gloves off and frost all the way up both his arms to the elbow. He could not freeze another knot.

Qin Hao was unconscious in a gatekeeper's lodge at the river gate with Bai Lu's hands on his chest.

Lingyue was on the watchtower above him with her grandfather's sword. She could hold one face. Perhaps two.

Six.

I have run it.

Six lamps, in front of you, all looking at you, all covering each other's gaps.

There is no pocket. Not for you. Not for anyone living within two hundred meters of you.

The Spear Corps can take one. If its face is held. Lingyue can hold one. Perhaps two.

That is two. Of six.

I have run it eleven thousand times, Yuanshu.

He did not ask it for the number.

He looked at them coming. At the blank faces. At the lamps behind them that could read a mind three degrees at a time and hold it straight up and lift.

And he thought about a corridor at the edge of a field above the sky. A beam holding him straight up. And Seed, standing in the open behind his eyes with nothing in front of it, saying look at me. And the beam stopping. Not lifting. Looking. Two point three seconds. Because it knew what Seed was made of, and did not know why it was there.

"Seed," he said. "When it held me in the field. While it was holding me, was it blind?"

...yes.

A lamp that holds is blind to everything except what it holds.

That is why Ren was able to save the thirty-eight. That is why Su Mingli could pin the scout.

"And if all six held me at once?"

Seed did not answer for almost a second.

...then all six would be blind.

To everything.

To the First Legion. Eighteen thousand of them. On this wall. Within two hundred meters of the six.

And to the Lattice.

"Intent-sharing," said Yuanshu. "The Spear Corps struck one seam with three hundred spears on the Bridge-Breaker. The Lattice carries Adjutant Xu's note to fifty thousand. It carries intent. It did at the Western Pass." He was looking at the six blank faces. "If Old Seven's Spear Intent goes down the Lattice, through Adjutant Xu, to every soldier of the First Legion at once..."

...eighteen thousand edges. On six knots. In the same instant.

Three thousand cuts on each knot. All lit pale, if Lingyue's light can reach them all.

No knot can pull across three thousand.

Yuanshu. To make all six hold you at once, you have to be the brightest thing they have ever seen.

Brighter than the dying woman. Brighter than Ren.

You have to stand in the open. Not quiet. Not hiding. Shouting.

And they will read you. All of you. Six lamps, at once. Everything you are.

And if they decide, they will lift.

"Unless they hesitate."

...unless they find me. Standing in the open. And do not know why I am there.

Two point three seconds, in the field. One lamp.

I do not know about six.

He did not ask the city. There was no time. Article Eleven said where the answer of one would be acted upon in the name of all, and this was not that. This was his. His mind. His yes.

But he asked the dead.

Through Adjutant Xu, down the Lattice, one note, to every soldier of the First Legion on the north wall of Lingzhou and to the two hundred and seventy-two of the Spear Corps and to Old Seven at the top of them. He told them what he was going to do. What would happen if it worked. What would happen if it did not. He told them that when the knots came undone, the threads would lash, and that some of them would be unwoven, the way the twenty-eight had been, and would not come back.

You can say no. If you say no, you hold the wall, the way you promised, and nobody asks you twice.

It took eleven seconds for the answer to come back up the Lattice.

Ma Lianhua says: we held this wall thirty-one years ago against one of these. We were alive. We lost.

She says: we would like to hold it again.

Old Seven says nothing. Old Seven has already gone up the wall.

And on the watchtower above him, Lingyue said, through the staff, very quietly:

"I'll make it rain."

He stepped up onto the parapet of Sector Nine.

The six walkers had stopped in front of the wall. In a line. Nine meters tall, a hundred meters apart, with the rubble of the Black Tide and the silk of their dead companions around their feet and their blank faces turned toward him, level with the top of the wall.

He did not go quiet.

He did the opposite. He did the thing that every child on Blue Star had been taught never to do when a lens was in the north. He opened. Everything. The Field, folded over the whole city. The staff, with Jiang Lan awake in it and his mother's fragment asleep. The Death Law, thirty-seven point six percent of it, all the way down to the cool dark door at the bottom where things went when they were finished. Every name he had ever asked. Every answer he had ever been given. Old Seven in a hut at the edge of a cemetery. A father's notebook. His mother in a gold column. A girl on a wall at dawn with her grandfather's sword across her knees, saying only you.

He lit up like a lantern in the dark.

And Seed stood in the open behind his eyes, with nothing in front of it, and said:

Here.

Look at us.

Six lamps held.

All at once. Straight up. He felt them find him the way he had felt the tribulation find him in the glass, the way the Collector had found him in the hall, the way one lamp had found him in the field: completely, from the outside in, six times. His head turned. All the way. His face went up to the black sky over the north wall of Lingzhou. And six beams came down on him at once and began to read.

Everything.

He felt them go through him. The cemetery. The Academy. The first night. The voyage. The field. His mother. Lu on a switch in the round chamber. Ifeoma on a jetty. Major Zhuo in a column. Lin on a watchtower forty minutes ago. Every slice of him, at once, from six directions, and something at the end of every beam, very far away, learning.

And then they found Seed.

Standing in the open. Made of what they used to make.

And all six stopped.

Not lifting. Looking.

One.

Lingyue's light fell.

Not a drop. Rain. From the watchtower, from the point of a King's blade swung overhand into the sky against the slope of the Field and held at the top and let go, the way a woman casts a net from a boat: a great wide falling rain of silver-gray, heavy, over the whole north wall, over eighteen thousand rusted edges, so that every one of them lit pale at once.

Two.

Old Seven struck.

And down the Lattice, through a dead signaler on a chair by a bronze door, through eighteen thousand banked gray points on the north wall of Lingzhou, his intent went with him. Spear Intent. Twenty-one percent. A captain of the Eastern Line on a wall at night, with the city behind him and the beasts coming and no one else left, striking.

Eighteen thousand edges struck with him.

Into six knots. In the same instant. Three thousand cuts each.

Three.

He did not see them come apart. He could not see anything. His face was turned straight up at the sky and his mind was turned straight up with it and six beams were holding him, and then, all at once, there were no beams, and he was falling off the parapet of Sector Nine backward onto the stone, and someone caught him.

He heard it, though. Everyone in Lingzhou heard it. A sound like a great bolt of silk tearing, six times, all together, along the outside of the north wall in the dark. And then the soft enormous whisper of millions of threads falling, slowly, glittering in the floodlights, over the rubble of the Black Tide and the old stone wall and the First Legion standing on it with their lit edges raised.

And the lashing.

Four hundred and eleven.

Of the First Legion. Unwoven. Gone.

Ma Lianhua is not one of them. Old Seven is not one of them.

They knew. Every one of them. They were asked.

Three point four seconds, Yuanshu. Six lamps held you for three point four seconds and read everything you are.

And they read me.

All of me.

And then they were gone.

He lay on the stone of the Sector Nine battlements with his head in someone's lap and blood running out of his nose and his ears and his eyes, and could not see, and could not move, and heard Lingyue's voice above him, very close, saying his name. Not shouting. Saying it. The way the Quiet say a name. Once. And then again. And then again.

On every speaker in Lingzhou, a quiet, sharp, slightly hoarse voice said:

"Lingzhou. Breathe."

And two million eight hundred thousand people in the dark did.
