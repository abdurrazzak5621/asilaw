Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 121The Round Chamber

czverse12 min read

The cage still took four minutes.

The last time Yuanshu had ridden it down the raw rock shaft under his mother's laboratory, he had been Level 22, he had never left Lingzhou, and the thing at the bottom had turned its head three degrees to look at him. Now he was Level 33, he had been to the edge of the Star Sea and back, and the thing at the bottom had turned its head all the way up.

Su Mingli stood at his left shoulder, as she had then. Director Lu stood at the front of the cage with his hands clasped behind his back, as he had then. The difference was everyone else. Lingyue, with her sword across her back and her eyes on the rock going past. Doctor Iris Kwan, holding a steel instrument case against her chest with both arms and a tablet balanced on top of it. And in the back corner, folded nearly double under the cage roof, Old Seven, with his spear in a hand that now had one finger and a thumb.

"It started the night the sphere died," said Lu, without turning around. "At 4:02 in the morning, our time. Every array in the chamber jumped to three times its normal draw and stayed there. At 4:09 the night camera recorded the head moving." He paused. "It didn't turn. It tipped back. Like a man looking up at a plane."

"And since then?"

"It hasn't moved again." Lu turned his head slightly. "Everything else has."

Two hundred meters.

The temperature is lower than last time. Six degrees lower.

Something down there is taking the heat out of the rock.

The chamber was the same size it had always been. That was the only thing about it that was the same.

The containment arrays still ran in rings around the walls, but they were no longer a steady blue. They flickered, pale and strained, like lights in a building whose generator is failing. The hum had gone up in pitch. It sat in the back of Yuanshu's teeth like a dentist's drill heard through a wall.

The law-glass cylinder stood on its platform in the center. The not-quite-liquid inside it, which had once been clear, had clouded gray from the bottom up, a third of the way to the top. And suspended upright in the cloud, its seams and its missing arm and its smooth reflectionless head exactly where they had been for thirty-one years, the faceless one.

Its head was tipped back. The smooth front of it pointed straight up at the ceiling of the chamber, and at the shaft beyond that, and at the city, and at the sky.

Yuanshu climbed the three steps of the platform and looked at its chest.

The hole was closing.

The last time he saw it, it had been a great ragged wound through the middle of the body, big enough to put an arm through, where something had gone in and come out the other side. Now its edges were rimmed with gray crystal. Fine, branching, cloudy, cracked with dark lines like old ice. Growing inward from the rim, the way frost grows across a window, a few millimeters at a time.

Behind it, in the center of the hole, where a heart would be, something the size of a peach. Gray. Dull. Lit very faintly from inside, the color of a coal.

That is the seed.

It is not eating the scout.

It is sewing it shut.

"Tell me what you've measured," Yuanshu said. "All of it. Then tell me what you think it means, and I'll tell you if Seed agrees."

They told him.

Kwan went first, because she always went first. She had the array logs on her tablet: eight months of power draw, hour by hour. A flat line, then a cliff at 4:02 six nights ago, then a slope going up. "Triple on the first night. Three point four on day two. Three point nine on day three. It's climbing." She swallowed. "On day three Director Lu ordered the outer ring cut by ten percent for one hour, to see what happened."

"What happened?"

"The crystal grew slower." She turned the tablet around. "Not stopped. Slower. Sixty percent of the rate."

Su Mingli went next. She did not need instruments. She stood at the foot of the platform with her hands in the pockets of her long gray coat and her eyes half closed, and Yuanshu felt the faint pressure in the air that meant a King was paying attention to the weight of things.

"It's heavier," she said. "I held this cylinder when they lowered it in. I know what it weighs." She opened her eyes. "Forty kilos more than that. Most of it in the chest."

Then Lingyue, who had not been asked.

She had drawn her sword, unlit, and was holding the flat of the blade up to the glass the way you'd hold a mirror to catch the sun. She let a single thread of Light come out of the edge. Thin as a hair, white-gold, aimed straight through the cylinder at the far wall.

It did not reach the far wall.

Halfway through the gray cloud, the thread of Light bent. Gently, the way a river bends around a stone, except that it bent inward. Toward the chest. Toward the dull gray peach behind the closing wound. It went into the seed and did not come out.

"It drinks light," said Lingyue. "Not all of it. The way a sponge drinks water. Slowly." She let the thread go out. "That's not in your logs, Doctor."

Kwan was already writing it down.

Three models.

Seed laid them out behind his eyes the way it had laid out the force chain of a spear thrust in the white training hall at Tianshu, a long time ago. Clean. Numbered.

One. It grows on its own, like a crystal in a jar. Steady rate. If that is true, the wound closes in about sixty days.

Two. It grows by eating what we feed it. The arrays push power into the cylinder to hold it, and it drinks the power, the way it drinks Lingyue's light. The harder we hold it, the faster it grows. If that is true, the wound closes in nine days, and it gets faster every hour.

Three. It is not growing at all. It is repairing. The mass is the scout's body coming back. When the wound closes, it stops.

And the data?

The cut on day three kills model one. If it grew on its own, the cut would change nothing. It slowed.

But the cut also hurts model two. If it lived only on our power, cutting ten percent should have cut ten percent of the growth. It cut forty. Food does not work like that. A bellows does.

It is not eating our power. It is using our power to do something it would do anyway, faster.

So: three, fed by two. A repair, paid for by the arrays.

Best estimate: the wound closes in nineteen days. Plus or minus three.

He relayed it aloud. Lu took off his glasses.

"So we can't hold it tighter," said Su Mingli, "because it eats the grip. And we can't let go, because the arrays are the only thing keeping the glass in one piece."

"Not for nineteen days," said Yuanshu.

Nobody said what happened on the twentieth.

He did not like model three.

It was the best of the three. Seed had tested it against everything they had, and it fit. But it left a question with no answer in it, and the question was the one that mattered. A repair, for what?

A soldier repairs a wall so he can keep holding it. A doctor closes a wound so the patient can walk again. Things are mended because they are for something. Thirty-one years in a glass jar, and the first thing this one did with a Hunger's seed in its chest was sew itself shut, and tip its head back, and look up.

"Kwan," he said. "The draw. Not the daily numbers. The hourly ones. Eight months, before the cliff."

"It's flat," said Kwan. "I told you. It's been flat."

"Show me anyway."

She showed him. A long gray line across the bottom of the screen, eight months wide. Flat. Almost.

He saw it before Seed did. He would remember that, later, with a small cold pleasure that he was not proud of. Along the flat line, so small they looked like noise, were teeth. Tiny spikes in the power draw, a hair above the baseline, each one an hour wide, spaced out along eight months of data like the marks on a ruler.

Seed. How far apart are those?

A pause. Then, very fast:

Thirty-one hours. Fourteen minutes.

The chamber seemed to get colder.

Thirty-one hours and fourteen minutes. He had last heard that number on the bottom step of a staircase in a teahouse on Teahouse Lane, at three in the morning, trying not to think, while Seed counted the pulse coming down the threads out of the north. The heartbeat of the harvest. It had stopped almost a year ago, when a keeper in a field above the sky decided to ask instead of take.

It had stopped up there.

It had not stopped down here.

It is keeping the old clock.

The thing that sent it stopped calling. It never heard. It has been answering a pulse that does not come any more, once every thirty-one hours, for eight months. Probably for thirty-one years.

Like the march. Two hundred thousand of the dead, walking north because nobody told them they could stop.

Then what's it repairing itself for?

But he already knew. He could see it in the shape of the thing, tipped back in its cloud of gray. You do not look up to fight. You look up to call.

"It isn't healing so it can get out," he said aloud. Everyone turned. "It doesn't care about the glass. It's healing so it can report."

"Report to who?" said Lingyue.

"Whatever sent it. Whatever marked this city thirty-one years ago and was supposed to come back for it." He looked at Lu. "Lu Xuanming wrote it down. Something will come back for what it marked. It was a scout. It was sent to look and tell. It's been trying to tell for thirty-one years, and it never had the strength. Now somebody's given it a Hunger's seed for a heart."

Lu Zhengtian did not put his glasses back on. He held them in one hand, and looked at the faceless thing in the glass, and Yuanshu watched thirty-one years of a question arrive at its answer in the man's face.

"Nineteen days," Lu said.

"Plus or minus three."

He asked it.

He had to. Rule One did not have an exception for things he was afraid of. He knelt on the platform with the staff across his knees and Old Seven a step behind him and Su Mingli's hand already raised, and he reached down into the Death Law, gently, the way he had reached for every one of the dead he had ever met.

I'm Zhou Yuanshu. I'm the one who asks. Is anyone in there? What do you want?

Nothing answered.

There was no echo in it, because it had never been alive. There was no will in it, because nothing had ever been put there except a purpose. He could feel the purpose the way you feel the cold coming off a block of ice: patient, flat and enormous, pointed upward. There was nobody inside it to say yes or no. There never had been.

Let me try the seed.

Not the scout. The seed. I know what a seed is from the inside. I remember.

Carefully.

Carefully.

Seed went down through him, through the Death Law, through the law-glass and the gray cloud and the rim of frost-crystal, into the coal-colored peach in the scout's chest. He felt it go the way you feel a friend step out onto ice ahead of you.

For one second, it was fine.

Then the seed noticed.

It did not pull, the way the sphere in Port Halvard had pulled. It rang. A single low note, so deep he felt it in his spine rather than heard it, the way you feel a bell that is too large to hear struck somewhere under the ground. And out of the seed, in every direction, away through the rock and the city and the curve of the world, the note went looking for its sisters.

Seed came back so fast it hurt. Yuanshu was on his hands on the platform, with blood running from his nose onto the steel, and Su Mingli's fist in his collar.

I am all right.

I am all right. It did not read me. It did not know what I was. It only knew something touched it, and it called the others.

And they answered.

The others.

The Port Abiri seeds. The ones we could not find.

Four of them. I felt all four answer. For half a second, I knew where they were.

Where?

One at sea. Moving. South of Concordia, going east.

One in mountains. High. Cold. Somewhere in Yamato.

Two close together, on land, far to the west. Not moving.

I do not have coordinates. Only directions and a feeling of distance. It was half a second.

But Yuanshu. They answered fast. Like soldiers in one unit. Like something already listening for the call.

Yuanshu said it aloud, direction by direction. Kwan wrote it down so hard her pen tore the paper.

They rode the cage back up in silence until two hundred meters, the way they had the first time.

"Nineteen days," said Su Mingli, at last. She was looking at the rock going past. "And four more seeds out there, listening. If it calls, they'll hear it. If they hear it..." She did not finish.

"What would it take?" said Lu. "To destroy it. Before."

Yuanshu had already asked.

I have run it eleven thousand times.

At your strength, with everyone in this cage, and the Kings, and the dead on the wall, the scout dies in a little more than half of the runs.

In most of the runs where it dies, it gets the call out first.

At your strength, Yuanshu, we lose the race.

And if I'm stronger?

How much stronger?

Field. And past it. As far as the backlog goes. Gravity, Ice, Spirit, Tide, Force, Space, Time, Light, Fire. Everything you've been carrying and not running because you were taking the stairs.

A long silence from behind his eyes.

Then the runs change. A great deal.

But it is not a stair, Yuanshu. It is a leap. The iron weather will hear it. The first time, I hid in a dead man's core and came back with holes in me.

I do not know where I would hide this time.

"If you do it here," said Su Mingli, who had been watching his face and did not need to hear Seed to know what it was saying, "the tribulation comes down over three million people."

"Then not here," said Yuanshu.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she had nodded on the day of the Stele, when the Bureau man had reached for his arm and she had stepped in front of him.

"North, then," she said. "There's a lot of empty glass up there."

The cage climbed on through the rock. In the back corner, folded nearly double, Old Seven shifted his weight, and reached out with the butt of his spear, and tapped Yuanshu once on the ankle.

The way he had in the training hall at Tianshu, a long time ago, when the stance was wrong.

He says your weight is on your heels.

He says that is how you stand when you are about to step back.

He says: don't.
