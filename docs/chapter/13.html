Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 13Bone Brokers

czverse8 min read

Breakfast was served on the top floor of the Heishui Coal and Power office building, in a glass room that looked down the whole length of the valley.

From up here the town looked almost pretty. Morning sun on the river. Mist lifting off the hills. The three shaft towers standing in a row like the masts of a ship, and below them, small as ants, the first shift of the day filing in through the gate in yellow hard hats. Living men. Hundreds of them.

The table was white linen. There was congee, and fried dough sticks, and small plates of pickles, and tea in a pot that probably cost more than Mrs. Pan's house. Two waiters stood by the wall pretending not to exist.

Old Seven stood in the corner by the window with his spear grounded, coal dust still on his bones, and both waiters were very carefully not looking at him.

Gao Yinping poured the tea herself.

"I'll start," she said, "because you're young and I'm old and that's polite." She set a cup in front of him. "You think I'm a monster."

"I haven't decided yet."

"Good. Don't, until you've heard the numbers." She sat back. "Heishui Number Three used to kill eleven men a year. Roof falls. Gas. Flooding. When my father ran this company, there was a funeral in this town every month. You can still see the wreaths in the temple, they put the old ones in a room in the back." She sipped her tea. "Two years ago I bought my first core. Since then, Number Three hasn't lost a single living worker. Not one. Seven hundred and thirty-one days. You saw the sign."

She let that sit.

"The skeletons do the dangerous work," she said. "The deep faces. The unstable roof. The men stay in the safe tunnels and run the conveyors. Their wages went up. Their children go to a better school. Heishui has the lowest death rate of any coal town in Huaxia." She put her cup down. "Now you've switched off my core and laid fifty workers down on the rails, and this morning I'll have to send living men back into the deep face. What do you think the sign will say by Christmas?"

Yuanshu didn't answer right away.

I have checked her numbers against public safety records while she was talking.

They are true.

Before the core: an average of eleven deaths a year. After the core: zero.

He looked down the valley at the line of yellow hard hats filing through the gate.

"Where did they come from?" he asked. "The fifty."

"Some were ours. Men who died in Number Three before we had the core. The tunnels were sealed. The bodies were still down there." She said it without flinching. "The rest we bought."

"Bought."

"From recovery contractors. Licensed ones." She reached into her jacket and laid a business card on the white tablecloth. It was black, with silver lettering. Northern Gate Remains Recovery. Licensed. Discreet. "The Wastes are full of the dead. Forty years of soldiers and settlers and scavengers who went past the wall and didn't come back. Contractors go out and bring them home. Unclaimed remains can be sold for research or industrial use. That's been legal for twenty years." She turned the card around so he could read it properly. "They sell by the kilo, Mister Zhou. I paid fair prices."

"And the cores?"

"Also from the north gate. Different seller." For the first time, something flickered across her calm face. "I don't ask where those come from. They arrive in crates. They work."

She is telling the truth about that too.

She does not know what the mark means.

"Here's the part you won't like," said Gao Yinping.

She leaned forward.

"Everything I've done was legal until three o'clock this morning. The Black Tide Act controls Necromancers. I am not a Necromancer. Nobody in my company is. The core is classed as rift equipment, like a drill or a pump. The remains were bought from a licensed seller. Last night, while you were driving here, Concordia passed an Accord that says all methods now need consent." She sat back. "So, fine. I'm sure my lawyers will spend a very expensive year finding out what that means. But until three this morning, I was the most law-abiding employer in this province."

She refilled his cup, though he hadn't touched it.

"So here is my offer," she said. "You raise the dead and they listen to you. My core shouts at them and they listen to it. We both use the dead to do things the living can't. The only difference between us is that your way is slow and expensive and needs a teenage boy with a talent nobody else has. Mine comes in a crate." She spread her hands. "Work with me. Teach us your method, help us make the cores ask instead of shout, and put your name on it. Consent-certified mining. Every coal town in Huaxia would buy it. You'd save more miners in a year than the Defense Corps saves in ten."

It was, Yuanshu thought, a very good offer.

That was what made it dangerous.

I have modeled her proposal.

If you accept, the number of mining deaths in Huaxia falls by an estimated 74% within three years.

It is a good outcome.

It is also an outcome in which the dead become a product.

"Pan Guolin," said Yuanshu.

Gao Yinping blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"One of your fifty. The third cart. Gold tooth on the left side." He set down the teacup he hadn't drunk from. "He was yours. He died in Number Three two years ago. You told his wife the tunnel was sealed and he was at peace." He looked at her. "And then you put a core on him and had him push coal past the place he died. For two years. While his wife lit incense to a photograph."

Gao Yinping's face did not change. But her hand, resting on the white tablecloth, very slowly closed.

"When I reached for him last night," Yuanshu said, "there was almost nothing left. The core wore him out. He could only say one word."

He stood up.

"Your safety numbers are real. I believe you. And I'll help you keep men out of the deep face, if you want it. I'll help design machines, or rescue teams, or better roof supports, or anything else." He pushed the chair in. "But I won't help you make the dead into a product. Not even a kind one. Not even a safe one."

"Because?"

"Because he said home," said Yuanshu. "Not coal."

The door of the glass room opened.

A slim man in a dark suit and silver-rimmed glasses walked in as if he had been invited, and smiled pleasantly at everyone.

"Madam Gao," said Director Lu Zhengtian. "Mister Zhou. Good morning." He looked at the breakfast table. "Ah. Congee."

Gao Yinping rose. Her face had gone very smooth. "Director. This is a private meeting."

"It was," Lu agreed. He took a folded paper from his breast pocket and laid it on the white linen beside her teacup. "As of seven o'clock this morning, by order of the Awakening Bureau, rift cores bearing the mark recovered by Mister Zhou at Bonefield Hollow are classified as necromantic devices under the Black Tide Act, Article Nine." He smoothed the paper flat. "Possession requires a Bureau license. You don't have one. Number Three is closed pending review. I'm sorry about the breakfast."

Gao Yinping looked at the paper for a long moment.

Then she sat back down, picked up her teacup with a perfectly steady hand, and said, "My lawyers will be in touch."

"I look forward to it," said Director Lu, and meant it.

They walked out together through the mine yard, between the coal heaps, with the morning shift watching them from under their yellow hard hats.

"You moved fast," said Yuanshu.

"I've been waiting for a reason for two years," said Lu. "I knew someone was selling cores out of the north gate. I didn't know what they were, and I couldn't prove the mark meant anything." He glanced sideways. "You brought me the canyon shard, the photograph, the mine, the dead man, and the Accord, all in one week. That's a reason."

"And what do you want for it?"

Lu smiled faintly. "You're learning." He stopped by the cut in the fence, where Mrs. Pan was still sitting in the dust with the bundle in her arms and Dabao standing over her with the umbrella. "The cores. All of them. The Bureau has twenty-three, seized over two years from smugglers we couldn't charge. They're in the Rift Research Institute. Nobody knows how they work." He looked at Yuanshu over his glasses. "Seed does. Or will. Come to the Institute and tell us what they are. Let the Bureau watch you do it."

This is the same thing he asked for at the hearing.

Cooperation. Access. Watching.

But this time he has given something first.

Yuanshu thought about it.

"I'll come," he said. "On two conditions. The first one is that Sister Ning of the Quiet can be there whenever I touch the dead in your building."

Lu raised his eyebrows. "The Quiet? They hate you."

"They're afraid of me. It's different. And the second..."

He looked down the hill at the gate, where a truck was pulling up with a black business card taped to its windshield. Northern Gate Remains Recovery. The driver got out, saw the Bureau cars, saw the skeleton by the fence, and very quickly got back in.

"The second is that you tell me who sells bodies by the kilo," said Yuanshu, "and where they buy them."

Director Lu followed his gaze to the truck as it backed up, turned, and drove away down the valley much too fast.

"That," he said, "is a much longer conversation."

That night, at Tianshu, Grandpa Xu listened to the whole story without interrupting, in the white room, with his hands folded on his cane.

When it was over, he was quiet for a long time.

"There's a market," he said finally. "At the north gate. Has been for twenty years. Starts after midnight, gone by dawn. Out past the last checkpoint, on the Wastes side of the wall, so no city law reaches it." He looked at his hands. "Soldiers' bones. Settlers' bones. Beast bones, by the ton, for the alchemists. Whatever comes back out of the Wastes." His voice was flat. "They call it the Night Market of Bones. Everybody knows. Nobody goes. The dead don't vote, boy. Nobody ever made a law about who owns them, because nobody ever had to."

He looked up.

"Until you."
