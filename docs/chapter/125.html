Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 125The Wall Votes

czverse10 min read

He asked them at dawn, on the north wall, with a clipboard.

Xiaoyu's clipboard, because she would not let him use anyone else's, and Xiaoyu's question, because she had written it out the night before at the kitchen table and crossed out four versions and made him read the fifth aloud to their mother twice.

The thing under the Institute may wake in fifteen days. If it does, or if anything comes because of it, the fighting may be inside the city. In the streets. Where people live. Where people will see you.

Article Seven says the dead may defend. This is defending.

Will you fight inside Lingzhou, if it comes to that?

You can say no. If you say no, nobody will ask you twice.

He did not read it nineteen thousand times. He did not need to. He stood on the battlements of Sector Nine with the white staff in one hand and the clipboard in the other, and the staff carried the question down the wall, east and west, out of sight, to every gray flame banked along forty kilometers of old stone. Jiang Lan carried it. Seed counted.

It took four hours.

Yes: 17,964.

No: 1,138.

Only to speak: 300. They want to say why, and then they will do whatever the others do.

The three hundred spoke first.

Most of them said the same thing in different words. They had been dead for thirty-one years. For the first two days of that, they had walked. Through their own streets, in Lingzhou and Heishui and the river towns, with a dead man's order in them, past their own front doors. Their families had seen them. Their children had seen them, standing in the road with their eyes gone violet, and run screaming.

I don't want my granddaughter to see me like that, said a woman with a hairnet still tied over her skull, through the staff. She was six. She's thirty-seven now. She has children.

She'll see you holding a wall, said Yuanshu. Not walking past her door.

Will she know the difference?

He did not have an answer to that. He told her so. She said that was the first honest thing anyone had said to her in thirty-one years, and she would fight.

The 1,138 did not change their minds. He did not ask them to. He asked them, instead, what they wanted.

They want to stand on the outside of the wall.

Facing out. Toward the Wastes. Away from the city.

They say if something comes from outside, they will hold it. They say they will not go into the streets. They say they would rather go quiet than be seen there again.

"Then that's their post," said Yuanshu. "The outer face of the wall. Facing out." He wrote it on Xiaoyu's clipboard, under the numbers, in his bad handwriting. "Nobody moves them inside. Ever."

They say thank you.

They say: facing the right way.

Seventeen thousand nine hundred and sixty-four of the dead, standing in a line forty kilometers long, was not an army.

It was a crowd. A very patient crowd, with a few thousand rusted rifles and some bayonets and a great many bare hands, and no ranks, no officers, no idea of where to stand if the sky came down over the Institute. Old Seven walked the wall all morning with his spear and came back at noon with his gray flame burning low and steady and a look on his skull that Yuanshu had learned to read without Seed's help.

He says they are good people.

He says they are not soldiers. Most of them. Some of them were.

He says he cannot command eighteen thousand alone. He has one finger and a thumb and a head full of men who are in a fire somewhere south of the stars.

He says he needs sergeants.

So they found him sergeants.

Seed did it, mostly, the way it had once found the one man in Fat Tan's eleven whose ledger was too clean: by looking for what did not fit. In a crowd of eighteen thousand farmers and clerks and grandmothers and postmen, there were embers that stood differently. That held their place in the line without being told. That turned toward a sound before the people beside them did.

Three hundred and forty-one of them were soldiers when they died. Defense Corps, mostly. Some Eastern War Zone. Two from the old river garrison.

Eleven were officers.

One of them was a captain.

Her name was Ma Lianhua. She had commanded the river gate on the last night of the Black Tide, with ninety men and a broken radio, and held it for six hours until the river gate came down on top of her. She was a short broad skeleton with a captain's cord still knotted around what was left of her shoulder, and when Old Seven walked up to her on the battlements and stopped, she came to attention so fast that her bones clattered.

They looked at each other for a while. Two captains of the same dead night, from opposite ends of the same city.

Then Old Seven held out his hand. The short gray one with one finger and a thumb. She took it.

She says she heard about the eastern end.

She says everyone did. She says the river gate held one hour longer because someone shouted that the east was still standing.

He says: so it was you.

She says: so it was you.

There was a postman.

He was not a soldier. He had been delivering the last mail of the day in the old quarter when the Black Tide came over the wall, and he had died with his bag still over his shoulder, and when the staff asked him in the valley he had said yes in a voice that sounded, Seed said, like a man who has just remembered he left the stove on. His name was Wei Desheng. He knew every street in Lingzhou. Every alley, every stair, every courtyard, every shortcut through every market. He had known them for twenty-two years.

"The city's changed," Yuanshu told him, through the staff. "It's been thirty-one years. There are new towers. New roads."

He says the streets under them are the same.

He says the city is like a face. It gets older, but the bones do not move.

He says: give me a week and I will know the new parts too.

There was a girl in a wedding dress.

Song Yanyan. Nineteen. She had died at her own wedding banquet in a hotel by the river, in red silk, which had gone gray in thirty-one years, and she carried a bouquet of stems she would not put down. She was the fastest thing on the wall. Seed clocked her running the length of the Sector Nine battlements and back while the others were still turning around.

"Runners," said Ma Lianhua, through the staff, looking at her. "We'll need runners. Radios fail. They always fail."

So Song Yanyan, who had been dead for thirty-one years and had never had a job, became the First Legion's first runner. She took it very seriously. She asked Wei Desheng to teach her the city.

Hu Dabao came up the stairs to the Sector Nine battlements at four in the afternoon, in the dark green fatigues of the Defense Corps, with a tower shield on his back that was taller than Xiaoyu.

He stopped at the top of the stairs and looked at Yuanshu.

Yuanshu looked at him.

They had not been in the same place for most of a year. There had been a north, and a field above the sky, and a Sea, and a fire. Dabao had been on the wall the whole time. Yuanshu had seen him twice in the crowd. Once on the night the keeper came down. Once on the night of the Lanterns, standing on the battlements with his eyes shut, holding his shield.

"You went to space," said Dabao.

"Sort of."

"You went to space and you didn't call."

"There wasn't any signal."

"There's always signal. You just don't call." Dabao's face did something complicated and wet. "Captain Zhao went quiet and you didn't call. I found out from the news. I found out from a girl at the dumpling stall. My own mother knew before I did."

"I'm sorry."

"You should be." Dabao wiped his face on his sleeve. Then he took the tower shield off his back and set it down on the stones with a clang that made half the dead on the battlements turn their heads. "Captain Lin's been training me. Every morning. Zhao's drills. The ones he did with me on the roof of the Ossuary, before you all left. Behind me is home. I can hold a line of fifty now. Behind this." He thumped the shield. "Elite. Level forty-one. I've been on this wall every day for a year, killing things that come out of the Wastes, while you were in a fire."

Level forty-one.

Yuanshu, who was Level 33, looked at his oldest friend for a long moment.

"Good," he said. "Then you're on the shield line."

"I'm already on the shield line. I've been on the shield line for a year." Dabao picked the shield back up. His face was still wet. "I'm asking if I'm on your shield line. When it comes."

"Yes."

"Good." He sniffed. "Also my mother wants you to come to dinner. She says you're too thin. She says that woman in the wheelchair says the same thing, and that woman in the wheelchair is right."

Lingyue spent the afternoon at the Academy.

She came back at dusk with forty-one students behind her, in the dark blue of the Lingzhou Martial Academy, every one of them carrying a sword or a spear or, in one case, a long-handled hammer. All of them held some fraction of the Law of Light. None of them were older than twenty.

"Su Mingli's idea," she said. "Anyone with Light who's inside your field gets pale fire. So we need everyone with Light standing inside your field." She looked at the forty-one students, who were trying very hard not to stare at eighteen thousand skeletons. "They've never fought next to the dead. Some of them have never fought at all."

"What are you going to do?"

"Teach them to bend it." She lit her sword. Inside the field, it went silver-gray from the hilt up, without anyone touching it. Forty-one students made a sound like a breath going out. "Light burns what it burns. Unless you tell it where not to." She looked at him sideways. "I learned that in a canyon. Bending it around fifty skeletons I'd just met, for four hours, while somebody told me I was competition."

"You were competition."

"I still am." She turned to the students. "Line up. Facing the dead. Hold your blade out. If you burn anyone on this wall, I'll make you apologize to them in person, and they'll accept, because they're nicer than you."

At nine o'clock that night, Yuanshu sat on the battlements with the clipboard on his knees and the numbers under his hand.

First Legion. Seventeen thousand nine hundred and sixty-four inside the walls, under Old Seven, with Captain Ma Lianhua as his second. Three hundred and forty-one old soldiers made sergeants. A postman for a map and a bride for a runner. One thousand one hundred and thirty-eight on the outer face of the wall, facing out. Forty-one students with pale fire. One Shield Guardian who had not been called.

Soul-power load with the Field holding them: sixty-one percent.

You can carry them. You cannot carry them and fight at the same time. Not yet.

Fifteen days.

His phone buzzed.

It was Kwan. He could hear rain behind her, and the hum of the Institute's arrays through a wall.

"Something's happening," she said. "In the rifts. Not the big ones. The small ones, inside the city. The grade-ones in the old quarter and by the harbor and the two under the Exhibition Hall." A pause. "The Defense Corps cameras just picked it up. Every beast in every rift in Lingzhou has stopped moving."

"Stopped?"

"Stopped. And turned." Her voice was not steady. "They're all facing the same direction, Yuanshu. Every one of them. They're facing the Institute."

He looked north, past the dark Wastes, to where a small white ring hung low over the end of the world. Then south, across the lit city, to the river, and the old industrial district, and a squat gray building with a cage three hundred meters under it.

Thirty-one years ago, before the Black Tide, Lu Xuanming had written it down in a notebook. The beasts sat facing north.

These were facing inward. Toward the Institute.
