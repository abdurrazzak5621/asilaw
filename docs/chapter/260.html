Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 260What the Quiet Know

czverse13 min read

Ichiro talked all night.

He had said he would. He had been quiet for a thousand years, he said, and he was not going to be quiet for one minute more, and he did not care who heard. So he talked, through Seed, from the fog that stood round the end of the old stone breakwater at Shiomi bay, while his sister sat on the stones in the middle of it with the lamp in her lap and listened, and the town behind them went to sleep, and the fishermen in their boots and caps stood at the far end of the breakwater until midnight not understanding a word and not wanting to leave.

Yuanshu sat on the stones a little way off with the staff, carrying it. Lingyue sat beside him, wrapped in a blanket Ren had brought down from the shrine, very quiet. Old Seven stood at the landward end, facing out, as if someone might come along the breakwater who should not.

Seed translated. All of it. It said afterward that it was the longest anyone had ever talked through it, longer than Jiang Lan, longer than the keeper, and that it had enjoyed every word.

He says: the threads came down on the night of the cutting, and he was on the boats, and he was talking to the fish, and he was taken.

He says: he was in a column. In the gold. Above the sky. With everybody. It was warm and he could not move and nobody asked him anything and he talked to himself for three hundred years so he would not forget how.

He says: then something came for the column.

The fog did not move. But the lights in it, the hundreds of small dim lights that were a village, drew a little closer together.

He says: a walker. Long legs. Gray. It took the column out of the field and folded it up and carried it away along a thread into the dark. Toward something big.

He says: and halfway along, in the deep water, gleaners came.

He says: little things. Like dogs made of ash. Hundreds. They went for the walker the way they went for your dead on the island. For the embers. And the walker fought them, and while it was fighting, the column broke.

He says: and Shiomi fell out.

Sister Kiyo, in the fog, did not say anything. But her hands on the lamp in her lap moved, once.

He says: they fell into the deep water. All of them. The whole village. Three hundred and eleven. Out of the column into the dark. And the gleaners turned round to look for what had fallen.

He says: and the headman said: be quiet.

He says: the headman said it in the fog-code. On the small bells. The warning. Long, short, long. Stop rowing. Be quiet. Something is close to you in the fog and it is bigger than you.

He says: and they were quiet. All three hundred and eleven. And the gleaners went past them in the dark and did not find them.

He says: and they stayed quiet. For nine hundred years.

He says: even him.

He says: they were not the only ones.

It was past midnight. The fishermen had gone home. The lamp in Sister Kiyo's lap burned pale and steady, the size of a lamp, the color of whale oil in a fog.

He says: the deep water is full of quiet.

He says: things that fell out of hands. Things that hid when the hands went by. Things from worlds that do not exist any more, that learned to be dark so they would not be picked. Drifting. Not talking. Not burning. Not anything. Just being quiet, together, in the dark, for as long as it takes.

He says: Shiomi drifted with them. For nine hundred years. They learned each other's quiet. There is a way to be quiet with someone that is like talking. He says the Watch of the Shelf will learn it. He says he passed them on the way in, at the edge of the cloud, asking, and he rang once to them in the fog-code, and the woman in the coat looked up.

He sat very still on the stones.

Nana Ampofo.

He says: yes. That was her name. He asked. She said: tell the boy we're fine. She said: tell him it's cold and dark and we're singing the names. She said: tell him it's not the sea, but it'll do.

He says: she said: ask us again in a year.

He says: and then the field.

He says: two years ago, a little more. Something happened in the Shallows. The quiet ones all felt it. A keeper that had never asked anything asked. And a field emptied. Not taken. Given back. And all through the deep water, the quiet ones who had been dark for a thousand years and a thousand thousand, turned their heads.

He says: he could not help it. He rang.

He says: the headman told him not to. Everyone told him not to. He rang anyway. The boat call. Five quick notes. He says he was always too fast in the middle. He says he knew Kiyo would know it was him.

He says: and she did.

"Tell him I always know it's him," said Sister Kiyo, through Seed, from the middle of the fog, dryly. "He's the only one in Shiomi who can't ring a boat call properly."

Out of the fog came a sound Seed did not translate. It did not need to. It was the sound a man of twenty-two makes when his older sister has said exactly the thing she has said to him every day of his life.

And now the thing he has been wanting to say all night. He has been saving it. He says it is important and he did not want it to get lost among the rest. He says Kiyo always said he buried the important things in the middle.

The fog was very still.

He says: the quiet ones are not the only things in the dark that saw.

He says: when you went bright on the pier, by the other sea. For five minutes. Everything that looks saw it. The quiet ones saw. And the loud ones.

He says: there are loud ones.

Lingyue, beside him, had sat up.

He says: two kinds.

He says: the first are people. Like you. Like Shiomi. Many worlds of them, along the arms, a long way from the Shallows. The quiet ones call them the Lamplit, because they burn on purpose. They have stones too. Like yours. Their stones read them and mark them, the way yours did. And they took the marks, and made them into a ladder, and put the brightest at the top.

He says: they are very proud of being bright. They think bright is strength. They fought the one who picks for ten thousand years. They lost a great deal. They never won. But they never stopped fighting, and they made a great many weapons, and they made themselves into a great many worlds that talk to each other, and they call that a Federation.

He says: they saw the pier.

He says: they saw the one who picks stop.

He says: they do not know it was you. Not yet. But they saw a Lord-bright flare on a world they do not have in their lists, in the Shallows, five minutes, and a week later the thing they had fought for ten thousand years stopped. And they can count.

He did not say anything.

He says: the second kind are not people. Not like you.

He says: the quiet ones call them the Singers.

He says: they live on a world near the deep water. Very old. Very beautiful. When the one who picks first came, a long time ago, they did not fight it. They sang to it. They found out that if they gave it their brightest, every age, on purpose, singing, it would take those and spare the rest.

He says: so they did. For longer than the Lamplit have existed. Every age, they chose their brightest, and dressed them in light, and sang them up a thread. A tithe. And the one who picks came and took the tithe and went away and left the rest of them alone.

He says: their whole world was built on it. Their gods. Their kings. Their songs. All of it. Every song they have is about the tithe.

He says: and this age, they chose their brightest, and dressed them in light, and sang.

He says: and nothing came.

The lamp in Sister Kiyo's lap burned steadily in the fog.

He says: the Singers are very afraid.

He says: they think they have done something wrong. They think the one who picks has refused the tithe. They think it is angry. They think it will come and take everything, the way it did before they learned to sing.

He says: they have sent a ship. Along the thread their stones used to send up. To the place where the one who picks sits. To find out why it did not come.

He says: and when they get there, they will find it sitting still with its hands open, and a girl's writing in its face, and they will follow the last thread it read.

He says: to here.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then, from the fog, Sister Kiyo. Dry. Twelve hundred years old again.

She says: Ichiro. How long?

He says: the Lamplit, he does not know. They are fast. They have ships that fold. Months. Perhaps less.

He says: the Singers are slow. They sing as they go. A year. More.

He says: but they are both coming. To look at the world where the one who picks stopped.

He says: he thought the boy should know. He has been saving it all night.

He says: Kiyo always said he buried the important things in the middle. He put it at the end. He would like that noted.

"Noted," said Sister Kiyo of Shiomi, and Yuanshu heard, through Seed, for the first time, the sound of a twelve-hundred-year-old woman almost laughing.

The keeper came down at three, the way it always did.

He had not asked it to. He had not known whether it could reach this far east, to a bay in Yamato, so far from the old banking hall. But it came, down the gold thread out of the north, rough and close and a third smaller, into the fog off the end of the breakwater, and stopped there. At the edge. Not coming in.

...SHIOMI.

The fog went very still. Every light in it.

...I KNOW THIS NAME NOW. I DID NOT KNOW IT THEN.

...I TOOK YOU.

...TWELVE HUNDRED YEARS AGO. I PUT DOWN THREADS AND I TOOK YOUR VILLAGE INTO MY FIELD AND I DID NOT ASK. I DID NOT KNOW HOW. I DID NOT KNOW ANYONE COULD BE ASKED.

...AND THEN A WALKER TOOK YOUR COLUMN AND I DID NOT STOP IT. I THOUGHT IT WAS WEATHER.

...I AM SORRY.

...I HAVE NEVER SAID THAT TO ANYONE I TOOK. I DID NOT KNOW WHERE THEY WERE.

...I AM SORRY. I AM TELLING YOU FIRST: WHATEVER YOU SAY, I WILL STILL BE SORRY.

The fog did not answer.

For a long time, nothing. The lamp in the nun's lap. The bay going gray toward dawn. The keeper at the edge of the fog, waiting, the way it had learned. The bowl down. The eyes turned aside.

Then, from the middle of the fog, the sound of a bell.

Not Ichiro's. A slower one. A deeper one. The bell the headman of Shiomi had rung, nine hundred years ago, in the deep water, to tell three hundred and eleven people to be quiet. Seed said so. Seed said it was the oldest sound it had ever heard.

It rang once.

...he says...

The headman says: you asked.

He says: in Shiomi, when a boat came in late and the lamp was already lit, and the boat had done something wrong out at sea, broken a net, lost a line, frightened the fish, the headman would go down to the shore and ask the boat what had happened.

He says: and if the boat told the truth, the headman would say: you came in. The lamp was lit. That is enough for one night.

He says: keeper. You came in. The lamp was lit.

He says: that is enough for one night.

The gold thread at the edge of the fog trembled, the way a thread trembles when something very large at the other end of it has had to hold very still for a moment so as not to break it.

...THANK YOU.

...ASK ME AGAIN TOMORROW?

He says: we will be here. We are home.

At dawn, on the end of the breakwater, with the fog thinning off the bay and the lamp in the nun's lap going pale in the daylight, Lingyue said it.

She said it the way she said everything: all at once, without warning, looking at the sea and not at him, the way she had said then we cut it in the banking hall and I'm the SSS, they should see one in the car.

"I'm pregnant," said Shen Lingyue.

He did not say anything. He could not. He sat on the stones of the old breakwater at Shiomi with the staff across his knees and looked at his wife, who was looking at the sea, with a blanket round her shoulders and her grandfather's sword beside her and her jaw set the way it set when she was about to fight something.

"Bai Lu told me before we left," she said. "I made her promise not to tell you. I wanted to tell you myself. I didn't know when." She looked at the fog. "I was going to tell you on the mountain. Then there was the nun. Then there was the fog. Then there was the lamp. I gave the lamp away." Her voice was very level. "I thought, I'm going to give the lamp away and then I'm going to tell him. I'm going to give the light to someone who's been waiting twelve hundred years for it, and then I'll say it."

She turned her head. She looked at him.

"Say something," she said. "You're doing the face."

"How long?" he said. It was the only thing he could find.

"Ten weeks. Bai Lu thinks. Since before the deep water." She almost smiled. "Since before Port Halvard. I flew across an ocean pregnant. I cut a hand open pregnant. I held you down on a platform for nine hours pregnant and pulled you back." She looked down at her hand, the one that had held the lamp. "I didn't know. I just knew the light kept coming back from underneath."

...Yuanshu.

Your heart rate is a hundred and fifty-one.

It has never been a hundred and fifty-one.

I am not recording. I would like to ask her, first, whether I may.

He did not answer Seed. He did not answer anyone. He sat on the stones and looked at Shen Lingyue, and thought, very clearly, of a battlement in Lingzhou at night, and his wife coming up the stairs and sitting down beside him and saying, in the dark, looking north:

Nobody asked you whether you wanted to be born.

"It can't say yes," he said. "It's not anyone yet."

"No," said Lingyue.

"So we decided."

"We decided," said his wife. "On a slope. At a wedding. In front of everyone. We just didn't know we had." She looked at him. Her eyes were very bright, and she was not crying, and her hand had found his on the stones, the one with the ring on it. "Article Twelve. Your mother's one. You decide once, for something that can't answer yet. And then you owe it the question."

"Every day."

"Every day," said Shen Lingyue. "Until it can answer. And after."

From the middle of the fog, very faint, a bell rang. Five quick notes. A little too fast in the middle.

He says: congratulations.

He says: sorry. He was listening. He is always listening. Kiyo says he was born listening.

He says: in Shiomi, when a child was coming, the whole village rang the small bells at dawn. All the boats. So the child would know, before it was born, that there were people waiting for it on the shore.

And across the bay, from the fog, from three hundred and eleven small dim lights that had been quiet for nine hundred years and had come home, all the small bells of Shiomi rang at once.

Nobody on Blue Star could hear them but Seed.

Seed played them for him anyway. And for her.

I asked, said Seed. She said yes. She said: obviously.

I am recording this.
