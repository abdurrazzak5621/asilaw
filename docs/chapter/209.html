Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 209The Shore

czverse8 min read

The field was gold again when they came back to it, and the keeper could not stop talking.

They saw it from a long way off. On the eleventh night by Bai Lu's clock, at the leading edge of what was left of the island, a kilometer and a half of pale ground with fifty thousand dead standing close on it, Old Seven lifted his skull and looked ahead into the dark, and there it was. A light. Not small. Not a house across a valley with one window lit. A city. A great soft gold glow in the black ahead of them, so wide and so warm that the far lights around it looked dim.

...THEY ARE HERE. ALL OF THEM.

...I ASKED THEM THIS MORNING. AND YESTERDAY. AND THE DAY BEFORE.

...MOST OF THEM STILL SAY: WE DO NOT KNOW YET. ASK AGAIN TOMORROW.

...SOME OF THEM ASKED ME THINGS. ONE OF THEM ASKED ME WHAT I WANTED.

...I DID NOT KNOW WHAT TO SAY.

...I SAID: TO BE ASKED. SOMETIMES.

...IT SAID IT WOULD.

The Elder, standing at the center of the island with the gold thread running out of her, was smiling. He had never seen her smile. He had not known her face could.

"It has been like this for nine days," she said, in her glass-note voice. "It does not stop. It tells me everything. Every column. Every answer." She looked at the gold light ahead. "My people made it out of a yes, under a red sun, a very long time ago. We thought we had made a thing that keeps." Her long hands were pressed together. "We did not know it could be happy."

They came in through the far door at dawn by Bai Lu's clock.

What was left of the island drifted in against the edge of the field the way a boat drifts in against a jetty when someone who knows how is at the tiller, and touched, and stopped, and the pale ground of the island and the pale ground of the field met with a long low sound that he felt in his teeth, and closed, and were one ground again.

...THERE.

...I HAVE IT BACK.

...A LITTLE SMALLER. IT WILL GROW.

...PERHAPS.

The field was full.

He walked across it with the staff, slowly, because his legs were still not entirely his, with Lingyue beside him not helping. Gold columns. Everywhere. In every direction, as far as he could see, filling the faint rings on the pale ground where the old columns had stood, and the ground between them, and the haze at the edges. Not in rows. In clusters. The way people stand when they have just arrived somewhere and have not decided yet where they want to be. Cousins of the world that asks, small and pale, a little like Hush. Things from the walls of the Mouth he had no names for: something like a tree, something like a cloud of fine gold dust, something with a great many hands, folded. All of them looking out. Curious. Wondering.

And at the near edge, where four faint rings lay side by side on the pale ground, a cluster of a hundred and seventy-five columns who had stayed when the field was empty, standing close together, the way the first ones in a room stand when the room fills up.

They turned when the dead of Blue Star went by.

All of them. All at once. The way people turn to watch a parade.

The keeper laid the roads down at sunrise over Blue Star.

Six of them, the way it had laid them going out. Gold. Wide enough for nine to walk abreast. Down out of the small white ring over the end of the world, across the morning sky, to every place the dead had come from.

The Western Legion went down to the Western Pass first.

He watched it go from the top of the road, at the edge of the field. Twenty-five thousand dead soldiers of the Western Pass, in their rows, walking down a road of light out of the sky toward a mountain of graves in the snow, with a short broad skeleton at the front with a pick over his shoulder, counting. Sixteen of them were not there. The gleaners. Sergeant Ma had said their names at the rim of the island every night of the voyage home. He would say them on the mountain. Every one.

At the bottom of the road, on the first terrace, beside a gray stone in row two hundred and twelve, an old man with one arm was standing in a greatcoat, with his cap on.

He did not say anything when his soldiers came down out of the sky. He took his cap off.

The road to Sector Nine came down last.

He went down it with Old Seven in front of him, and Lingyue beside him, and the Spear Corps behind, and the First Legion behind them, and the living scattered among the dead the way they had been on the island: Bai Lu with her bag and her grandmother's clock, Hu Dabao with his shield, the Mo twins with one pack between them, Kwan with three instrument cases and a new list, Qin Hao with his bandaged arms and his notebook. Adjutant Xu with his cane. The Elder, tall and pale, with the gold thread running through her into the road.

And Liang Fu, with his weight off his right leg.

And in Old Seven's hand, small and pale, uncurled now, all its limbs out, all its dark eyes open, looking down at Blue Star coming up toward them out of the morning: Hush.

They were all there.

He had not asked them to be. Nobody had. The whole city, on the north wall, in the gray dawn, from the river gate to Crossing Six, the way they had been on the morning the roads went up. Standing. Not talking. Su Mingli at the top of the stairs. Orlov. General Han, in his dark green uniform, with his cap under his arm. Captain Lin Wen in the watchtower above Crossing Six, standing in the footprints by the north slit. Sister Ning with her candle. Fat Tan, crying openly. Principal Yue. Hendrik Maas, who had driven a bus from the station again.

At the foot of the gold road, where it came down onto the battlements of Sector Nine: his mother, in her wheelchair, with a blanket over her knees and the microphone in her lap.

And standing beside the wheelchair, not behind it, in a hoodie too big for her, with a black marker uncapped in her hand: Xiaoyu.

She had been there all night. He could tell. Her eyes were red. She had marker on her fingers.

She did not say anything when he came down the last of the road onto the stone. She did not hit him on the arm. She did not run to him. She stood where she was, at the bottom of the road, with the marker in her hand, the way she had said she would.

So that he would know which shore was his.

Old Seven reached the stone first.

He stepped off the end of the gold road onto the battlements of Sector Nine, a tall dead soldier two meters ten with a spear in one finger and a thumb, and stopped in front of Xiaoyu. And crouched down. The way a tall man crouches to talk to a child, though she was seventeen now and nearly as tall as Lingyue and had called a whole city in the dark.

And held out his other hand.

Hush looked at the girl with the marker.

The girl with the marker looked at Hush.

Nobody on the wall breathed.

...you're the small loud one.

"Yes," said Xiaoyu. Her voice was rough. "I write it on the wall. Every month. Everything you said."

...I know. The keeper told us. In the fire. It said a girl was writing it on a wall in a city, in capitals, and putting the seconds underneath.

...I liked that you put the seconds.

Xiaoyu did not say anything. Her chin was shaking.

Hush uncurled one thin pale limb from the old soldier's palm. In it, worn soft at the edges, faded, red, was a playing card.

...I said I still had it. Every month. So that someone would know I was still here.

...I am here now.

...I don't need to have it any more.

It held the card out.

Not to Old Seven. To Xiaoyu.

...will you put it on the wall?

...at the end. Where you write the seconds.

Zhou Xiaoyu took the queen of hearts in her marker-stained fingers, very carefully, the way you take something someone has held for four hundred and forty days in the cold, and could not say anything at all. She nodded. Once. Twice. And then she sat down on the stone of the battlements of Sector Nine, very suddenly, in front of a crouching dead soldier with a small pale thing in his hand, and put her face in her hands, and cried.

Hush climbed out of Old Seven's palm onto her knee.

And sat there. Small. Uncurled. Very cold. In the gray dawn on the north wall of Lingzhou, with the whole city watching, on the knee of a girl who had written down everything it ever said.

Yuanshu knelt beside his mother's chair.

She did not say anything for a long time. She took his face in her thin hands, the way she had on the morning he left, and looked at it. At the soft edges still in his eyes. At the blood dried in the creases of his ears that nobody had had time to wash out. At whatever else was there, that had been there since a door at the bottom of the Death Law had opened wider than any door should, for nineteen hours, and not quite closed.

"You're different," said Zhou Wanqing.

"Yes."

"Tell me what you wanted," said his mother. "The whole time. You said you would."

He knelt on the stone of the battlements with his mother's hands on his face and the city on the wall around him and his sister sitting on the stone a meter away with a small pale thing on her knee and a playing card in her fingers, and thought about it.

Not quickly. He had learned that.

"I wanted them to be asked," he said. "Everyone. Every one of them. The ones in the walls. The ones in the columns. The ones in the ground. I wanted nobody to ever be in the dark again without someone asking them what they wanted." He was quiet. "And I wanted to come home. And I wanted Lingyue to come home. And I wanted to sit on the steps in the Ossuary and let Xiaoyu shout at me."

Wanqing looked at him.

"That's a lot of wants," she said.

"I know."

"Good," said his mother. "Keep them." She let go of his face. "Don't let anything take them. Not above the sky. Not anywhere."

She lifted the microphone off her lap, and pressed the button, and said, in a quiet, sharp, slightly hoarse voice, on every speaker on the north wall of Lingzhou:

"They're home."

And all along the wall, in the dawn, the city breathed out.
