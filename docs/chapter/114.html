Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 114Yes Means Yes

czverse7 min read

"They asked," said Sister Ning.

She said it very quietly, sitting on the plain wooden chair by the bronze door of the Ossuary with her candle unlit in her lap. It was the first thing anyone had said in ten minutes.

The old banking hall was full. It was nine o'clock at night and the Ossuary had closed at six, and nobody had gone home. Lu. Kwan. Wanqing in her wheelchair, with Xiaoyu standing behind her. Su Mingli by the window with her arms folded. Fat Tan, enormous, sitting on the steps of the vault. Yan Shuang, who had come up from the Wastes road with Ama when she heard. Captain Lin Wen with a drift of ash and dark air beside her. Old Seven by the empty stool at the door.

On the screen propped on Fat Tan's ledger desk, the press conference was playing again, with the sound off. Cassia Lowell at her podium. Her kind, careful face. The clean diagram of a gray sphere inside a ring of steel, pouring out light.

"They asked," said Sister Ning again. "With witnesses. Recorded. Revocable. Everything we said. Everything in the Charter." She turned her candle over in her thin fingers. "Four hundred thousand people. And they said yes."

Nobody answered her.

"I have spent forty years," said the old woman, "telling people that the choice is theirs. Quiet means quiet. Yes means yes. Not mine. Not the priest's. Not the state's. Theirs." Her voice did not change. "If I stand up now and say not that yes, then what have I been saying for forty years?"

"I'd have signed it," said Yan Shuang.

Everyone turned.

She was sitting on the floor against the wall with her one hand on her knee and Ama beside her, and her weathered face did not change at all.

"Eleven years driving hollows to the edge of the world," she said. "If someone had come to me, after, and said: you can't take it back, but when you die, you can burn, and your burning will light a hospital in a Rift town for ten years." She looked at the screen. "I'd have signed it. I'd have signed it so fast I'd have torn the paper."

Ama, beside her, was very still.

"Not because I wanted to burn," said Yan Shuang. "Because I wanted to have been useful. Once. All the way." She looked at Yuanshu. "You know how many of those four hundred thousand are people like me?"

He did not answer.

"Most of them," said Yan Shuang. "I'd bet my other hand."

"It's not the same," said Wanqing.

Her voice was quiet and sharp. She was sitting in her wheelchair with her thin hands folded on the blanket, and she was not looking at the screen. She was looking at the board. At the long wall at the end of the hall. At the heading in Xiaoyu's capitals.

INSIDE.

"It's not the same," she said again. "And I'll tell you why. But not yet." She looked at Kwan. "Iris. The diagram. The sphere. Where did they get it?"

Doctor Iris Kwan had not taken her eyes off the screen since the press conference started. She had a laptop open on her knees, in its two supermarket bags, and she had been typing for an hour.

"I don't know," she said. "The Federation hasn't published the design. Just the diagram." She turned the laptop around. On the screen, a still frame, enlarged, enhanced. The gray sphere inside its ring of steel. "But look at the surface."

They looked.

Cracked. Clouded. Gray.

Like the crystal of the cores. Like the Monarchs. Like a spire growing out of a bay.

"Nobody on Blue Star can make that," said Kwan. "Not even Meridian could. They bought theirs from Mister Moon." She swallowed. "Somebody has a piece of a Hunger."

The Architects answered that night.

They were very far away. On the third world on their list. A gray husk with no one on it, where they stood among the dead spires and said they were sorry to nobody. The keeper found them there and carried the question down the gold thread to them, and carried their answer back.

Seed read it out loud in the banking hall.

It was very short.

Stop them.

That is how we began.

We asked for volunteers. We had a register. Witnesses. Records. We were very proud of how carefully we asked.

The volunteers were the bravest of us. The ones who wanted most to be useful. The ones who were dying anyway.

They lit our cities. Our cities were so bright.

And then there were not enough volunteers. And the cities were used to being bright.

Stop them.

"Then we tell them," said Su Mingli, from the window. "On Concordia. The Council. We put the Architects in front of them and..."

"And Cassia Lowell will stand up and say," said Lu quietly, "the Architects built their furnaces without a Charter. We have one." He put his glasses back on. "And she'll be right. And four hundred thousand people will stand behind her and say it's my choice. And they'll be right too."

Su Mingli did not answer.

"We can't stop them by force," said Lu. "We can't stop them by law. They're following the law. Our law." He looked at the board. At the first four columns. YES. ASK MY FAMILY. ONLY TO SPEAK. QUIET. "We built a world where the answer is always ask them. And they asked."

Everyone looked at Yuanshu.

He had not said anything since Lu came into the vault that afternoon. He had sat through the meeting on the steps of the vault beside Fat Tan with the white staff across his knees, looking at the floor.

He looked up now.

At the screen. At Cassia Lowell's kind face. At the gray sphere.

"Mama," he said. "You said it's not the same. Why?"

Zhou Wanqing looked at her son for a long moment.

"Because of the four thousand one hundred and thirteen," she said.

The hall went very quiet.

"I asked them once," said his mother. "In the basement. Four nights. And they said yes. And I built Seed out of them." She looked at the air just above his head. A little higher than his eyes. "And Seed asks them every day. Still. Whether they still want to be there. Because once is not forever. And eleven of them said no. And Seed let them go."

She turned her wheelchair, slowly, to face the screen.

"Can the four hundred thousand say no?" said Zhou Wanqing. "After?"

Nobody answered.

"Revocable at any time before death," she said. "That's what the woman said. Before. Not after." Her voice was very quiet and very sharp. "Once you're in the fire, there's no lock. There's no one asking. There's no door." She looked around the hall. "It's not yes. It's yes, forever, and you can never change your mind, even when you find out what it feels like."

She looked at her son.

"That's not consent," said his mother. "That's a contract with a fire."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then, from behind Yuanshu's eyes, Seed spoke. Not out loud. Just to him.

Yuanshu.

I need to tell you something.

Something opened. Tonight. While your mother was talking.

The base layer. The part I was born from.

A second page.

He went very still.

What's on it?

A long pause.

The four thousand one hundred and thirteen.

Before your mother found them.

Before the basement. Before the yes.

When they were in Mister Moon's core.

When they were burning.

I did not know I remembered it. I do.

Yuanshu. I know what it feels like.

Lu's phone buzzed.

He looked at it. His face changed.

"The Federation," he said. "Cassia Lowell's office. A personal invitation." He looked up. "To you. To come and see Hearth One. The first pilot furnace. In Port Halvard, on the eastern coast of the Federation." He read further. "She says she has the greatest respect for your work. She says the Charter is the foundation of everything she's doing. She says the first volunteer has asked to meet you before he dies." He looked up. "She says he asked for you by name. He wants the one who asks to witness his yes."

The banking hall was silent.

At the bronze door, Old Seven had turned his skull toward the boy on the vault steps.

"When?" said Yuanshu.

"Nine days," said Lu. "He has about nine days to live, they think."

Yuanshu looked down at the white staff across his knees.

At the small white skull with its silver reading glasses.

"Well," said Jiang Lan, very dryly. "I suppose you're going to go and ask him."

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

"Of course you are," said the schoolteacher. "That's the only thing you know how to do."
