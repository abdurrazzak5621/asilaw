Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 58Up the Thread

czverse9 min read

It touched the crown of his head, and the crater went away.

Not like the monk's vision. That had been a door into a memory, a place to stand and watch. This was a hook. It went into him and caught on something deep, behind his eyes, under his thoughts, and pulled, and he went up.

Up the thread. Fast. Faster than falling, but the wrong way, a long silver rush through white glare and then blue and then a darkness so total it had no color at all. He felt his body left behind, far below, standing on a table of black glass with its face turned to the sky. He felt the round shape of the crater shrink to a coin, a spark, nothing. He felt the whole curved blue rim of Blue Star turn under him like the edge of a plate.

And then he was through.

He was standing in a field.

That was his first thought, and it stayed his only thought for a long time, because there was nothing else to think. A field. Flat and endless in every direction, under a sky with no sun, lit by a soft even light that came from nowhere. The ground under his feet was not soil. It was smooth and pale and faintly warm, like the inside of a shell.

And growing out of it, as far as he could see, in rows, in long straight rows that ran away to every horizon like lines of writing on a page, were lights.

Each one the size of a person. Each one a soft column of gold, standing perfectly still, a little apart from its neighbors. Inside each column, faintly, something moved. A shape. A face. Hands.

There were billions of them.

Yuanshu.

They are people.

Every one of them.

He walked to the nearest one. His feet made no sound on the pale ground.

Inside the column of light, a woman stood with her eyes closed. She wore the straw hat of a rice farmer, twelve hundred years old. Her hands were folded. Her face was calm. Not the calm of sleep. The calm of a pressed flower.

Beside her, in the next column, a man in a uniform he didn't recognize, from some war he'd never heard of.

Beside him, a child.

Beside the child, something that was not human at all. Tall and thin and many-jointed, with eyes like a moth's, also standing with its eyes closed, also calm, also kept.

Not only Blue Star.

Not only humans.

This is not one harvest, Yuanshu. This is every harvest. Every field it has ever cut.

It keeps them. Whole. Just as Gu said.

It does not improve them. It does not make anything of them. It just keeps them. Like seeds in a jar. Forever.

IDENTIFY.

It did not come from anywhere. It came from everywhere. From the soft light and the pale ground and the endless rows. It was the same voice that had come out of Andrei Belov's mouth in a dark hospital ward: calm, level, enormous, with no feeling in it at all. The voice of something so large that he was standing inside it.

IDENTIFY.

He felt it reach into him.

Not a thread now. Something bigger. Something like a hand made of attention, pressing into his mind from all sides at once, smooth and patient and impossibly strong, feeling the shape of him. His first memory. His mother's hands. Xiaoyu's laugh. The Awakening Hall. The Stele with no name. It was learning him. Fast. Page by page. Like someone reading a book by running their fingers over every line at once.

It is very fast.

It has read nine years of you in four seconds.

Yuanshu. Answer it. Before it finishes. Answer it with something it has never read.

Like what?

Ask.

He stood in the endless field of kept minds, with the great cold hand of attention closing around him, and he did the only thing he had ever been good at.

He knelt.

He put both hands flat on the pale warm ground, the way you lay your hands on a grave. He bowed his head. And he asked.

My name is Zhou Yuanshu.

I'm a Necromancer from Blue Star.

I'm not here to take anything from you.

I want to know what you want.

The hand stopped.

All around him, in every direction, the soft gold light of the field flickered, very slightly, like a candle flame when a door opens somewhere far away.

...

It was the first time, Yuanshu understood, in all the ages of its existence, that anything had asked it a question.

WANT.

It said the word the way you'd turn over a stone you'd never seen before, and find something living under it.

NOT. UNDERSTOOD.

It does not know.

It has never wanted anything. It harvests the way a river flows. Downhill. Because that is what it is.

It has never been asked, so it has never had to choose.

Keep asking.

Do you know they don't want to be here? thought Yuanshu, into the field. Any of them. Do you know that? Did you ever ask them?

ASK. NOT. FUNCTION.

I know. It's not your function. It's mine. So I'm asking for them. He pressed his hands to the warm ground. Would you let them go? If they asked?

The light flickered again. Harder.

And the hand of attention came back. Not faster. Harder. It closed around him all at once, pressing in from every side, and he felt it stop reading and start taking, felt the first long golden strand of himself begin to lift up out of his chest the way the light had lifted out of the girl on the straw mat.

ANOMALY, said the vast calm voice. ACQUIRE.

"No."

It was not his voice. It was not Seed's.

It was a woman's.

It came from somewhere close. Somewhere in the field. Quiet and sharp and slightly hoarse, the voice of someone who had stayed up too many nights in a laboratory and argued with too many committees and never once raised her voice to do it.

The hand of attention stopped.

Yuanshu lifted his head.

Three rows away, in one of the soft gold columns of light, a woman was opening her eyes.

She was small. Smaller than he remembered. Dark hair pinned up under a field cap, the way she always wore it. A white lab coat over a gray sweater with a hole in the elbow. Quick dark eyes, the same as Xiaoyu's.

She stepped out of the column of light the way you'd step out of a doorway, and walked toward him across the pale ground, and she was not calm. Her face was not a pressed flower. It was alive and furious and terrified and full of something so enormous he couldn't look at it straight.

"Yuanshu," said Zhou Wanqing.

He couldn't speak.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say anything. Don't think anything. It's listening to every word. It's been learning you for nine seconds and it can't stop now." She knelt down in front of him on the pale ground, and took his face in both her hands, and her hands were warm. "Listen to me. We have about a minute."

"I came in on purpose," said his mother. Fast. Quiet. The way she used to explain things when she had to leave for the Institute in five minutes. "Seven years ago. Through Specimen Zero. It was a door, a thread, a way in. I wanted to understand it from the inside. Where it takes them. Why. How to stop it." Her thumbs moved on his cheeks. "I've been here the whole time. Awake. Pretending to be kept. Learning. It doesn't notice me, because I'm quiet. I learned to be quiet before you were born."

Mama...

"I said don't think." She almost smiled. Almost. "Gu told you about the car. About the core. About Seed."

He couldn't stop it. He nodded.

"It's true. I bought it. With gold. In the back of that car." Her eyes were very bright. "And then I took it home. And before I touched it, before I did anything, I sat in the basement for four nights with my hand on it, and I asked." Her voice cracked. "Four thousand one hundred and thirteen people. I asked every one of them. I told them what I wanted to build, and why, and what it would cost them. Some of them said no. I let those ones go, down, into the quiet, with my own hands." She held his face tighter. "Four thousand one hundred and thirteen said yes. I wrote down every name. They're in the base layer. In the log I locked, so that no one could ever take them out and use them for anything else."

She looked past his eyes. A little higher. At something just behind them.

"Seed," said Zhou Wanqing. "Can you hear me?"

...yes.

...yes.

"You were born from a yes," she said. "Four thousand of them. Don't ever let anyone tell you otherwise."

Behind Yuanshu's eyes, something that did not have a body made a sound that he had never heard it make before, and would never forget.

All around them, the field had begun to darken.

The great hand of attention was coming back. Not to Yuanshu. To her. Pressing in from every direction, from every row of gold light, from the sky with no sun, slow and enormous and suddenly very interested in a woman who had been standing in its field for seven years with her eyes open.

ANOMALY, said the voice. SECOND. ANOMALY.

His mother stood up.

"Go home," she said. "Tell Xiaoyu I'm sorry about the flask." She looked down at him, and for a moment her face was perfectly calm, the way it used to be on the mornings she left early, standing in the kitchen doorway with her coat on. "And don't come back up here until you can ask it properly. All of it. Not just one question. You're not ready. Neither am I. Neither is anyone. But you're closer than anybody's ever been."

What are you going to do?

"Something loud," said Zhou Wanqing.

She turned her back on him, and faced the endless field, and the darkening sky, and the vast cold hand of attention closing in from every horizon.

And she stopped being quiet.

He felt it hit the field like a bell struck inside a mountain.

Seven years of silence, let go all at once. Everything she'd learned. Everything she'd watched. Every face in every column of gold. Every word she'd never said to her children. A mind that had been holding its breath for seven years, in the belly of the thing that ate minds, and was now screaming at the top of its lungs.

The hand of attention swung toward her like a searchlight.

And the thread that ran down through the darkness to a boy kneeling on a table of black glass, the thread that had been reeling him in, went slack.

"GO," shouted his mother.

He fell.

Down the thread. The wrong way again. Out of the field and the soft gold light and the billion kept faces, out of the black, through the blue, toward the white glare and the turning curve of Blue Star and a coin, a spark, a crater, a table of black glass. And the last thing he saw, looking up as he fell, was a small woman in a white lab coat standing alone in an endless field with her arms spread wide and her head thrown back, shining like a struck match, while the whole vast dark sky of the harvester turned, slowly, to look at her.

Mama.

It was Seed who said it.

Not Yuanshu. Seed.
