Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 288Anthos

czverse11 min read

Anthos burned on purpose, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and he understood at once why nobody who lived there had ever wanted to stop.

They came out of the last fold on the fortieth day, the flagship and the twelve small lights of the Rowed Fleet together, and the window of the observation deck filled with a world.

Not gray, like Corraine. Not blue and small, like Blue Star. Gold. A world of light. Its whole night side lit, from pole to pole, with cities that ran into each other like rivers of fire, towers and spires and domes and bridges, every one of them burning, white and gold and the colors of a thousand Houses, so that from orbit Anthos looked like a lamp that someone had lit and turned all the way up and never turned down. Rings of light round it, in orbit: stations, yards, docks, the long glittering curve of the shipyards where the Great Houses built their cathedrals. And above the largest city, on the dayside terminator, where night met morning, a single building so vast he could see it from orbit: a dome of black stone, open at the top, like a bowl turned to the sky.

"The Assembly," said Iselde. She had come across from the Eighth Candle to stand beside him at the window. "Four thousand seats. One for every House and every world on the Ladder." She did not look away from it. "It's built from the stone of the first reading-hall on Anthos. They say if you stand in the center and speak, every seat can hear you."

He looked at the bowl of black stone on the edge of the night.

"We have one like that," he said. "Four thousand seats. In Lingzhou. On East Ring Road."

"What is it?"

"The Awakening Hall," said Yuanshu. "Where the stone is. Where everyone used to go to be told what they were."

The twenty-nine were waiting.

He saw them as the flagship came round the curve of the world. Twenty-nine cathedrals of the Great Houses, the ones that had folded away from the Shallows burning, the ones whose Paragons and Captains had been asked on the ninth night what they wanted and said to be first. Hanging in orbit over Anthos in a great curved line, like a wall. Lit from end to end. Burning the old way. Between the Rowed Fleet and the world.

One million and thirty-four thousand people in their walls.

They are burning harder than when they left. I can feel it. Tam can hear it.

They are afraid.

Not the people in the walls. The ones on the bridges.

Tam, at the window, had his eyes shut.

"They were asked," he said. "On the ninth night. They answered. Someone heard." His voice was steady. "And now the Captains have turned the burn up. To drown it out. So they can't think." He opened his eyes. "They're thinking anyway."

The voice came at once.

Not Severin's. A woman's. Through the flagship's own transmitter, which Seed said had not been used to receive anything in forty-one years except orders. Old. Very old. As precise as Iselde's had been on the first morning and as cold as Severin's had been on the radio over Lingzhou. With a grade in every sentence.

Dacian Severin. SSS. Formerly First of the Ladder.

This is Aurelle Teller. SSS. Paragon of the House of Teller. Speaker of the Assembly, in the absence of a First.

You have returned to Anthos in a ship rowed by cores taken without a Festival. You have brought with you the Lens of Severin, which is held by an unknown intelligence. You have brought with you the ungraded one of Unlisted Orchard Four-Four-Seven-One, who stilled the Reaper and emptied the arrays.

You have been absent from the Ladder for ninety-two days. The Ladder has no First. Precedence has failed in every House. The faces of every Paragon in the Federation darkened on the ninth night of your absence and have not recovered.

The Assembly will sit in three days. You will attend. The ungraded one will attend.

The twenty-nine Houses who returned have asked the Assembly to restore the Ladder.

They have asked that the Lens be broken, and its contents with it, and a new First chosen.

On the observation deck, Lingyue had put her hand flat on Xiaodeng's back in the sling.

She did not ask anything.

But she said asked. Three times.

I do not think she noticed.

The First of the Ladder answered from the Lens chamber.

He had gone there the moment the voice began. Not running; he did not run. He walked down the long corridor from the observation deck to the bow of his ship at the same even pace, with the cracked face in his pocket, and went into the chamber where the disc of black stone the width of a courtyard stood, ground so thin you could see through it, with a lamp burning in it and a fog-ringer talking. And he sat down on the floor with his back against the wall, the way he had sat for ninety days, and spoke.

Seed carried it. To the observation deck. And Ichiro, in the Lens, who caught everything, carried it further, though nobody had asked him to and he said afterward that he would apologize if anyone minded and nobody did.

"Aurelle," said Dacian Severin. Without a grade. "I'll come. To the Assembly. In three days."

A pause.

"And I'm telling you first," he said. Badly. Like a word in a book. "The Lens is not going to be broken. If it breaks, every face in the Federation breaks with it. It is the root. Every stone was cut from it. The House of Severin kept it for nine thousand years and did not know."

Silence on the other end.

"And there are two people in it," said the former First of the Ladder. "A fog-ringer and a nun. From a world in the Shallows. They were asked to go in. They said yes." He paused again. "If the Assembly wants them out, the Assembly can ask them."

They went down to the yards the next morning.

Not to the city. Iselde said the city could wait. She said there was someone in the yards who had been waiting for nearly a year, and had sent a mark through the Lens forty days ago to say so, and would throw something at all of them if they went anywhere else first.

The yards at Anthos were a ring of steel and light in low orbit, kilometers wide, where the Great Houses built their ships and docked them and pressed their cores. Most of it burned. The docks of the twenty-nine. The great slips where new cathedrals stood half-built. But one arc of the ring, a long curve of empty docks where eleven ships stood with dark walls that could not fold, did not burn. It glowed. Low. The color of the Low Fire.

And in its halls, in the long bright rooms where the Houses had once kept the cradles and the pressing-frames, children.

Thousands. Twelve in the body. Twenty and thirty and fifty inside. The unpressed of seven Houses, who had come out of the walls of eleven ships over ten months and had not gone home, because their homes were fuel orchards whose families had taken the payment, and they had asked, Iselde said, to stay where someone would ask them things. They had painted the walls. They had planted things in the cradles. They walked barefoot, mostly, still, and held each other's hands, the one behind holding the one in front.

And at the end of the longest hall, by a window that looked down at the burning world, a girl.

She was taller.

He saw that first. Not by much. A year with a sky. Twelve in the body, still, and twenty-three inside, and something in the way she stood that was not either age. A girl with short dark hair and Iselde's sharp face and a gray coat too big for her, standing at a window with her back to the hall.

She turned round when she heard them.

She looked at her sister. At Lingyue, with the baby in the sling and the sword and the lamp. At Old Seven, one-armed, in the doorway, two meters ten. At Tam and Seri, barefoot. At the boy with the staff.

"You came," said Maelis Varr-Anthos.

"You told me I'd better."

"I did." She almost smiled. "It's been a year. Nearly. Three hundred and forty days. I counted. My mother says I count like a C." She looked at Old Seven in the doorway, one-armed, and her face changed. "What happened to your arm?"

He says: the First of the Ladder.

He says: it was the sergeant's anyway. It's buried by a stone with dumplings on it.

Maelis looked at the tall dead captain for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way the Candle's children nodded when someone told them a true thing that was not good.

"He's on your ship," she said. "The First. My mother says he sits on the floor."

"Yes."

"Good," said Maelis.

She took him to the window. She did not take anyone else; she asked Iselde to stay by the door, and Iselde did, and Lingyue stood by her with the baby, and Old Seven stood in the doorway facing in, for once. And Maelis Varr-Anthos stood at a window in the yards at Anthos looking down at a world that burned on purpose, beside the boy who had asked her to wait a year.

"You said: ask me again in a year," she said. "When I've had a year with a sky. When I've seen what I'd be giving up."

"Yes."

"I asked to go into the stone. The one on Anthos. The one that read me. So no other twelve-year-old on Anthos would say yes to the honor without being asked first."

"Yes."

She looked down at the burning world.

"My mother asked me what I want," she said. "Every day. Ninety-one days now. She hasn't stopped. Through the stone. Marks. Clumsy ones. Like a child's knot. Every day the same words. Maelis, this is your mother, what do you want." She did not look at him. "She's never said the same thing twice to anyone in her life. My sister says."

"Iselde told me. On the way."

"I didn't answer for seventy days," said Maelis. "And on the seventy-first, I did."

She was quiet for a while.

"I said: I want to grow up."

He did not say anything.

Below them, Anthos burned. Rivers of light running into each other across the night side, white and gold and a thousand colors, every tower lit, every bridge, every dome, all the way up and never turned down. And on the edge of the night, on the terminator, a bowl of black stone open to the sky.

"I don't want to go into the stone," said Maelis Varr-Anthos. Very steadily. In the Federation's language, without the grade. "I wanted to, a year ago. I meant it. I'd been in a wall for eleven years and the stone had told me I was an E and the House had given me to the fold and the only thing I could think of that would make any of it mean anything was to go into the stone and stop it happening to anyone else." Her hands were flat on the window. "And then I had a year. And I learned to wear shoes. And I planted things in a cradle and they grew. And an engineer called Torvin taught everyone in the yards a card game with no queens in it, because he said he'd heard it was how you taught someone not to cheat. And my mother asked me every day what I wanted."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"I'm twelve," she said. "I've been twelve for twelve years. I never got to be thirteen. I want to be thirteen. I want to be twenty. I want to find out what I'm like when I'm forty. I want to be someone who was never anything but a girl who grew up."

She was crying. She did not seem to have noticed.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I said I'd go into the stone. I'm sorry. I'm saying no."

...Yuanshu.

Tell her.

He told her.

"Don't be sorry," said Zhou Yuanshu. "That's the best answer anyone's ever given me."

Maelis looked at him.

"I asked you to wait a year so you'd know what you'd be giving up," he said. "You found out. And you decided you didn't want to give it up." He looked at the window, at the burning world, at the girl standing at it. "That's not failing. That's the whole point. That's what being asked is for. So you can say no and mean it."

She stood at the window for a long time.

"Then who goes in?" she said. "The stone on Anthos. Somebody has to. Or the children keep getting read."

"I don't know," said Yuanshu. "Not you."

And from the doorway, behind them, where she had been standing beside Lingyue with her hands at her sides and her face doing what it had done on the bridge of the Eighth Candle eleven months ago, Iselde said, very quietly:

"My mother knows who."

Maelis turned round.

"She sent a mark this morning," said Iselde. "Through the Lens. Ichiro caught it. She asked him to give it to me and not you, so you'd have your answer first." She held up her hand, palm open, and in it, a small gray knot, clumsy, like a child's. "It says: tell Maelis I am proud she said no. It says: tell her I said yes for her once, without asking, and I will not do it again."

"And?"

"And it says," said Iselde Varr-Anthos, "the Matriarch of the House of Anthos would like to be asked. In the Assembly. In front of everyone. What she wants."

She closed her hand on the knot.

"She says she has an answer. She says she has been practicing it for ninety-one days."
