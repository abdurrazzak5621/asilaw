Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 103Halfway

czverse7 min read

The Architects came back on the fourth day, walking across the dark.

Not riding a current. Walking. Seven tall angular shapes in gray, coming toward the half-raft out of the black, single file, their long thin limbs folding at too many joints, each step landing on nothing, the way the dead had walked through the threads on the Wastes. The seams down the fronts of their long smooth faces were open. A soft gray light shone out of each one.

They stepped onto the raft one by one, and folded down, and sat in a circle at its center, the way they had sat in the cold place.

The oldest one turned its long head toward Yuanshu.

...the first world on our list, it said, in its voice like grinding stone. ...we reached it.

...we stood on it. On the gray. Among the spires.

...we said we were sorry. Out loud. As we said we would.

"Did anyone hear?"

The old thing was silent.

...no.

...there was no one left to hear.

...we said it anyway.

...we will go back. After. We have a very long list.

The second ring came to meet them.

Seed watched it every day. A gray speck in the far dark, past the far small lights. Growing. Not fast. Not the way the first one had grown, rushing toward the Lantern world. Slowly. Carefully. The way someone walks toward a frightened animal, a step at a time, stopping, waiting, stepping again.

It is moving at less than half the speed of the first.

The Sixth said it is starving. Ten million years of burning low.

It is weak. It is slow.

And it is coming anyway.

I think the Sixth are steering it.

As much as they can. A little at a time. For the first time in ten million years.

The Elder said it would take thirty-one days instead of sixty.

Halfway.

They were the strangest thirty-one days of Yuanshu's life.

Nothing happened. That was what made them strange. For the first time since the Awakening Hall, nothing was trying to eat anyone. Nothing was screaming. Nothing was falling out of the sky. The raft rode a long slow silver current through the dark, and the second ring grew in front of them, and the gold thread ran out behind them all the way home, and every day Bai Lu wound her brass clock and made everyone eat something and sleep.

And they talked.

The twins taught Hush to play cards.

It did not understand the rules. It did not understand winning, except at the game of statues, which it said was the only real game. But it liked the colors on the cards. It would sit in Old Seven's hand and hold one card up with a single thin limb and look at it for an hour. The queen of hearts, mostly. It said the red was the color the Lanterns used for the feeling of someone you love being nearby.

The Mo twins let it keep the queen of hearts.

Bai Lu sat with the dead.

Every day. An hour in the morning, an hour at night, by her clock. She would walk along the rim of the raft, among the sixty-two soldiers of the Third Battalion standing their watch, and stop at each one, and put her hands on them. Not healing. She could not heal the dead. She had tried for two days on Old Seven's arm and failed.

But she said she could feel where it hurt.

Old breaks. Cracks in bones that had been cracked for thirty-one years, from the eastern wall, from the long walk north. Places where their embers flickered and pulled. And when she put her hands on them, and let the soft green light go into the cracks, the embers steadied. Just a little.

"It doesn't fix anything," she said, when Yuanshu asked her. "I know it doesn't. They're dead. They don't need fixing." She looked down at her hands. "But I think it helps. I think it's like holding someone's hand in a hospital. It doesn't make them better. It makes them less alone."

Sergeant Huo, who had stood very still while she held her glowing hands against the stump of his left shoulder, raised his remaining hand and touched the top of her head. Once. The way you'd touch a child's head in a crowd to make sure she was still there.

On the nineteenth night, Lingyue asked him about the box.

They were sitting at the rim of the raft, beside the dead captain standing in his doorway, looking out at the second ring. It was big now. Bigger than a moon. Gray and cracked and frosted all over with something pale, like rime on a winter window. And in its center, very faint, a dull red glow. Not a furnace. A coal. Banked.

"In the Ledger," said Lingyue. Not looking at him. "Who may raise you. I put your name."

"I know."

"You never said anything."

"You told me not to make you use it."

"I know what I said." She ran her thumb along the flat of her sword. "I'm asking what you thought."

He thought about it. Honestly. The way he thought about everything he was asked.

"I thought," he said slowly, "that I've asked over a million people what they want done when they die. And I've raised thousands. And I've laid down two hundred thousand. And nobody, not once, ever told me they wanted only me." He looked at the gray ring. "Everyone else writes ask my family. Or quiet. Or yes, and then lets anyone with the Death Law do it, because it doesn't matter who, as long as they ask."

"It matters to me who," said Lingyue.

"I know."

"So what did you think?"

He was quiet.

"I thought," he said, "that I'd better not die before you do. Because then nobody could."

Lingyue did not say anything for a while.

Then she put her sword down on the pale ground beside her. Carefully. The way you'd put down something you might need again.

"That's the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me," said Shen Lingyue. "And it was about corpses."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry." She leaned sideways, just a little, until her shoulder touched his. And stayed there. "It was a good answer."

At the other end of the rim, Old Seven turned his skull very deliberately away from them, and looked out at the dark, and so did sixty-two soldiers of the Third Battalion, all at once, as if they had been ordered to.

Behind Yuanshu's eyes, Seed said nothing at all.

Very loudly.

On the thirty-first day, the second ring stopped.

It stopped perhaps a thousand kilometers away. Hanging in the black. Turning, very slowly. Enormous. Bigger than Blue Star. A great cracked frosted wheel of gray stone, its surface covered in mouths, tens of thousands of them, like the mouths of the Monarch, like the mark on Fat Tan's cores.

Every one of them was closed.

Shut. Sealed. Frosted over. As if they had not opened for a very long time.

And in the center, where the furnace was, the dull red glow of the banked coal. Pulsing. Very slowly. Like the heartbeat of something asleep.

They stood at the rim of the half-raft and stared at it.

"It's not doing anything," said Mo Xing.

"It's waiting," said Mo Shi.

...it is asking, said Hush softly, from Old Seven's hand.

They looked down at it.

...it is not moving. It is not opening. It is not doing anything.

...that is how we used to ask, on my world. When we met someone new. We would stop. Very still. And wait. To show them we were not going to do anything they did not want.

...it is asking if we want to come in.

Yuanshu walked to the very edge of the raft.

He stood beside a dead captain in a doorway, with the white staff in his hand, and looked across a thousand kilometers of dark at a ring that had eaten worlds for longer than his sun had been lit.

He went quiet. And then, very carefully, he let himself be heard. Just a little. Just enough.

We came, he said. Like we said we would. We'd like to come in.

If you want us to.

Nothing. The frost held.

Then, on the vast frosted face of the second ring, directly in front of them, one mouth opened.

Just one. Out of tens of thousands. Slowly. The frost on its lips cracking and falling away in a glittering cloud. Not wide. Not hungry. Just open. Enough. The way a door is opened by someone who has been waiting on the other side of it for a very long time, and is not quite sure they are allowed to hope.

And from inside it, very faint, across a thousand kilometers of dark, a light.

Not red. Not the gray-red of a furnace.

Low. Soft. Gold.

Like a candle in a window.
