Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 138The Juniper

czverse7 min read

They did not raise Lu Zhengtian, and they did not read him, and the whole city came to watch them not do it.

That was Sister Ning's idea. She said it on the morning after the round chamber, sitting on the wooden chair by the bronze door of the Ossuary with her candle in her lap, when the Bureau asked the Quiet what a funeral was for someone who had chosen QUIET in the Ledger. Nobody had ever needed one before. The Ledger was a year and a half old. Its first QUIET name had been Lu's, in his own handwriting, on the night nine people in a lodge invented it.

"You let people see it," she said. "That's all. You let them see someone keep their word to the dead."

So on the twentieth day, they carried him through Lingzhou.

Not on a gun carriage. On a plain wooden stretcher, under a plain gray cloth, carried by six of the living, because he had said don't ask me and the dead could not carry him without being asked to. Su Mingli carried the front left corner. Captain Lin Wen the front right. Doctor Kwan, who was too small for it and would not give it up. Hu Dabao. Fat Tan, enormous, with tears running down into his collar. And Zhou Yuanshu, at the back, with the white staff strapped across his shoulders so that his hands were free.

The dead lined the route. Nineteen thousand of them, the whole First Legion and the cemetery companies and the eleven hundred from the outer face of the wall, who had come in off their post for one morning and stood in the streets of the city they had said they would never be seen in again. They did not salute. They had not been asked to. They stood in their nines along both sides of the road from the Institute to the War Memorial Cemetery and turned their skulls away as the stretcher passed, every one, so that the man under the cloth would not be looked at by anyone he had not chosen.

Behind them, the living. Three million, or near enough. On the pavements and the balconies and the rooftops. Many of them had candles. None of the candles were lit.

They buried him at the top of the hill, beside Zhao's gate.

Not inside it. Beside it. A plain stone with his name and no dates and, under the name, one word in Xiaoyu's careful capitals, because she had asked to cut it and the stonemason had let her.

QUIET.

Sister Ning stood at the head of the grave. She did not give a speech. She held her candle, unlit, for a long minute in front of the three million and the nineteen thousand and the Kings, and then she bent down and set it on the stone, still unlit, and stepped back.

Nobody lit it. That was the point.

Gu Hanshan came to the Ossuary that night with a small twisted juniper in a clay pot.

He had walked from the Listening Pavilion with it in both hands, a Bureau officer on either side of him, because he was still serving his sentence and was not allowed to walk anywhere alone. He was thinner than he had been at his trial. His cardigan was the same. He stood inside the bronze door with the plant held against his chest and looked at the board on the far wall for a long time, at the long columns of names, and then at Yuanshu, sitting on the vault steps.

"He said to give it to me," said Gu. "Lin Wen told me."

"Yes."

"He said I'd know." Gu looked down at the juniper. It was very old and very carefully pruned. "I gave it to him. Seven years ago. The week your mother went into the light. I found him in his flat at three in the morning sitting on the floor in his suit, with all the lights off, and I didn't know what to say. So I went home and dug up the oldest thing in my garden and brought it to him. I said a man who lives alone should have something to keep alive." He turned the pot a little in his hands. "He watered it every day. Too much. I told him every time I visited. He never listened."

He was quiet.

"I used to think he kept it because of me," said Gu. "Because I was kind to him. Because something in me had learned how to be loved, and he was lonely, and it used that." His voice did not change. "I don't know if it was that. I'll never know now. I didn't ask him while I could."

"You could ask the juniper," said Xiaoyu from the registration desk. Then, at Gu's face: "Sorry. That was stupid."

"No," said Gu Hanshan. He almost smiled. "It wasn't. I'll water it properly. That's a kind of asking." He held the clay pot a little closer. "On Thursdays. With your mother's lemon tree."

The Concordia Council met in emergency session on the twenty-first day, by screen, from five capitals and one crater.

Yuanshu sat in the old banking hall under the board with Su Mingli and Orlov and Shirasagi Ren, in front of a screen propped on Fat Tan's desk. On it, five delegations and Chair Adaeze Okafor in the crater, with her hands folded on the black glass table.

The Atlantic Federation's new Secretary spoke first. He was a careful man with a careful voice, and he did not raise it.

"Eleven thousand people," he said. "Witnessed. Registered. Each of them had made a recorded choice about their own mind. Each of them was removed from public land against that choice, by force, at the order of a man who has spent two years telling the world that quiet means quiet." He paused. "I am not asking the Council to punish anyone. Mister Zhou may well have saved Lingzhou. I am asking what the Charter means now. Because if a yes means yes only when he agrees with it, I don't know what we signed."

Nobody on the screen answered him.

Yuanshu stood up.

"He's right," he said.

The banking hall was very still.

"I moved them," said Yuanshu. "They'd said yes, and I moved them anyway. The Charter doesn't have a word for that. It should." He looked at the screen. At Okafor. "Their choice was about their own minds. I'd defend it to anyone. But they made it sitting less than a kilometer from something that would have taken three million people who'd never been asked. Their yes was in someone else's window." He took a breath. "If you want to write that into the Charter, write it. Your yes is yours. It stops at someone else's window. And if I was wrong to move them, then say so, in writing, and I'll stand in front of the Council and hear it."

Chair Adaeze Okafor looked at him for a while across the screen.

"Article Ten," she said finally. "Drafting committee. Thirty days." She unfolded her hands. "Mister Zhou will not be on it. He'll be a witness."

"Yes, Chair."

"And Mister Zhou," said Okafor. "The next time you move eleven thousand people, ask the Council first. We'd have said yes. But we'd like to have been asked."

The Abbot's second message came at midnight.

Same room. Same cushion. Same calm, kind face. He looked, Yuanshu thought, watching it on Lu's tablet, which Kwan had given him because nobody else knew what to do with it, exactly like a man who had just heard good news.

"The scout is gone," said Kurose Tōya. "I'm sorry for the man who died. I'm told he was brave. I'm told he was Quiet, and that you kept his choice." He bowed his head slightly. "That was kind of you, Mister Zhou. That was what we asked for too."

He looked up.

"But before it went," said the Abbot, "it rang. And its sisters heard. All of them. We were listening." His face did not change. "The lamp in Lingzhou has gone out. The others are lit. The word has gone from one to the next, the way a word goes down a line of people holding hands. We don't need the scout any more. We have what it said."

He folded his hands.

"We'll take it somewhere high," he said. "Where there's no one to move us. And we'll give it a voice."

The screen went dark.

He is telling the truth.

I can feel them. All four. Since the round chamber. They are not quiet any more. They are humming. Like a bell that has been struck and has not stopped.

Two of them are moving. The one at sea, and the one in the mountains of Yamato.

The two in the west are not. The ones that were close together.

I know where they are now. I can feel the ground around them.

It is full of graves.
