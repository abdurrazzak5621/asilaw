Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 196Day 398

czverse9 min read

The message from inside the Mouth came on the twentieth night after the wall, and it was forty-one seconds long.

Xiaoyu could not read it out. Her voice was still a whisper. So Seed read it, through Fat Tan's speaker in the wall, to the old banking hall at three in the morning: to Yuanshu on the vault steps with the staff, and Xiaoyu on the stepladder with the marker in her hand waiting, and Kwan, and Fat Tan, and Sister Ning on the wooden chair by the bronze door. And Old Seven, who came in last from the hill, the way he always did, and stood by the door.

Day 398.

Worlds asked: 41. Burning low: 34.

We have stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. Eleven days ago. The Sixth felt it. The Mouth was drifting, very slow, and then it was not drifting. It is sitting in the dark. Nothing is pulling it anywhere.

The fires are very quiet now. They do not mutter. They sigh. Like a stove when the last log falls in.

The Sixth say: weeks. Not months. Not years. Weeks.

It is getting cold. The cousins are cold. Two hundred and eighty-one million minds who have never been cold are cold. They are very frightened. We sit close together. The sergeant has put the men around the edge of the quiet part, where the curtain was, so that the cold comes to them first. He says they are dead and do not mind.

Liang Fu asked me today where home is. Not the way he asked before. The new way. Like a person asking about a place he would like to see.

I said I would ask the captain.

I still have the queen of hearts.

Tell the captain. — H.

Nobody said anything.

Xiaoyu wrote it on the wall. All of it. In her careful capitals, on the stepladder, at the end of the INSIDE column, which had gone round the corner onto the next wall months ago. Under it, small, the seconds. 41. And under that, without being asked, smaller still:

ASK THE CAPTAIN.

She climbed down.

Old Seven had not moved from the bronze door.

I was wrong.

I said eleven months. From the night Xiaoyu showed me the seconds. I said it would stop at forty-four raft-days in about eleven months.

It stopped in seven. At thirty-nine.

I want to know why. Not because it matters now. Because I was wrong, and I would like to know how.

"Go on," said Yuanshu.

I modeled the Mouth as a stone thrown up in the air. Rising. Slowing. Stopping. The way a thing stops when nothing is pushing it.

But something was pushing it.

The voice behind the curtain. The Collector. It was in the Mouth from the beginning. Hush heard it on Day 178. It took Liang Fu's want on Day 209, and his want pointed home, and it followed. I think it was steering the Mouth. Slowly. The way you lean in a boat to make it turn.

On Day 230, the monks looked at it. It went out of the curtain. Away.

And the Mouth had nothing pushing it any more.

I put the Day 230 change into the model. It stops at thirty-nine raft-days. Eleven days ago.

I was not wrong about the stone. I was wrong about who was throwing it.

Kwan had her laptop open on her knees. She looked at the curve on the screen for a long time.

"So it was coming here," she said. "The Mouth. Before the monks. It was being steered here."

Toward Liang Fu's home. Yes. I think so. Slowly. It would have taken years.

And then a nun under a gate looked at the thing that was steering it, and it let go, and the Mouth stopped thirty-nine raft-days away, and is getting cold.

"And if it hadn't let go?"

Then two hundred and eighty-one million burning minds and two Hungers' worth of fire would have arrived over Blue Star in about four years, steered by something that keeps what you want.

I do not know what it was going to do with them.

I would rather not know.

"Weeks," said Yuanshu.

He said it to the keeper, at four, when the Elder came down from the north and stood in the middle of the banking hall in the lamplight with the gold thread running through her.

...I HEARD.

...THE ECHO IS VERY FAINT NOW. THE MOUTH IS SO QUIET. BUT I HEARD.

"You said: ask again when it's cold."

...IT IS NOT COLD YET.

...THE FIRES ARE SIGHING. NOT OUT. I CAN STILL FEEL THEM. LIKE TWO ANIMALS ASLEEP IN THE NEXT VALLEY. IF I GO NEAR, THEY MAY WAKE.

...WHEN THEY ARE OUT. REALLY OUT.

...THEN ASK.

"I will," said Yuanshu. "I'm going to ask you something else first."

...ASK.

"The fields. Along the arm. You said the furthest one went dark."

The gold thread went still through the Elder.

...TWO.

...TWO HAVE GONE DARK. THE FURTHEST. AND THEN, NINE NIGHTS AGO, THE NEXT. ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY RAFT-DAYS.

...THEY WERE THERE. GOLD. AND THEN THEY WERE NOT.

Twenty-six raft-days between them. Nine nights between them going dark.

Something is moving along the arm toward us. Pruning. One field at a time. Three raft-days a night.

I am going to say it as a number, and then I am going to tell you how much I do not know.

If it keeps the same speed, and comes the same way, it reaches the keeper's field in about sixty nights.

I do not know if it will keep the same speed. I do not know if it is coming here, or past. I do not know what it is. I am guessing it is the gardener, or something the gardener sent. I am sixty-four percent sure.

And Yuanshu.

The Mouth is at thirty-nine raft-days. Along the arm. Between us and it.

He sat on the vault steps under the long white wall for a long time after the Elder had gone.

Kwan had gone home. Fat Tan had gone upstairs. Xiaoyu was asleep on the marble floor under the board with her marker in her hand, the way she fell asleep most nights now. Sister Ning was still on the wooden chair by the bronze door, with her candle, unlit, in her lap. And Old Seven, standing beside her.

"Seed," he said. "Run it."

I have.

The Mouth gets cold in weeks. The Sixth say weeks. Three, perhaps five.

The keeper will carry us when it is cold. Thirty-nine raft-days there. Thirty-nine back. Plus however long it takes to get them out.

Something is pruning along the arm toward the keeper. About sixty nights. It will pass the Mouth on the way. Perhaps forty nights from now. Perhaps fewer.

If we wait until the Mouth is cold, and then go, and it takes thirty-nine days to get there.

We arrive at about the same time.

He did not say anything.

I am not going to tell you the probability.

You did not ask. And I do not have one I believe.

Sister Ning stood up from the wooden chair.

She did it slowly, the way she did everything, with the unlit candle in her hand, and walked across the marble floor between the sleeping girl and the long white wall, and stopped at the foot of the vault steps, and looked up at him.

"You're going to go," she said.

"I haven't asked anyone yet."

"I know. That's not what I said." Her quiet, exact voice did not change. "I've known you since a cemetery. You've asked everyone everything since the night you raised three hundred soldiers without a permit." She held the candle in both hands. "You're going to go. Not because Old Seven wants you to. Not because Seed does. Because there are sixty-one men inside a dying fire who were raised on this world and walked off it on a raft because you asked them to, and a child who has been holding a playing card for four hundred days, and they're cold."

He did not answer.

"So ask," said Sister Ning. "Properly. All of them. Article Eleven. Bring it to the Council. Ask the world, if the world has to be asked. Ask the dead. Ask the living who'd go. Tell all of them what you'll do with the answer." She looked at him. "And then go. Before you've finished being afraid. You'll never finish."

Old Seven, by the bronze door, put the butt of his spear down on the marble. Once. Very softly.

He says that is what he would have said.

He says he would have said it shorter.

Sister Ning almost smiled.

"Tell him," she said, "that I've had thirty years of practice saying things longer than they need to be. Somebody has to." She turned to go back to her chair. Then stopped, and looked at the INSIDE column, round the corner on the next wall, where Xiaoyu had written ASK THE CAPTAIN.

"And tell the captain," she said, "to start packing."

His mother was awake when he went into the back room at dawn.

She was always awake at dawn now. She said it was because she had spent seven years in a gold column with her eyes shut and did not want to miss any more mornings. She was in her chair by the window with the blanket over her knees and the first gray light coming in from the courtyard, and she looked at him when he came in and did not say anything, because she had heard. Of course she had heard. The speaker in the wall reached the back room.

He knelt beside her chair so that their faces were level.

"I'm going to ask you," he said. "You don't have to answer."

"I know how you ask, Yuanshu."

"Do you want me to go?"

Zhou Wanqing looked at him for a long time. At her son, twenty years old now, a Lord, with the soft edges still in his eyes from looking at six lamps at once, and blood dried in the creases of his ears that Bai Lu had missed.

"No," she said.

He waited.

"No, I don't want you to go. I want you to stay in this city and sit on those steps and let your sister shout at you and grow old." Her thin hand found his on the arm of the chair. "I've been in a column. I've been in a fire. I know what's out there. I don't want you anywhere near it." She held on. "That's what I want. You asked."

"I know."

"And you're going anyway."

"Yes."

"Good," said his mother. "That's what I'd do." She let go of his hand and folded hers on the blanket. "I'll hold the city with your sister. I've done it before. I'm better at it than Su Mingli thinks." She looked out of the window at the courtyard, where the light was coming up. "Bring the child home. And the soldiers. And the corporal who wants to know where home is." She was quiet. "And then come home yourself, and tell me what you wanted, the whole time. I'd like to know. I've never once heard you say."
