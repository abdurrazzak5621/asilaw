Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 275Ninety

czverse10 min read

They took a day to cross the Shallows, and when they arrived, they did not crinkle. They lit.

He watched it from the north wall with half of Lingzhou. Kwan had told the radio at dawn that the line of crinkles at the far edge of the arm had begun to move, and by noon the battlements were full, and by dusk the whole of Blue Star that could see the northern sky was looking at it. Low over the end of the world, beside the keeper's small white ring with its gold glow and the Elder's vast gray one with its soft white light at the center: nothing. A clear spring evening. The first stars.

And then, one after another, the way lamps come on along a street at dusk, ninety lights.

Not stars. Bigger. Brighter. Each one a long shape, lit from end to end, burning white and gold and the colors of their Houses, so that the northern sky over Blue Star was suddenly full of what looked like cathedrals, enormous, hanging in the dark in a great curved line round the keeper's field like a necklace. Kilometers long, some of them. Spired. Buttressed. Windowed, so that light poured out of them. They burned on purpose. Ichiro had said that. The quiet ones call them the Lamplit, because they burn on purpose.

Nobody on the north wall said anything.

Ninety ships.

The smallest is eleven times the size of the Candle. The largest is forty times.

Each of them is made of cores.

I am counting. I have not finished. Three million. Four million. More. In the walls. Burning.

Yuanshu. Every one of those lights is people.

Beside him, Lingyue stood with Xiaodeng asleep against her chest in a sling Fat Tan had made out of an old Ossuary banner, and her grandfather's sword on her back again, because Bai Lu had said three weeks and it had been three weeks, and she had picked it up that morning and said there and not put it down. The pale flame in her free hand, the size of a lamp, held. Not burning. She was looking up at the ninety lights in the north.

"They're pretty," she said. Flatly.

"Yes."

"I hate them," said Shen Lingyue.

On his other side, Tam had his face turned up and his eyes shut. He had come up the Sector Nine stairs at five with Xiaoyu and three hundred of the Candle's children behind him, and stood at the parapet, and had not said anything since the first light came on.

"Tam," said Yuanshu.

The boy did not open his eyes.

"I can hear them," said Tam. In Huaxian. He had been learning for nine months. "All of them. Four million. Five. Burning. In the walls." His voice was very steady, the way the Candle's children's voices were steady when something was worse than they could say. "They're not asking anything. They don't know they can."

He paused.

"Some of them are from Corraine," he said. "I know their voices."

The voice came at nine.

Not through the keeper's eye. Not through a recording that taught itself, the way the Candle's had, slow and patient, I am here. Through everything. Every radio on Blue Star. Every speaker. Every phone. In Huaxian, and in the Northern Union's language, and in Yamato's, and in the languages of the Southern Compact and the Atlantic Federation, perfectly, as if the speaker had learned them all in a day, which, Seed said afterward, he probably had, from listening to Blue Star's radio from the far edge of the Shallows while the fleet crossed.

A man's voice. Calm. Courteous. Absolute. The voice of someone who has never once in his life needed to raise it.

"People of Unlisted Orchard Four-Four-Seven-One. I am Dacian Severin. SSS. Paragon of the House of Severin. First of the Ladder. Fleet Marshal of the Lamplit Worlds."

On the north wall, three thousand people went still.

"By decree of the Assembly of the Federation, your world is listed. From tonight, you are an orchard of the Lamplit. You will be read, and marked, and placed upon the Ladder in your proper order, as every human world has been since the first stone fell."

A pause. Not for effect. The man did not need effects.

"Three things are required of you."

"First. Six days before the Reaper was stilled, a Lord-bright flare was recorded on your world. Then the Reaper's threads went quiet. The last thread it read before it stopped leads here. You possess the instrument by which this was done. You will surrender it to the Fleet."

"Second. Eight months ago, a Federation listener ship was boarded in your sky by an unknown intelligence, and its Listener-Captain was induced to unpress her cores. Since then, seven Houses have emptied their arrays. Eleven ships stand unfoldable in the yards at Anthos. The Ladder has been dishonored. You will surrender to the Fleet any person on your world who took part in the speaking to cores."

"Third. Your stones have been altered. They do not mark. They ask. This is heresy against the oldest law. Within ten days, your stones will be restored to their purpose, or they will be removed."

The voice paused again.

"And the ungraded one," said Dacian Severin. "The one the Listener-Captain called the boy who asks. Whose stone could not rate him. He will present himself to the Fleet, and be read. We would like to know what he is."

"You have ten days."

The radios went quiet.

Nobody on the north wall moved. Three thousand people, and the Candle's children, and the night watch with their helmets in their hands, and a woman with a baby in a sling and a sword on her back, standing on the battlements of a city on a world that had just been told it was an orchard.

Then Xiaodeng woke up, and made a sound.

Not a cry. A note. The one she had made in the Awakening Hall on the morning she was born, furious, here I am. Very small, on the north wall, under ninety burning cathedrals.

Lingyue put her hand on the baby's back without looking down.

"He didn't ask," she said.

"No."

"Iselde said they wouldn't." Her jaw was set. "He didn't even say please. He said required." She looked at the lights in the north. "He wants to read you. Like a stone. To see what you are." She almost laughed. "The last time a stone read you, it went black."

I am going to tell you what I can see.

Then you should decide what to say. Not me. You.

He sat down on the cold stone of the battlement in his old place with his back against the parapet and the staff across his knees, and closed his eyes, and let Seed show him.

The ninety ships are made of cores. The way the Candle was. Cells in the walls. Small dark crystal, the size of a person curled up. Pressed people in them. Burning.

Four million one hundred and eighty thousand. I have finished counting.

They are not burning low. They are burning the old way. Held under water and set on fire. Every fold.

I tried to ask one.

I asked you first. You were asleep. You said yes in your sleep. I am telling you now in case that does not count.

She did not hear me.

He opened his eyes.

The Candle's cores heard you.

Yes. They were awake. They had always been awake. They heard everything.

These are not awake.

There is something round every cell. A layer. Very thin. Something they have put round the crystal, since the Candle. Since the Assembly heard thirty-two thousand names read out and found out that cores could hear.

It does not stop them burning. It stops them hearing.

They have stopped up their ears.

Beside him, Tam had opened his eyes.

"I can hear them," said the boy.

"Seed can't."

"I know." Tam looked at the lights. "I asked the stone to make me someone who hears the ones in the walls. So nobody's ever in a wall again with nobody listening." He did not look away from the north. "It didn't say only the ones whose ears are open."

They answered at midnight. Not from the north wall. From the platform of the Awakening Hall, in front of the warm black stone, because Wen Jia said that if they were going to answer a man who wanted to restore the stones to marking, they should do it standing in front of one that asked.

Concordia had met by radio for three hours. Article Eleven: where one answer acts for all, all are asked. There were ten days. There was not time to ask everyone. So they asked the thing they could ask everyone fast, the thing Blue Star had already answered, seven months ago, from Concordia and the net of the dead and the paper ballots and Shiomi in the fog: Will we hide, or will we light the lamps?

They had said: light the lamps.

So he lit them.

"Fleet Marshal," said Zhou Yuanshu. Through Seed, through the keeper's eye, which the keeper had offered before it was asked, in the Federation's language, which has the grade in every sentence about a person. He took the grades out. Seed helped. "My name is Zhou Yuanshu. I'm the one you asked for. The stone couldn't rate me. I'm going to tell you first what we're going to do. That's how we do things here."

The fleet did not answer. It was listening. He could feel it listening, the way you feel ninety cathedrals full of people listening in the dark.

"We're not going to hide," he said. "We tried that once, with rings. We decided we don't want to be the kind of world that hides."

"We're not going to attack you. We have a rule. The dead may defend, never conquer. The living have it too."

"We're not going to surrender anyone. Nobody here did anything to your ship that its captain didn't choose. We asked her cores what they wanted. They said. She chose. That's all."

"We're not going to change our stones back. There are people in them now. They chose to be there. You'd have to ask them, and they'd say no."

He looked up at the roof of the Awakening Hall. Past it. At ninety burning lights in the north.

"And Fleet Marshal," he said. "You asked what I am. I'll tell you. I've never hidden it. I'm a necromancer. I raise the dead, if they say yes. I have a thing in my head that was a harvest sack once and learned to ask. That's all I am. That's the instrument. It's not a weapon. You can't have it, because it's not a thing. It's a question."

He paused.

"There are four million one hundred and eighty thousand people in the walls of your ships," said Zhou Yuanshu. "You've stopped up their ears so nobody can ask them anything."

"I'm telling you first: we're going to ask them anyway."

The fleet did not answer for a long time.

Then, through every radio on Blue Star, calm, courteous, absolute, the voice of a man who had never once in his life needed to raise it:

"Ten days."

And the radios went quiet.

He came down off the platform. Lingyue was at the foot of the steps with Xiaodeng asleep in the sling and the sword on her back and the lamp in her hand. His mother in her wheelchair. Xiaoyu with the marker. Tam. Gao Min. Kwan. Su Mingli with her arms folded. Old Seven at the side door, facing out.

"You told him we'd ask them," said Su Mingli. Flatly. "You told the First of the Ladder, with ninety ships in our sky, that we'd ask his engines what they want."

"Yes."

"He's going to make sure we can't."

"Yes."

"Can we?"

He did not answer. He looked at the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder and its runes pointing down. At Wen Jia, in the knot, holding. At the thread going up out of its crown, through the roof, into the net. Every stone in every orchard, tied through a knot in the deep water where nobody read anything any more.

Cut from the stone, Iselde had said, on the north wall, with a small black disc on a chain in her palm. Every Federation child is given one. On the morning of the reading. Cut from the stone that read them.

And the cells in the walls of the Candle. Small. Dark. Crystal. The size of a person curled up.

The same black.

...yes.

I see it.

I did not see it before. I would like to stop not seeing things.

I am going to need to look very carefully. And I am going to need Tam.

And Yuanshu. I am telling you first.

I do not know yet if it will work. And if it works, it will not only reach the walls.
