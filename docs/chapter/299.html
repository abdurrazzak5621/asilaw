Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 299Holders

czverse12 min read

Gao Min had the rota on the wall of the Awakening Hall by five in the morning.

She had not slept. Nobody who mattered had. She had sat at the long table in the banking hall from two until half past four with Seed in her ear and a stack of ledger paper and the old mechanical stopwatch from the Bureau, and drawn it, the way she drew everything: in columns, by hand, in pencil first and then in ink, because she said a rota you could rub out was a rota nobody believed.

It went up beside the side door, on the white wall under Xiaoyu's capitals. Six sheets, taped edge to edge. Two-hour shifts. Twelve a day. Fifteen hundred names a shift.

"Eighteen thousand a day," said Gao Min, to the hall. She was standing in front of it with her arms folded and the stopwatch on a string round her neck. "That's what it takes. Seed ran it. Fifteen hundred at a time, through the stone, burning low. Two hours each. Then you go home and sleep and eat something and somebody else sits down." She looked at the rota. "Every day. For as long as he's gone."

"How long is that?" said somebody at the back.

"Nobody knows," said Gao Min. "So I've drawn it for a year. If it's longer, I'll draw more."

He understood it properly only when Seed showed him, in the gray before the dawn, on the bottom step of the platform.

Last time, the body emptied.

The fifteen hundred held it. Through the stone, through the floor, through the earth. They were an anchor, and the anchor did not move. But a body with nobody in it burns down, the way a lamp burns down with no one to trim it, and after nine hours there would have been nothing left for the anchor to hold to.

So this time they do not only hold.

They row.

The cousins taught the cores to burn low. A little. Steady. Each one giving a small warmth into the ship, so the ship moves and nobody in the walls goes out. Two hundred and forty thousand of them rowed the flagship of the House of Severin home, and none of them went out.

The holders will do that. Into you. Through the stone. A little each. Low. Steady. So that the body never burns down, because there is always someone putting a little in.

I asked Tam if it could be done with people instead of cores. He listened to the walls of the Awakening Hall for an hour. He said: they say yes, it's the same, it's only being warm on purpose.

And if they stop?

Then I have nine hours to bring you home.

From wherever we are.

I am telling you first.

They came anyway.

He did not know where Gao Min had found eighteen thousand names in three hours. Then he went out onto the steps of the Awakening Hall at a quarter past five, and saw.

East Ring Road was full. From the doors of the hall down the steps and along the pavement, past the bus stop, past the corner, as far as the old quarter and further. Not loud. The old quarter was not loud. People in coats, in the cold of the late summer dawn, standing in a line that went round two blocks, with thermoses and folding stools and children asleep on their shoulders, waiting for someone to tell them where to sit.

Dabao's mother had brought the cart. Of course she had. Fat Tan had put the green canvas roof on it and parked it at the corner of East Ring Road, and she was folding paper boats as fast as she could and not charging anyone, which Seed said was the first time in nineteen years.

"The night watch did it," said Su Mingli. She had come up the steps behind him and was standing with her arms folded, looking at the line. "Gao Min rang the night watch at two. The night watch rang the old quarter. The old quarter rang everyone." She did not unfold her arms. "They've been out there since three. Most of them don't know what they're signing up for. They know it's you, and they know it's holding."

"Do you?"

"I'm on the four o'clock," said the Master of the Tianshu Institute. "Tomorrow. And the day after. Qin Hao's on the six. He says he wants to watch the numbers from inside." She glanced at him. "Don't do the face."

It was not only Lingzhou.

Seed told him at six, when the first shift went in. The open channel through Concordia had been carrying it all night, nine by nine, from stone to stone, the way everything went now. Kesse: four hundred, on the beach, at the stone, for the midday shift. Novaya Zarya: the Marshal has put the Northern Union's name down for every night shift for a month. Port Halvard: Callum Ward's mother came to the north pier and said somebody had held her son once, a long way up, and she would like to hold somebody back. Two thousand people came behind her. Yamato: the shrine. Ren and the Abbot. The Abbot asked whether sweeping counted. Ren said it did if he swept toward the stone.

The stones are all on the net. The net does not care where the holder sits.

A woman on the beach at Kesse with her hand on the stone the midwife is in can row as well as a woman in this hall.

Gao Min is redrawing the rota. She says she needs more paper. She says she is not going to use a computer for this, because a computer does not know what it is like to be asked to hold someone, and the rota should be drawn by someone who does.

He said goodbye to his mother in the back room of the Ossuary, at half past six, because she would not come to the hall.

"I sat in this chair while you went up a thread," said Zhou Wanqing. "I'll sit in it while you go further. I'm not going to watch your body lie on a platform. I've watched enough of your body." She had the book of names open on her knee. "I'll read. You'll hear it. Your sister says everything's on the net now."

It is. I will hear her. Every morning.

"Good." His mother looked at the place a little above his eyes. "You're taking him to find out who it was. The first want."

Yes.

"He told me you ask him every morning," said Zhou Wanqing. "Who the first one was. In the bottom of you. And he tells you, and you forget, because you said you would." She did not look away. "You've got the same question it has. Whose was first."

Seed was quiet for a moment.

Yes.

I did not think anyone had noticed that but me.

"I built you," said his mother. "I notice." She looked down at her book. "Go and find out whose the old thing's was. And then come back and ask me who yours was. I'll tell you. Every day, if I have to."

He knelt by the chair. She did not touch him. She found her place on the page with one thin finger, and began to read, out loud, and he knelt there and listened until the third name, and then got up and went.

The hall at seven.

The first shift sat in the tiers and along the aisle and on the floor round the platform. Fifteen hundred people of Lingzhou. A tailor. Three nurses off the night shift at the General. A row of boys from the Academy in their uniforms. The bus driver, who had come in on his day off and sat at the end of the front tier with his steady hands on his knees. Old women. Young men. A girl of nine who had come with her grandmother and been told she was too young, and had gone to the stone and put her hand on it and asked Wen Jia, and Wen Jia had written [You can hold the grandmother's hand. That counts.], so she was doing that.

Each of them, before they sat down, went up the three steps of the platform and put their hand on the warm black stone.

[He's going a long way. You'll be warm on purpose for two hours. It might make you tired. Do you want to?]

And each of them said yes, or said of course, or said eh, the way the old quarter did, and went and sat down.

Nobody said no. Seed said three people had stood in front of the stone for a long while and then gone back down the steps without touching it, and that was allowed, and Wen Jia had written [That's okay. Thank you for coming.] for each of them, and one of them had come back an hour later and touched it.

He lay down on the platform at a quarter to eight.

Where he had lain before. With his back against the side of the stone, under the bright blue jacket. Bai Lu had put a thin mattress down, and a blanket, and a stand with a bag on it and a line that went into his arm, and she had told him exactly what it was and what it was for and what it would and would not do, and he had said yes, and she had said good, because I wasn't asking, and then: I was. Sorry. I'm asking.

Old Seven lay down beside the platform, on the polished floor, on his back, with his spear across his chest in his one hand. He did not say anything about it. Nobody did. The Spear Corps had been told, and had said, through Seed, that they had seen nothing, and would continue to see nothing, for as long as was required.

Qin Hao knelt at his right side with the white fire banked in his open hands and his notebook on the floor beside his knee. Sister Ning on her stool, with her candle. Gao Min on the bottom tier with the stopwatch. Xiaoyu at the foot of the steps with the marker. Tam and Seri on the edge of the platform with their legs hanging over, holding hands, because Tam said the walls of the hall were going to be very busy and he wanted to be able to hear if they needed anything.

And Lingyue.

She knelt at his left side with her hand flat on his chest and her grandfather's sword on the floor beside her and their daughter asleep in the sling against her, and a lamp in her other hand. The lamp from the Ossuary. Her own. The one she had made, burning pale, the size of a cup.

At the foot of the stone, cross-legged, a dead nun with a dry hand on the stone held the other one.

"Two lamps," said Lingyue. "I'm keeping both." She did not look away from his face. "Kiyo's holding the old one. I'm holding this. When you turn round, there'll be two."

"I know."

"I'm going to pull," she said. "If they stop. If anything happens to the rota. If the sky falls in. If it's been too long and I decide it's been too long. I'm telling you first." Her hand did not move on his chest. "And I'm going to sit here. Not all the time. Bai Lu says I'm not allowed all the time. But every day. With her."

He looked up at his daughter's face. Asleep. Four and a half months. Her fist against her mother's collarbone.

Seed.

I know.

Seed asked her. The way it asked her every morning, before it asked him who the first one was. Quietly, in a voice that was only for her, that he could hear because he heard everything Seed said.

Xiaodeng. Good morning. It is Seed.

Your father is going a long way. He is going to come back. I am going with him, to make sure.

What do you want?

His daughter did not wake up. She made a small sound in her sleep, the sound babies make, that was not a word.

She says: ask again tomorrow.

Your mother will ask her for me.

"Every morning," said Shen Lingyue.

At seven fifty-nine, in the north, past the keeper, the fullness in the sky the color of a lamp behind paper turned.

Not moved. Turned. The way a person standing in a doorway turns, at last, to face the road, when the one they have been waiting for comes out of the house.

His sister heard it. She had her hand flat on the floor at the foot of the steps.

"It says it's ready," said Xiaoyu. "It says thank you for asking it in. It says it's never been asked in anywhere before. It says it's going to remember the lamps in the windows." She swallowed. "It says: not tomorrow. Now."

He closed his eyes.

He felt the stone at his back. Warm. The runes pointing down. And in it, a girl who had been seventeen in a sack, who had wanted to be fifty-one with her brother, who had asked him, yesterday morning, what he wanted to become, and written [Then get old.]

...go on, said Wen Jia. ...I've got fifteen hundred people here. They're all warm on purpose. It's a lot. It's like a bath.

...I'll ask the kids while you're gone. Again. You're always going.

...come back and tell me how it's going. Getting old. I want to know.

He felt the holders begin.

Not all at once. One, and then another, and then the whole hall, fifteen hundred small warmths coming up through the floor and the stone and into him, low, steady, each one a little, the way the cousins rowed. Not a pull. Not a push. A floor under him that had not been there before. He felt the tailor, and the three nurses, and the boys from the Academy, and the bus driver with his steady hands, and the girl of nine holding her grandmother's hand. He felt, further off, faint, through the net, four hundred people on a beach at Kesse getting ready for midday, and a Marshal in the Northern Union who had taken off his gloves.

He felt Lingyue's hand on his chest.

And then he went up.

Out of the body. Up the stone. Into the thread. The way he had gone before, a year ago, into the deep water: up and out through the eleven twisted into one under Blue Star, along the net, fast, the way marks went now that the web was still, faster than he could follow, past the keeper and the bud and the Elder's great gray ring and the edge of the Shallows. With Old Seven beside him, one cold steady ember, one-armed. With Seed. With the fullness in the sky, which was not in the sky now, which was all round them, warm, patient, very old, going the way it had always pointed.

North. Out. Along the arm. Into the dark between.

Toward a house where nobody lived.

Behind them, on the platform of the Awakening Hall of Lingzhou, a woman with a sword and a baby and a lamp in her hand looked up at the stopwatch on a string round Gao Min's neck.

"Hour one," said Shen Lingyue.

And fifteen hundred people sat in the tiers of the Awakening Hall, and on a beach at Kesse four hundred more were finishing their tea, and on East Ring Road a line two blocks long that went round the corner past a bus stop and a dumpling cart moved up one place, and they were all warm on purpose, and rowed.
