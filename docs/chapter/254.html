Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 254The Deep Water

czverse9 min read

There was no map.

Seed said it the moment they left the end of the arm, and then did not say anything else for a long time, and he understood why. He felt it the way you feel a room go dark around you: not a sudden thing, a slow one. Behind them, the Shallows. The keeper's field, the twelve gold dots of the other fields along the arm, the dead husks of pruned ones, the cold fire, the faint long curve of the arm itself going back toward a small blue world. Things Seed had seen through the keeper's eye and counted and named.

Ahead, nothing. Not dark. Dark was a thing; dark had a shape. This was the absence of anything Seed had a word for. The dark between arms, where no keeper's eye had ever looked, where the first ring had come from before it was a ring, where the trunk of the tree went back into and the Collector came out of, and where, at the far end of eleven fine threads, something was sitting.

I am counting.

It is the only thing I know how to do here. I am counting the distance along the thread. That is all I have.

One hour and two minutes.

Seven hours and fifty-eight to go.

The river of sparks went on along the thread.

He could feel it all round him. Twelve thousand embers and more, cold and dim and steady, in their rows of nine where they had rows, in their knots where they had knots, the Spear Corps close round him like a hand round a match, the Third Battalion in the middle behind its captain and its sergeant, the Watch of the Shelf at the back, slow, the color of deep water, as if the deep water were a thing they already knew.

Nobody spoke. Embers do not speak. But through the net, nine by nine, round and round, slower than ever this far from home, things came to him. Small things. Grid Nine had seen something off to the left that might have been a light and might not. A corporal of the First Legion wanted it known that this was further than the Black Tide, further than anybody in the First Legion had ever marched, and he would like it written down. The Watch said the deep water was not like the sea. The sea pushed back. This did not push. It just let you go further and further in, as if it did not care whether you came out.

Xiaoyu will write it, he sent back. All of it. When we're home.

At the second hour, the threads began to join.

He saw it the way Seed saw it: not with eyes. Their thread, the one they were on, the eleven twisted into one somewhere in the dark under Blue Star, ran on ahead straight and taut into nothing. And then, off to one side, very faint, another thread came in out of the dark from somewhere else and ran alongside it. And then another. From the other side. From above, if there was an above. Fine pale threads, each one coming in out of the deep water from somewhere Seed had no name for, and joining theirs, and running on with it toward the same place.

Ten. A hundred. More.

...they are from other stones.

Other orchards. Other worlds. Each one with its stones, reading what ripens, marking how bright, sending the mark up.

I can feel the marks going up them. Still. All of them. They have not gone quiet. Only ours has.

A thousand. Two thousand. I am counting. I cannot count them all.

By the third hour, the thread they were on was not a thread. It was a rope. A cable. Thousands of fine pale threads twisted together, from thousands of worlds, all running the same way through the dark, all humming with the small steady traffic of marks going up. Every child in every orchard who had ever put their hand on a stone and been told what they were. Every grade. Going up. Into the deep water. To the one who picks.

He did not want to think about how many.

Seed thought about it for him.

I am not going to tell you the number.

I am telling you that I counted it, and that I am not going to tell you, so that you know there is a number, and that I am keeping it for later.

It is very large.

At the fourth hour, they saw the hands.

Not near. Off along the cable, in the dark, where other threads came in. Things moving along them. Vast, slow, gray, hand over hand, the way the hand had come down the Port Halvard thread. Going out along a thread toward some world Seed did not have a name for, or coming back along one, toward the place where all the threads were going. A hand with its fingers curled, full. A hand with its fingers open, empty, going out.

They are its hands. All of them. It has thousands. It keeps them on the threads.

They go out to the bright places and pick and come back.

They cannot see us. We are not bright.

Stay dim.

He stayed dim.

He felt the door in his chest the whole time. Closed. Exactly where it was. He felt how easy it would be, here, in the dark, with nothing to see by, to open it a little. To be bright. To see.

He held on to the staff that was not there and to a small gray knot in his right hand that was, and stayed dim, and went on.

A hand went past them on the cable. Close. So close that the embers of the Spear Corps drew in round him without being told. Vast and gray and slow, going back along the cable toward the place everything was going, its fingers curled round something that shone between them, faintly, the way a lamp shines through someone's fingers when they carry it in from the yard at night.

Picked. From somewhere. Being carried home.

He did not look at it. He looked at it anyway.

Seed, he thought. What's in it?

I do not know.

People. Minds. From a world I do not know. They are burning low. The way the cousins burned.

I cannot ask them. If I ask, I have to go near. If I go near, it will see.

We came for one man.

I am sorry. I am writing it down. All of it. Every hand that goes by. Your sister can put it on the wall.

The hand went by, into the dark ahead of them, toward the place everything was going.

He did not say anything for a long time.

Five hours.

Three to go.

We are close.

He felt it before Seed said it. The cable thickened. The hum of the marks going up it grew louder, not a sound, a feeling, the way you feel a city before you see it, coming down a road at night. And the dark ahead was not empty any more.

Busy.

He had seen it before, from the end of the Port Halvard thread, from the edge of the lantern's light: a place where the dark was full, the way a forest at night is full. He had thought he had seen all of it then. He had seen a corner.

It was everywhere.

Ahead, above, below, on every side, the busy dark. Something enormous and patient and complicated, not a shape, a place, sitting in the deep water at the center of a web of ten thousand cables like this one, each cable twisted from ten thousand threads, each thread running out into the dark to a stone on a world in an orchard. And on the cables, everywhere, the hands. Thousands. Most of them still. Waiting. Holding on. The way a spider waits at the center of a web, with a foot on every line.

At the center of it, where all the cables came together, a knot.

Not small. The size of the keeper's field. The size of the arm, maybe; he could not tell, there was nothing to measure it by. A vast slow tangle where every thread from every stone in every orchard in the deep water ran in and was read. He could feel it reading. The marks coming up the threads and going into the knot and being read, glanced at, one after another, a billion a second, the way Gao Min ran her finger down a column of grades.

That was its face. The place where it read.

And held up in front of it, very close, in one vast gray hand, so close that the light of him lit the knot from outside like a lamp held up to a page:

The lantern.

Callum Ward was still burning.

White, gold, every color. Arms out. The shape of a man who had been holding something heavy over his head and had never put it down. He was the only light in the deep water. The only light for as far as the deep water went. Everything else in all that vast busy dark was gray and slow and cold. And the thing at the center of the web had stopped all its hands, stopped walking, stopped everything, and drawn him in, and was holding him up to its face.

Looking.

It has been looking at him for six days.

Since the hand came back from Port Halvard with an empty palm.

I do not know what it sees.

He stopped on the cable, at the edge of the busy dark, with twelve thousand embers and sixty more stopping round him, and the small gray knot in his right hand, and looked at the burning man held up in front of the face of the oldest thing anyone had ever made.

Callum, he thought. It's me. It's tomorrow.

At the bottom of the well, under the roar, a voice.

...yeah.

I'm here. Close. I've got it. The mark. My sister wrote it. A small thing with all its legs out tied it.

...I can feel you. You're very close. You're... you're in the dark right behind me.

Yes.

...it's looking at me. It's right up close. It's looking at me like... The voice stopped. ...like it doesn't understand what I am. Like it's never seen one. Like I'm the first one it's ever looked at instead of looked by.

You are.

The fire roared.

...is Mum still holding?

Yes.

...then come and give it me, said Callum Ward. ...I've got something to tell it.

Five hours and nine minutes.

If you go to him, you go into the light.

You will be close enough for it to see you. In his light. Right in front of its face.

You will have to stay dim inside the brightest thing in the deep water.

I know.

I have run it.

I am not going to tell you the number.

I know.

He says...

Seed stopped. Then went on.

He says: the line goes with you.

He says: not to the light. To the hand. The hand holding the lantern. It cannot see us. It cannot feel us. It is a glove.

He says: when the boy gives the man the mark, and the man holds it up, every one of us is going to be standing on the fingers of that hand.

He says: and when it is time, we will open it.

Ahead of him on the cable, cold and steady and dim, an ember that he would have known among a million turned, just a little, the way a captain turns at the head of a column to see that it is still behind him.

Beside it, a short square ember with the shape of a pick on its shoulder.

Huo says: sir. That's not a question either.

He went into the light.
