Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 199Cold

czverse8 min read

The message came on the thirty-first night after Day 398, and it was the shortest one Hush had ever sent.

It took four seconds.

Seed read it out through the speaker in the wall to the old banking hall at three in the morning, to everyone who was there, which was everyone: Yuanshu on the vault steps, and Xiaoyu on the stepladder with her voice back, mostly, a little rough, and Kwan and Fat Tan and Sister Ning and Lingyue on the floor with her grandfather's sword across her knees, and Qin Hao with his arms still in bandages, and the Mo twins, and Bai Lu, and Hu Dabao with his left arm out of its sling at last, and Ma Lianhua, dead, by the bronze door beside Old Seven, and Adjutant Xu on his chair. And on the floor under the long white wall, around the unlit lamps, forty strangers who had come to sit in the dark because they could not sleep.

Day 429.

The fires are out.

It is very cold.

We are all here.

— H.

Nobody moved.

Then Xiaoyu wrote it on the wall. All of it. Five lines. In her careful capitals, at the end of the INSIDE column, round the corner on the second wall. Under it, small, the seconds. 4. She did not write anything else. She climbed down the stepladder and sat on the bottom step of it and put her face in her hands, and Lingyue got up off the floor and went and sat beside her, and did not say anything.

There is no queen of hearts.

There is no tell the captain.

I think it was too cold to say them.

I think it said only what it had to.

The Elder came down at four, and Yuanshu asked the keeper.

He told it first what he would do with the answer. The way it had learned. The way they had all learned. If it said yes, they would go at dawn, as many as it could carry, and it would carry them down again after, whatever happened, the way it had promised on the gold road. If it said no, they would not go, and he would not ask it twice, and the names would go on the wall when Hush stopped sending messages. If it said it did not know, they would wait.

"It's cold," he said. "You said: ask again when it's cold. I'm asking."

The gold thread through the Elder was very still.

Then it brightened. Slowly. The way a coal brightens when someone breathes on it. All the way up, from the Elder's pressed hands to the crown of her head and on, out of the banking hall, north, out over the city and the wall and the Wastes, to the end of the world, where a small white ring hung low in the dark.

...I CAN FEEL IT.

...THE COLD FIRE. THIRTY-NINE OF YOUR RAFT-DAYS. NOTHING PULLING. NOTHING SCREAMING. NOTHING SIGHING.

...JUST COLD.

...AND SOMETHING SMALL, IN THE MIDDLE OF IT. HOLDING ON.

The Elder's silver eyes were open. She was looking at nothing.

...I AM STILL AFRAID.

...I WILL BE AFRAID THE WHOLE WAY. I WILL BE AFRAID OF WHAT IS PRUNING THE ARM. I WILL BE AFRAID OF FIFTY THOUSAND.

...I THINK THAT IS WHAT BRAVE IS. I THINK BRAVE IS BEING AFRAID AND CARRYING IT ANYWAY.

...YOU TAUGHT ME THAT TOO. YOU DID NOT KNOW YOU WERE TEACHING IT.

...YES.

...I AM BRAVE ENOUGH.

...DAWN.

He asked the living after that.

One at a time. On the floor of the old banking hall, in front of the long white wall, at four in the morning, with forty strangers watching. He crouched in front of each of them so their faces were level. He told each one what they were saying yes to. Thirty-nine raft-days into the dark. A dead Hunger. Something pruning the arm toward it, that might arrive when they did. People from the deep water who were coming too, and would not be there for a year. Fifty thousand dead who had said yes. A keeper who was afraid.

You might not come back. If you say no, you stay here and hold the city, and nobody thinks less of you. Nobody asks you twice.

Lingyue said yes before he had finished. He made her let him finish. Then she said it again.

Qin Hao held up his bandaged arms. "Ordinary fire," he said. "Until. I wrote it down." He said yes.

Mo Shi said yes. Mo Xing held out his right hand, empty, and then his left, with a pebble in it, and then his right again, with the pebble in it, faster than anyone in the room could follow, and said yes.

Bai Lu said yes, and picked up her medical bag, which was still too big for her, and which nobody was still allowed to carry.

Hu Dabao said yes. He did not say anything else. He picked up his shield with both arms.

Kwan said yes, and then said, "Forty-one pages. I've got a new list."

The Elder said yes. She did not need to. She was the keeper's voice; she went where it went. She said it anyway, because he had asked.

He came to his sister last.

She was sitting on the bottom step of the stepladder with Lingyue beside her and her face out of her hands now, red-eyed, with the marker in her fist. She looked at him crouching in front of her. Sixteen. Seventeen in a month. Her voice still a little rough from calling eleven hundred streets in one night.

"Xiaoyu," he said. "I'm going to ask you."

"I know."

"I'm going to tell you first what I'll do with the answer."

"I know." She did not look away. "You'll take me, if I say yes. Because it's mine. Because I'm not in anyone's window. Because Lingyue sent the Kirovsk kids down the mountain and they were twelve and nine and six, and I'm sixteen and I called a whole city in the dark, and you think that's different." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "You'll take me. And you'll hate it the whole way."

"Yes."

"And if I say no, you'll be glad, and you won't say so, and I'll know anyway."

"Yes."

Zhou Xiaoyu looked at her brother for a long time.

Then she looked past him. At the long white wall. At all of it. The first column from the cemetery, in her earliest, shakiest capitals. THE WALL. THE THROAT. CAME HOME. FROM OUT THERE. INSIDE, going round the corner. SHIOMI, round the next. GRID NINE. WESTERN LEGION. The column with no heading and Ifeoma Adeyemi in the middle. And at the very top, above everything, where she had needed the tall ladder from the church on East Market Road: HUNDRED-ARROW LIN. THE HUNDRED-AND-FIRST. SHE GOT IT.

"No," she said.

He did not say anything.

"Not because I'm scared. I'm scared. That's not why." She held up the marker. "Someone has to write their names when they come home. Hush's. The sergeant's. Liang Fu's. All sixty-one. The Sixth. Two hundred and eighty-one million, if I have to get more walls." Her voice cracked and steadied. "Someone has to be here with the marker when you come down the road. Standing at the bottom. So you know which shore is yours."

Light the lamp on the shore.

"Yes," said Xiaoyu. "That."

He did not say he was glad. She was right. She knew anyway.

At five, an hour before dawn, Old Seven read the wall.

He did it every month. He had done it a few nights ago. He did it again now, from the beginning, all of it, while the people in the old banking hall sat very still and let him. It took most of the hour. When he reached the end of the INSIDE column, round the corner, the five short lines from Day 429, he stood in front of them for a long time with his spear grounded.

Then he turned around.

He walked across the marble floor between the unlit lamps and the sitting strangers to the vault steps, and stood in front of Yuanshu, and did not say anything.

He is not asking.

He says he would like to say something. Out loud. In front of the wall. So that it is written down that he said it.

"Go on," said Yuanshu.

He says: when he was alive, on the wall, in the Black Tide, he had a company. A hundred and twelve men. By morning he had nine. He did not go back for the rest. There was no one to go back for.

He says: when he was dead, in the ground, in the eastern field, for thirty-one years, nobody came back for him.

He says: and then a boy came, at three in the morning, without a permit, and asked.

He says: a captain does not leave his men.

He says: Huo is not his man. Huo was Lu Xuanming's. The sixty-one are Lu Xuanming's. They walked off this world on a raft because they were asked, and they are in a cold fire because they said yes to standing guard over a child.

He says: they are his men now. All of them. He decided. He did not ask them. He will ask them when he gets there, and they can say no, and he will carry them home anyway.

He says: that is not Article Eleven.

He says: that is the only rule he ever had.

Nobody in the banking hall said anything.

Old Seven put the butt of his spear down on the marble floor. Once. Very softly. Then he turned and walked back to the bronze door, and stood beside it, facing out, the way he always stood.

He says: dawn.

At half past five, Xiaoyu got up off the bottom step of the stepladder and went to the floor under the long white wall, where forty strangers sat around a field of unlit lamps.

She took a box of matches out of her pocket. The one the old man in the cardigan had left there, the first night.

She struck one.

She did not light all of them. She lit one. A child's nightlight shaped like a rabbit, with a candle inside it, the smallest lamp on the floor. It flickered, and caught, and burned, small and yellow, at the foot of the wall of names.

Then she gave the matches to the woman sitting nearest, and went back to the stepladder.

The woman lit the next one, and passed the box on.

By the time the sky in the high windows of the banking hall was going gray, every lamp on the floor of the Ossuary was burning.
