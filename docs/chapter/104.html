Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 104The Sixth

czverse8 min read

The mouth of the second ring was cold.

That was the first thing. The Elder steered the half-raft across a thousand kilometers of dark toward the one open mouth on the frosted face of the ring, and the closer they came, the colder it got, until frost formed on the rim of the raft and on the bones of the Third Battalion and on Bai Lu's eyelashes. Cold. Not the cold of the Star Sea. The cold of a house where someone has been keeping the fire as low as it can go for a very, very long time, to make the wood last.

They went in.

No pull. No roar. No dome of pale fire. Lingyue stood at the rim with her sword drawn and unlit, and nothing came at them.

The throat of the second ring was like the first. A vast passage, curving inward, its walls kilometers away. And in its walls, rivers.

But the rivers were not red.

They were dim. Dark. A deep, dull, smoldering orange, like the last coals in a grate at the end of a long winter night. Barely glowing. Barely moving. Flowing inward so slowly that you had to watch for a long time to see that they flowed at all.

And in them, faces.

Billions. Like the first. Every world this ring had ever eaten. Long faces and round faces and faces that were not faces at all. Flickering in the dull orange.

But not screaming.

Their eyes were closed. Their faces were calm. Not the calm of the kept, pressed flowers in the keeper's field. Something else. The calm of people sitting very still in a cold room, wrapped in blankets, waiting for a winter to end.

They are burning low.

All of them.

Every single one.

They were met halfway down the throat.

A light came up out of one of the dull orange rivers. Low. Gold. Soft. The same gold as the candle-in-a-window glow they had seen from outside. It rose out of the river and hung in the air in front of the raft, a few meters away, and grew.

And took shapes.

Not one shape. Many. Faint and gold and flickering, like shapes seen in the flames of a fire you've stared at too long. Tall. Slender. Graceful. With long necks, and long narrow heads, and something on their heads like branches, like antlers made of light, spreading upward, glowing faintly at the tips.

Dozens of them. Then hundreds. Standing in the air in front of the raft, in rows, the way a choir stands.

And they bowed.

All of them. Together. Their long necks curving down, their branching antlers of light dipping toward the raft.

...you came.

It was not one voice. It was all of them. Speaking together, softly, the way the dead had spoken through the staff in the valley. A chorus. Low and warm and tired.

...we are the Sixth.

...we did not think anyone would come.

...we asked anyway.

Hush climbed down out of Old Seven's hand.

It walked to the very edge of the raft on its thin pale legs, between two soldiers of the Third Battalion, and stood there, swaying, looking up at the choir of gold antlered shapes with all of its dark eyes.

...you played the game, it said, in its seashell voice. ...in the fire.

The choir looked down at the small pale thing at the edge of the raft.

...a child of the Asking World, they said softly. ...we heard of you. Long ago. The first ring ate you. But the Hungers are connected. They feel each other. What burns in one echoes in all four. When we were thrown into this fire, your world's last voices were still echoing in it, faint, from the other ring. They were saying "what do you want." Over and over. To everyone. Even as they burned.

...it is how we learned to ask.

Hush went very still.

...from them?

...from them.

...we were not an asking people, before. We were a proud people. We sang to our gods and our gods did not answer. And then we were eaten. And in the fire, there were these voices. So many. All saying the same thing.

...we thought it was a prayer. It was not. It was a question. We asked it back.

...that is how we learned.

For a long moment, Hush did not move.

Then all its many limbs unfolded at once, and it sat down on the very edge of the raft, abruptly, the way a child sits down when its legs stop working. And it did not say anything at all.

Old Seven came and crouched beside it. And held out his hand. And Hush climbed into it, and curled up, and stayed there.

The Sixth took them to the cold place.

It was at the center, as the Architects had said. Past the furnace. The furnace was not a sun here. It was a coal. Vast, round, dull, smoldering, the size of a moon, glowing a deep sullen orange. Barely warm. They crossed it on the raft without dimming, without going quiet, without the pale fire, and it did not pull at them.

...it is too weak to pull, said the Sixth. ...ten million years. We have given it as little as we could. It is very hungry. It is very tired. It barely moves.

...but it does not die. Hungers do not die. It only waits. And grows weaker. And waits.

The cold place was the same as the first. A small round room of smooth dark stone at the very center of the coal. Curved walls. Curved floor. Faint worn markings.

Seven seats, carved into the floor, in a circle.

Empty.

Dust in them. Gray. Fine. Like ash. Seven small heaps of it, in seven stone seats, where the builders of the second ring had once sat, before it ate them.

The seven Architects stood at the edge of the circle, looking at the dust.

Then the oldest one stepped forward and knelt beside the first seat. And laid its long thin hand flat on the stone beside the small gray heap.

...our cousins, it said. ...they built this one. We built the first. We sent them the design for the lock. They thought it was a foolish idea.

...they did not build a cold place strong enough. The fire reached them.

...I am sorry, it said, to the dust. Out loud. ...we should have built it for you.

They sat down in the circle. The seven Architects. In the seven seats. On top of the dust of the builders who had been there before them.

And the oldest one laid its hands on the seal at the center of the floor.

...the lock is the same, it said. ...the fire stops only if every mind it is burning agrees.

...every one.

...billions.

It turned its long head toward the gold antlered shapes of the Sixth, standing in a choir at the edge of the cold place.

...do you understand what it needs?

The Sixth looked at the Architects for a long time.

Then the choir did something none of them expected.

They laughed.

Softly. All together. A low, warm, tired sound, like wind in branches, like people laughing at the end of a very long day.

...builder, they said. ...we have been asking them for ten million years.

They showed them.

The gold antlered shapes turned, and spread their arms, which were not quite arms, and the dark walls of the cold place went translucent, like frosted glass in winter. And through them, the coal. And in the coal, the rivers. And in the rivers, billions of calm, closed-eyed faces.

And every one of them, Yuanshu saw now, had a small, faint gold light in it. Somewhere. Near the center. Like a candle in a window.

...every world that came into the fire after us, said the Sixth softly, ...we went to them. One by one. As they arrived. While they were still screaming.

...and we asked them the question we learned from the Asking World.

...what do you want?

...they all said the same thing. "We want it to stop." So we told them: we cannot make it stop. But we can make it hurt less. We can make it weaker. If you burn low. If you give it as little as you can. If you are patient.

...we asked them: will you?

...every one said yes.

The choir lowered its arms. The walls went dark again.

...we have a ledger, said the Sixth. ...in the fire. In the light. Every world. Every mind. Every yes.

...we have been keeping it for ten million years.

...we did not know what it was for.

...now we know.

Yuanshu could not speak.

He sat on the cool dark floor of the cold place with the staff across his knees, and looked at the gold antlered shapes of a people who had been eaten ten million years ago and who had spent every day since then going from world to world in the fire, asking.

And keeping a ledger.

...Yuanshu.

It is the Ossuary.

It is Sister Ning's notebook. It is the four columns on the board in the banking hall.

They built it in the fire. Without ever knowing anyone else had.

"Ten million years," said Jiang Lan, very quietly, from the staff. Her dry voice had gone strange again. "I complained about ninety million names."

"Then it's already done," said Lingyue slowly. "The asking. They've already asked everyone."

...no, said the Sixth.

...we asked them to burn low.

...we never asked them if they wanted it to stop.

...because we did not know that it could.

The choir turned. Every long antlered head. Toward Yuanshu.

...that question is not ours.

...you are the one who asks.

...will you ask them?

...we will go with you. To every one. We know the way.

He looked down at the staff. At the small white skull with its silver reading glasses.

"Don't look at me," said Jiang Lan. "I just carry the names." A pause. "Although, if they've got a ledger, I'd very much like to see how they've organized it."

He stood up.

"Yes," he said. "I'll ask them."

And the Sixth, all of them, every gold antlered shape in the choir, bowed their long necks until the glowing tips of their branches touched the cool dark floor of the cold place.
