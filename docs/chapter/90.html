Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 90The Door Left Ajar

czverse10 min read

The keeper came down to Sector Nine at dawn, because he asked it to.

Not to the Wastes. Not to Grid Nine. Not to a whirlpool of threads at the bottom of the world. To the wall. To the battlements of the Academy section, forty meters of old gray patched stone, where a boy had once sat every night looking north, and a girl with a sword had told him he was in her spot.

It came down out of the paling sky like a falling star that never fell. One thread. Gold. Then another. Then hundreds, winding together as they came, the way the stalk had wound together, until a column of soft gold light stood on the battlements of Sector Nine, three meters wide, rising straight up into the sky, north, toward a small white ring.

It did not touch anyone. It did not go toward anyone.

It waited.

The whole city had come.

This time nobody came quietly, and nobody brought candles. There was no point being quiet anymore. The thing that was coming had already counted them. Eight billion, one hundred and forty million. Dense. Bright. Highly organized. So Lingzhou had come to the wall the way it went to a festival. Shouting. Waving. Crowding every battlement and every rooftop and every window for a kilometer in either direction. Children on shoulders. Old people on folding stools. Vendors with carts of pork buns, doing extraordinary business.

Dabao's mother had the best spot, at the foot of the stairs, and was charging double.

And on the battlements themselves, in a long line beside the column of gold light, the dead stood their watch.

Nineteen thousand of them. Shoulder to shoulder. All along the northern wall of Lingzhou, sixty kilometers of it, from end to end. Gray bones in gray rags. A soldier beside a grandmother beside a man in a postman's jacket beside a woman in a gray wedding dress. Facing north.

Holding the line. So the living would have somewhere to come back to.

He said goodbye to everyone. It took two hours.

Fat Tan, enormous in his padded coat, who did not say anything at all, and pressed a ledger into his hands. A new one. Blank. Ruled in pencil. Write everything down, said Fat Tan finally, gruffly. Every name. Every place. Every price. Nobody can ever cheat you if you write it down.

Lu Zhengtian, in a suit he had not slept in for once, who shook his hand very formally and then did not let go of it, and stood there holding it, looking at him over his silver-rimmed glasses, and said, Your mother would kill me if I let anything happen to you, and then, She still might, and then nothing else.

Doctor Iris Kwan, with a bag of instruments she had built in eleven days that nobody understood, including her, and a list of things she wanted measured in the Star Sea that was forty pages long.

Yan Shuang, with Ama, who did not say goodbye at all. She just looked at him with her weathered face, and nodded, once, and put an unlit cigarette in his coat pocket. For when you get there, she said. Wherever there is. Light it and think of me.

General Qin Zhenyue, eighty-two now, who said nothing to Yuanshu at all, and stood in front of his grandson for a long time with his one hand on the boy's shoulder, and then turned and walked back down the stairs without looking back.

And Ifeoma Adeyemi, who had come four thousand kilometers in her wheelchair, and sat at the foot of the column of gold light with her blanket over her knees, and did not say goodbye either. She only took his hand in both of her thin dry ones, and held it, and said: Where you fight is what burns. Remember. Take it somewhere there is nothing of ours.

Gu Hanshan was not on the wall.

He was in a teahouse at the end of a narrow lane in the old quarter, sitting on a cushion beside a bed, with a candle in front of him and his eyes closed, the way he had sat every night for forty-four nights. Sister Ning had not let him leave his shift.

But there was a flask on the battlements, at the foot of the gold column. Steel. Dented. With a cork stopper.

Taped to the side of it, a note, in a neat old-fashioned hand.

Yuanshu. Jasmine. It will stay warm a long time. I know a trick. Please believe me this time. — G.

He unscrewed the cork. Steam rose. Jasmine.

He put the flask in his pack, next to the green one with the white tape.

Su Mingli was the last.

She stood at the end of the battlements, alone, in her long gray coat, looking north. She did not turn around when he came up beside her.

They stood there together, looking at the small white ring on the horizon.

"I won't say anything wise," she said finally. "I don't have anything wise. I never did. I just stood in front of things." She was silent for a moment. "You stand in front of things now. You don't need me to do it for you."

"I'll always need you to do it for me."

"No," said Su Mingli. "You won't. That's what it means when someone raises you properly. They stop being needed." She looked at him at last. Her eyes were very bright. "Your mother told me that. Under the old telescope. Seven years ago. She said: Mingli, if I do this right, one day he won't need either of us." Her mouth moved. "I didn't believe her."

She held out her hand.

He shook it. Like an equal.

Then Su Mingli pulled him forward by the hand, and put her arms around him, hard, for one second, on the wall at Sector Nine, in front of the whole city. Then let go, and stepped back, and buttoned her coat to the throat, and turned to face north, and did not look at him again.

"Go," she said. "That's an order from a King."

His mother and sister were at the foot of the gold column.

Wanqing in her wheelchair, with the blanket over her knees and her hair pinned up under her field cap. Xiaoyu standing behind her, with both hands on the handles, in his old hoodie, with the sleeves pulled down over her hands.

Neither of them said goodbye.

Xiaoyu took one hand off the wheelchair and held out her fist. He held out his hand, palm up, and she dropped something into it.

A key. Small. Brass. On a loop of red string.

"The flat," she said. "I changed the locks. While you were in the Wastes. Somebody kept losing his." Her scowl was not holding at all. "So now you have to come back. Because otherwise you can't get in. And I'm not answering the door. I'll pretend I'm not home."

He put the key around his neck.

His mother did not say anything. She just reached up, from the wheelchair, and took his face in both her thin hands, the way she had in an endless field above the sky. And looked at him.

And then, without seeming to know she was doing it, she hummed.

Four notes. Up and down.

Behind his eyes, Seed hummed them back.

They went up together.

Yuanshu first, with the white staff in his hand and two flasks in his pack and a brass key on a red string around his neck. Then Shen Lingyue, with her sword across her back, which she would light with pale fire in the dark. Then Qin Hao, with his father's colonel's star pinned over his heart. Then the Mo twins, side by side, elbows touching, with one pack between them. Then Bai Lu, with her medical bag, which was still too big for her, which nobody was still allowed to carry. Then Sergeant Vera Sokolova, in white winter fatigues, with Doctor Kwan's forty-page list in her coat.

Then the dead.

Old Seven, with two arms. Iron Wall Zhao, broad as a doorway, with his great empty shield of rusted iron. Hundred-Arrow Lin, a drift of ash and dark air with an iron arrowhead for a heart. Sergeant Huo, with one arm, at the front of seventy-one soldiers of the Third Battalion, in rows.

And at the very edge of the column, as they stepped in, a tall pale shape of soft white light, long hands pressed together, silver eyes half closed. The Elder. Who had asked to come. Who knew the way.

They stepped into the gold.

And the gold carried them up.

He looked down once.

He let himself. Nothing up here was listening anymore.

The wall of Lingzhou dropped away beneath him. Sixty kilometers of gray stone, curving along the southern edge of the black glass of the Wastes. Lined, from end to end, with nineteen thousand gray shapes standing their watch. And behind them, the city. Towers and rooftops and the silver curve of the river, packed inside the old wall. And on the battlements of Sector Nine, at the foot of where the column had been, a tiny figure in a long gray coat, standing very straight, facing north. And beside her, a smaller figure in a wheelchair. And a smaller one still, behind it, in a too-big hoodie, waving with both arms over her head, the way someone very small and very far away waves at an airplane that might never see them.

He saw them.

Then the gold closed over him, and he rose.

Through the lens. Small and white now, a door left ajar.

Into the field. Patched gold and dark, with empty columns standing like doorways. Fifty-two tall slender beings in a white column at the center, holding hands, watching them pass. A thousand others, from Port Abiri and the old fields, who had chosen to stay, lifting their heads.

And the keeper. All around them. Close. Vast. Watching. Not IDENTIFY. Not WHERE.

...GOOD LUCK.

A pause. Uncertain. Trying out a word it had never used.

...COME BACK.

"We'll try," said Yuanshu.

...THAT IS NOT YES.

"No," said Yuanshu. "It's the honest answer."

The keeper was silent for a moment.

...I AM LEARNING THE DIFFERENCE.

The Elder led them across the field.

Past the center. Past the white column. Out the other side, where the rows thinned, and the gold columns grew fewer and further apart, and then stopped. And the pale warm ground ran on, empty, toward a horizon that was not a horizon.

An edge.

The field simply ended. Like the edge of a table. Like the edge of a cliff. And beyond it, there was nothing.

The Elder stopped at the edge, and turned, and looked at them with her silver eyes.

"This is the far door."

"The keeper has used it ten thousand times. Going out. To find fields."

"It has never once held it open for anyone."

"Beyond it is the Sea."

Yuanshu walked to the edge.

He stood on the last of the pale warm ground with the white staff in his hand, and the living and the dead behind him, and looked out.

It was dark.

Not the dark of night. Not the dark of a room with the lights off. A dark so deep and so vast and so complete that his eyes did not know what to do with it. It went on forever. In every direction. Up and down and out, without end, without edge, without anything to measure it against.

And in it, scattered across it like islands in an ocean at night, there were lights.

Very far apart. Unimaginably far. Small and warm and soft, each one alone in the dark. Fields. Worlds. Some gold. Some blue. Some a faint green, like the inside of a wave. Some flickering. Some steady. Billions of kilometers apart from each other, with nothing in between but the dark.

And drifting between them, in the black, very far away, he could see other things.

Gray. Dead. Husks. Small cold worlds with no light on them at all, turning slowly in the dark like the shells of cicadas left on a tree.

And beyond them, very far, so far that he could only see them because Seed showed him where to look: four rings. Gray. Cracked. Clouded. Turning.

One of them, the nearest, was much bigger than it had looked from the wall.

And it was still coming.

The Star Sea.

Yuanshu.

It is so big.

Behind him, very softly, from the small white skull at the top of the staff, a dry, brisk, slightly impatient voice said:

"Well. Nobody ever said arithmetic was easy."

He took the unlit cigarette out of his coat pocket. Yan Shuang's. He looked at it. He didn't smoke. He put it back.

He took the green flask out of his pack and unscrewed the lid with its white tape. Steam rose. Jasmine. Still warm.

He drank. Then he passed it back.

Lingyue drank. Then Qin Hao. Then the twins, one sip each, elbows touching. Then Bai Lu. Then Sokolova, who looked at it before she drank, and then passed it back to him without a word, and he saw that her ice-gray eyes were wet.

He put the lid back on. He put it back in his pack, beside Gu's.

Then Zhou Yuanshu, nineteen years old, Necromancer, from a city on the edge of the Northern Wastes, with a brass key on a red string around his neck and a schoolteacher in his hand and a voice behind his eyes that hummed his mother's song, stepped off the edge of the field.

Into the Star Sea.

And the living and the dead stepped off after him.
