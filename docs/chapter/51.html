Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 51The Crater

czverse9 min read

There were four hundred and twelve steps from the harbor to the Council Hall, cut into the black rock of the volcano, and every delegation in the world had to climb them on foot.

That was the first rule of Concordia, and the oldest. No cars. No lifts. No flying, not even for a King. Whoever you were, whatever you had come to argue, you walked up the side of a dead mountain in the wind with everyone else, and arrived at the top out of breath, and sat down at the round table a little humbler than you'd left the boat.

Yuanshu climbed them with Old Seven behind him and the sea falling away below.

The steps went up in long zigzags across the face of the cone, with a rope rail on the outer side and nothing beyond it but black cliff and white surf and wheeling birds. The rock was rough and warm under his hand, faintly warm, even now, ten thousand years after the fire went out. Halfway up, the cloud came down around them and the world shrank to the next ten steps and the sound of the wind.

And all the way up, he could feel it.

The eye.

It is directly above us. Above the cloud.

It is not doing anything. It is not sending threads. It is not pulsing.

It is just looking.

He came up through the top of the cloud into sunlight, and stopped.

The crater opened in front of him like a great stone bowl. Perhaps three hundred meters across. Its walls sloped down in black terraces furred with green moss and small tough flowers, and at the bottom, on a floor of smooth dark stone, stood the Council Hall.

It wasn't a hall. It had no walls and no roof. It was a ring of white stone benches, tier above tier, cut into the floor of the crater, and in the center of the ring, on a raised black platform, a round table of polished black volcanic glass, ten meters across, with five high-backed chairs around it. Behind each chair stood a tall stone post, and on each post hung a flag, snapping in the wind.

The crimson of Huaxia. The white of the Northern Union. The blue and silver of the Atlantic Federation. The plain white circle on white of Yamato. The green and gold of the Southern Compact.

And a sixth chair. Set slightly apart from the rest. With a small placard on it.

THE SUBJECT.

Above it all, high in the pale morning sky, small and round and perfectly still, hung the ring.

Everyone could see it.

That was the strangest thing. In the north, the great lens had been invisible to ordinary eyes for thirty-one years; only Kings and instruments could find it. But this one hung over the crater as plain as a moon, and everyone who came up over the rim saw it, and stopped, and stared.

The press, three hundred of them, on the upper terraces behind a rope, had turned every camera they had straight up. The delegations, arriving one by one up the steps, stopped at the rim with their hands shading their eyes. Council staff in gray uniforms were hurrying between groups, talking into radios.

"Atmospheric," said a man near Yuanshu, very loudly, to nobody. "An ice halo. They get them at altitude."

Nobody answered him.

It is not ice.

It is made of the same law as the lens in the north, and the transmitter, and the threads.

And it is roughly three thousand meters directly above that round table.

Catherine Moore met them at the bottom of the terraces with a clipboard and a face like a closed door.

"The Chair met at dawn," she said. "Emergency session. Twenty minutes." She led them across the dark stone floor toward the ring of benches, talking fast and low. "The Atlantic Federation wants the hearing postponed until the 'phenomenon' is explained. The Northern Union wants it to go ahead immediately. Yamato said nothing. The Southern Compact asked whether anyone in the room knew what it was." She glanced at Yuanshu. "I said I didn't. Was I lying?"

"No," said Yuanshu. "I don't know what it is either. I know what it's made of."

"Is it dangerous?"

I do not know.

"I don't know," said Yuanshu.

Catherine Moore looked up at the ring for a long second. Then she looked down at her clipboard.

"The Chair decided," she said. "The hearing goes ahead. Today. On schedule. She said, and I'm quoting, 'If something is watching us, I would like it to see us do this properly.'"

The Chair of the Concordia Council was a small, gray-haired woman named Adaeze Okafor, from the Southern Compact, and she had been a judge for thirty-one years before she took this job, and it showed.

She sat at the round black table with her hands folded on it and waited, without speaking, until all three hundred people in the crater had stopped talking. It took four minutes. She did not hurry them. She just sat there, with the ring in the sky above her and the wind tugging at her gray hair, looking at each section of the benches in turn until it fell quiet.

Then she said, in a voice that carried to the rim without being raised:

"This is a hearing of the Concordia Council on the Consent Charter and the Remains and Residue Data Act. It will be conducted under the old rules. Nobody will raise their voice. Nobody will use their law. Nobody will speak who has not been called." She paused. "And nobody will look at the sky while they are giving evidence. Look at the table. The table is where the decision gets made."

She turned to the sixth chair, set apart.

"Mister Zhou. Please sit down."

He sat.

The chair was white stone, very old, worn smooth by a long line of people he would never know. From it, he could see the whole table: the five delegations in their high-backed chairs, each with a small team of advisers on the benches behind them. And behind each delegation, standing beside its flag post, perfectly still, a King.

Su Mingli, behind Huaxia, with her coat buttoned to the throat.

Marshal Kirill Orlov, behind the Northern Union, two hundred years old, tall and gaunt in his long gray coat, his pale eyes on the sky.

Shirasagi Ren, behind Yamato, all in white.

A heavy-shouldered man with a shaved head and a silver beard behind the Atlantic Federation, whose name Yuanshu had never been told, and whose aura sat on the crater floor like a weight on a drum.

And behind the Southern Compact, a very old woman in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees, who seemed to be asleep.

She is not asleep.

She is the oldest King on Blue Star. Older than Orlov.

And she is the only person in this crater who is not looking at the ring.

She is looking at you.

Behind the Huaxia delegation, on the lowest bench, sat his own people.

The witnesses in a long row, in their best clothes, holding hands without seeming to notice they were doing it. Fang Yu with her father's note. Mrs. Pan. Gao Yinping with her hard hat in her lap. Fat Tan with one ledger, just one, because Catherine Moore had told him he could bring one.

General Qin in his plain greatcoat, upright as a post.

Doctor Iris Kwan, who had arrived on a separate flight in the night and was sitting very small at the end of the bench in a gray suit, holding her laptop in its two supermarket bags on her knees.

Sister Ning, with her candle, unlit, and beside her twelve of the Quiet who had come on the Long Patience, all with candles, all with their eyes half closed.

And at the very end of the row, between two Bureau guards, in his gray cardigan, Gu Hanshan, with his hands folded in his lap, looking at the round table with an expression of polite, attentive interest, like a man at a lecture.

He caught Yuanshu looking. He smiled, and nodded, very slightly.

Yuanshu looked back at the table.

The Yamato delegation was last.

They came up over the rim of the crater without a word, and the noise in the benches died at once.

Eight men in plain white robes, walking in perfect step down the black terraces. On their shoulders, on two long poles of dark wood, they carried a palanquin. A small wooden house with a curved roof, no bigger than a wardrobe, with paper screens for walls. It swayed very slightly as they came down the steps. The men carrying it moved as if they were carrying something that might break if they breathed on it.

Shirasagi Ren walked in front of them. She did not look at anyone.

They set the palanquin down on the stone floor beside the Yamato chair, very gently, as if setting down a sleeping child, and stepped back, and knelt.

Through the paper screens, faint and gray, you could see a shape sitting inside. Cross-legged. Head bowed.

He came.

He has not been moved in twelve hundred years.

They carried him down the mountain, and across the sea, and up four hundred and twelve steps.

I can feel him listening.

From inside the palanquin, very faintly, so that only Yuanshu and Seed heard it, the old bell-voice spoke.

"Hello, child."

"You are still thinking too loudly."

"And so is everyone else here."

"I would hurry, if I were you. Something above us has noticed."

Chair Okafor looked at the palanquin for a long moment. Then at the ring in the sky. Then at the table, as she had told everyone else to.

"The Council will hear the first proposal," she said. "The Remains and Residue Data Act. The Atlantic Federation has the floor."

Secretary Harlan Voss stood up.

He was tall and handsome and silver at the temples, and he smiled at the crater like a man smiling at an old friend. Beside him, Garrett Hale sorted papers without looking up. On the bench behind them sat a small team from Meridian Dynamics, with a long black case on the stone at their feet, the size of a coffin.

"Madam Chair," said Voss, warmly. "Members of the Council. Before I begin, I'd like to say what everyone here is thinking." He did not look up. He very carefully did not look up. He just raised one hand, palm open, toward the sky. "We do not know what that is. And that, Madam Chair, is precisely our point."

He let it sit.

"Something is coming," said Harlan Voss. "We all know it now. We can all see it. And on the day it comes, the only question that will matter is whether we were ready. Whether we knew what our people would do. Whether we knew what our awakeners would do." He turned, slowly, and looked at the sixth chair. "Whether we knew what he would do."

Behind him, the Meridian team opened the long black case.

Inside, packed in gray foam, lay a slim black column of machine and glass, about two meters long, humming faintly. A small light near its top blinked green.

"Members of the Council," said Harlan Voss. "May I introduce WARDEN."

Three thousand meters above the round black table, the small pale ring in the sky turned, very slightly, the way an eye turns toward a sound.
