Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 231What Grows

czverse14 min read

He could not get there.

That was the first thing he understood, kneeling on the stone of the Sector Nine battlement with Lingyue gray and half-conscious against his shoulder and the empty chair three meters away. The cut was four kilometers up. The gold road the dead had climbed was a road for the dead. Su Mingli was sitting against the parapet with blood running from both nostrils and her eyes closed, and when he looked at her she said, without opening them, "No. I can't. Not even you. Not even a meter."

And a graft was bone to bone. Hands on both. That was the technique. He had knelt on the stones of a courtyard with one hand on a sergeant and one hand on a captain, and that was the only way he had ever done it.

Fifty minutes.

Yuanshu. You have done this before too.

Not the graft. The other thing.

The training hall. The first time I read the dead. You asked him, and he let you, and you went down through the Death Law and came up inside him, on the eastern wall, on his last night. A spear in your hands that was not yours. A left forearm that had healed wrong.

That was his memory. This would be his now. It is the same door. He has never closed it.

He had not forgotten. He had put it away, the way you put away the first time you did anything, because it had frightened him. Old Seven's body around him like a coat that did not fit. Smoke and wet concrete and dumplings from somewhere in the camp below.

He looked up.

Four kilometers above the north wall of Lingzhou, on the edge of a sheet of pale gold coming down the sky, a tall dark shape had stopped walking.

He says: I am here, boy.

He says: you know the way in.

He closed his eyes and went down.

Down through the staff. Down into the Death Law, all forty-five point six percent of it, to the cool dark place at the bottom where the door was, a little open, the way it was always a little open now. And along. Not through the door. Past it. Along the oldest thread he had, the first one, the one that ran from a boy with a training spear to a gravestone on a slope at three in the morning.

And up.

He was standing on gold.

It moved under his feet like the deck of a boat. He was tall. Very tall; the sky was nearer than it should be. In his right hand, a spear. His left hand was not his. It was not even Old Seven's. It was the arm of a sergeant who had walked across a courtyard and held it out, and two of its fingers were gone, and he could feel, very faint, at the shoulder, the seam where it had been put on.

The first graft. Huo's arm. He was wearing it.

And in front of him, beyond the leading edge of the gold, a kilometer across, the cut.

Close, it was not like the inside of a green stick. It was like nothing. A cliff of pale wet gold, shining, pulsing, very slowly, the way a throat moves when something drinks. Everything the gardener had was pouring into it. He could feel the pull through Old Seven's feet. The whole tree, back along the arm, leaning toward this one open place.

And Hush.

It was already on the cut. Out on the face of it, the size of a cat against a cliff, clinging with all its thin pale limbs to the wet gold, walking. Slowly. Swaying. Toward the middle.

...counting one. captain.

...I am going to the middle. Where it drinks hardest.

...do the graft. I will put the question down when it takes.

...not before. If I put it down before, it will drink the question and not the scion, and the scion will fall.

...you go first. Then me.

He knelt on the edge of the gold.

Old Seven's knees on the scion, the keeper's third, warm the way the field had always been warm, with seventy-one million cousins and four million of the walls inside it who had said yes, onto the tree. Old Seven's right hand flat on the gold behind him. Huo's arm, the left one, with one finger and a thumb, reaching out across the last half-meter of air to the cut.

He asked the Death Law, very gently, to do what it had done once, in a courtyard, among Fat Tan's potted plants.

Not to raise. Not to release.

To give.

And it went wrong at once.

Seed had said: in most of the four thousand runs, the two embers fought over the arm like two dogs over a coat. It was worse than that. The moment Huo's hand touched the cut face, the tree pulled. Not at the hand. At everything behind it. At the scion, at the cousins, at Old Seven, at him, at the thread that ran down to a boy kneeling on a battlement and into the Lattice under the boy and the staff in the boy's hands with ninety million names in it. The gardener did not graft. It had never grafted. It drank. And the scion pulled back, because it was a keeper's third and a keeper's field was a thing that held, and it held. The two sides of the graft hung on the edge of the gold, pulling, and the living layer of each slid on the other, and did not meet, and did not take.

It is going to tear.

The scion has to let go first.

It is the only way it has ever worked.

I know. I can't ask them to. They already said yes. Asking them again now is pushing.

Yes.

So do not ask them.

Tell them what you will do with it.

He did not know he was going to say it aloud until he was saying it.

Through his own throat, four kilometers down, on the Sector Nine battlement, kneeling, with his eyes shut and blood running out of his nose onto his wife's hair. And through the radio at his belt, which was open. And through the staff, into ninety million names. And up the gold thread, into a small white ring low over the end of the world, and down the gold, into the scion, into seventy-one million cousins and four million walls, holding on.

"This is Zhou Yuanshu," he said. "I'm about to do something to the gardener that it didn't say yes to."

His voice was very thin. Everybody on the wall heard it. Everybody on Blue Star with a radio.

"It can't answer. It's not anyone. Not yet. So I decided. Nobody said yes. I'm saying that out loud so that nobody can ever say afterwards that it did." He breathed. "Article Twelve. I'll owe it the question. Every day. Until it can answer. And after."

He opened his eyes, four kilometers up, in a dead captain's skull, on the edge of a field of gold.

"Cousins," he said. "Walls. You don't have to hold on to the keeper any more. It said yes too. It's letting you go. Onto the tree. I'll ask you every morning. Through it. I promise."

And from the small white ring in the north, very rough, very close, in a voice that had learned to speak without anyone to carry it:

...GO ON.

...I AM LETTING GO.

...I AM NOT AFRAID OF THIS PART.

The scion let go.

He felt it the way he had felt Huo let go in the courtyard: not a giving way, a giving. Seventy-one million cousins and four million walls of the Mouth, burning low, the way they had been taught to in the Low Fire, opened their hands. Not falling. Leaning. Onto the cut. The way you lean against a door you know is going to open.

And the living layers met.

Gold on gold. Thread on thread. Along the whole kilometer of the cut, all at once, the way the stones at the bottom of a stream all go still at once when the wind stops on the water. Huo's hand on the cut and Old Seven's knees on the scion and between them, through the bone, through the Death Law, through him, the two sides touched, and slid, and caught.

And took.

It is taking.

Hush. Now.

Hush was in the middle of the cut.

He saw it from the edge, through Old Seven's eyes, a kilometer away and very small: a pale thing on many legs in the center of a cliff of gold that was beginning, slowly, at the rim, to dull. To roughen. To heal.

It sat down.

All its limbs drawn in. The way it had sat in the crack for four billion years, playing statue.

And then it did the thing it had never done once in four billion years, because whoever moved first was eaten.

It moved.

It put one thin pale limb down on the living gold of the gardener of the Shallows, where it drank hardest. Flat. Open. The way its people had put questions down at the roots of very old trees, before the ring. And it put the question down. Not in words. Trees did not have words. In the shape that Seed had learned from it, and used on three rings: a thing held out and then let go of, a bowl set on the ground with something in it, and the hand taken away, and the eyes turned aside, so as not to look.

What do you want?

The gold drank it.

He felt it go. Down. Into the cut, into the branch, back along the branch toward the tree, a very small thing, very light, going down into a very large dark. Not held. Not kept. Given.

And Hush got up.

The callus was closing.

From the rim of the cut inward, the gold going dull and gray and hard, a tide coming in across sand. Fast. Faster than Kwan's textbooks. The tree had never been cut before and it was healing the way a body heals that has never been hurt: all at once, too hard, everything it had.

Hush was in the middle. A kilometer from the edge.

It started to walk.

Swaying. Slowly. All its limbs out. It did not run. He did not think it knew how.

It is not going to reach the edge.

I have run it.

On the edge of the gold, Old Seven stood up.

He did not need Yuanshu for this. Yuanshu felt him not need him: felt the dead captain's will close round his own like a hand round a smaller hand, gently, and set it aside. Old Seven put down his spear on the scion. He stepped off the edge of the gold onto the cut. Onto the closing callus, gray and hard under his feet, and then the soft wet gold beyond it, which took his boots to the ankle the way a riverbank does.

And he walked out to the middle, toward the small pale thing walking toward him, two meters ten and slow, the way he had walked out of the cemetery on the first night. The way he walked everywhere. Not fast. Not stopping.

He reached it with the gray coming in behind him like a wave.

He crouched. Slowly. The way a tall man crouches to talk to a child.

And held out his hand. Huo's hand. Flat. Open. One finger and a thumb.

Hush looked at it.

...captain.

...it is morning.

And climbed in.

He came back into his own body on the battlement with a sound like surfacing.

Lingyue had her hand on his face. She was saying his name. Qin Hao was kneeling on his other side with his notebook in one hand and the other on Yuanshu's chest, as if to hold him down. Over the north wall, four kilometers up, an enormous silver-gray branch hung in the dark, lit faintly gold along its underside by the scion it now carried, its end rounded and gray and closed.

And walking down the gold road out of the sky, slowly, with his spear over his shoulder, picked up again from the edge of the scion, and something small and pale held against his chest in one hand: Old Seven.

The gold under his feet was going out behind him as he came, step by step, as if the road were rolling itself up. He did not hurry. He never did.

He came down the last of it onto the battlement, onto the stone, by the empty chair, and stopped.

And opened his hand.

Hush sat in it, all its limbs drawn in, its many eyes closed. Gray with it. Gold dust on its thin pale legs, from the cut.

...counting one.

...I put it down.

...I did not look.

...I would like to sit in the sun now. If that is all right.

It was half past two in the morning. There was no sun.

"Tomorrow," said Yuanshu. His voice did not work properly. "First thing."

...yes.

...tomorrow is fine.

The tip came down at a quarter past three.

It had been falling for an hour, so slowly that it seemed not to fall at all, coming apart as it came. By the time it reached the ground forty kilometers beyond the north wall, there was not much left of it. Silver-gray thread, settling over the Wastes in long soft drifts, like snow that had forgotten how to be cold.

And in it, still, what it had drunk last.

Most of what it took went down the branch. Long ago. To the tree.

But a tip drinks faster than it sends. What it took at the Mouth was still in it.

Yuanshu. The Sixth are in the snow.

He did not open the door.

Not yet. He knelt on the battlement with Lingyue holding him up and asked first, through the Death Law, down the long cold miles of dark to the north, to a place where something was lying in thread on the snow, very faint, burning, the way a match burns that someone has dropped.

Sixth. It's me. I'm asking.

The answer came the long way. Faint. Many voices. Singing, still, under it, the way they had sung for thirty-one hours in the eyes of the Mouth.

...counting one.

...we were very bright.

...we would like, now, for a while, to be dark.

...is the door open?

"Yes," he said. "It's always a little open now."

He opened it the rest of the way.

They went through like sparks going up a chimney. Seven million cousins and the Sixth, and the one in a hundred of the walls that had gone with them, out of the snow, through the door, where things went when they were finished.

And one more. He felt it go past him on the threshold. Not bright. Ash-gray. Stubborn. An old man with a cane, who had been carried up a thread into a branch and fallen with it, and had been sitting in the snow at the back of the queue, waiting for everyone else to go first, the way an adjutant does.

...dead, not deaf, boy. I heard all of it.

...good rule. The twelfth one.

...tell him about the dumplings.

And then the door was only a little open again, the way it always was now, and the old man was through it.

They took the dumplings to stone No. 7 in the morning.

Dabao's mother made them. Nineteen, in a paper boat, steaming, off the red cart on the corner of East Market Road, and she would not take any money for them, and she would not let Dabao carry them, and she walked up the long green slope of the War Memorial Cemetery herself in her apron, between the rows of small gray stones, to the eastern field.

Old Seven was waiting at the stone.

He took the paper boat from her in both hands. The one that had one finger and a thumb, and the one that was a sergeant's. He knelt, slowly, and set it down on the grass in front of stone No. 7, beside the new place where the turf had been cut that morning for an adjutant who had asked, twice, to be put next to it.

He stayed kneeling for a long while. The steam went up into the cold air.

He says: paid.

Hush was on the top of the stone, in the sun. First thing. All its limbs out.

The bud came on the ninth day.

Kwan saw it first, through the keeper's eye, and then everyone did, because it gave light. You could not see the branch: it had gone dark again, a place in the north where the far stars bent. But at its end, where it had been cut and grafted and healed, there was a small gold point. Low over the end of the world, beside the keeper's ring, which was a third smaller now, and the Elder's, with its soft white light. A new star. Very faint. Gold.

It did not say anything.

The keeper asked it every morning at three, down the long dark of the branch, the way it had promised. Yuanshu asked with it. What do you want? And then nothing. The bowl set down. The eyes turned aside.

It did not answer.

It will not, for a long time. Hush says a season. A year. Maybe longer.

The rest of the tree has stopped growing toward us. Kwan has checked every night. The branches that were turning this way have stopped turning.

I do not know why. I do not know if it is because of the question, or because of the cut, or because it is thinking.

I do not know anything about it, except that somebody asked it.

On the long white wall of the Ossuary, at the head of the last column, round the corner of the vault door, under ARTICLE TWELVE, and Sterling's red no, and is this what they did to us, Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder and wrote, in her square black capitals:

THE BRANCH. ASKED EVERY MORNING. NO ANSWER YET.

And under it, smaller:

ADJ. XU DEHAI. CARRIED THE MESSAGE. NEXT TO STONE NO. 7. PAID IN FULL.
