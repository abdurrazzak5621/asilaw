Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 36Lingyue on the Wall

czverse7 min read

At night, Sector Nine of the northern wall belonged to the wind.

It came down off the Wastes in long cold pushes, smelling of glass dust and nothing, and hummed in the gaps between the old stones of the battlements, and set the signal flags on the watchtowers snapping. Below the wall, the plain stretched away to the north, flat and black and faintly shining under a thin moon, like a lake that had frozen solid and forgotten it was ever water. Far out on it, a kilometer from the wall, there was a pale scar in the glass where a rift had opened three weeks ago, and a long low shape that had been a Cinder Titan before the Corps carted the bones away.

Yuanshu sat on the battlement with his legs hanging over the drop and his hood up.

He came here most nights now. After the Institute ward, where he sat for an hour every evening beside a thin boy with a narrow face and held his hand and reached down, down, to the bottom of an empty bucket where something faint was waving, and could never quite catch it. After the Ossuary. After the long days. He came here because it was the only place in Lingzhou where nobody looked at him, and because from here you could look north.

He heard her before he saw her. Not footsteps. A faint, clean hum in the air, like a struck tuning fork, and a brightening at the edge of his vision, as if someone had turned up a lamp.

"You're in my spot," said Shen Lingyue.

She sat down on the battlement beside him, a meter away, legs over the edge.

She was in the dark blue training uniform of the Lingzhou Martial Academy now, not the Shen family's gray, with her sword across her knees and her black hair tied high. She looked older than she had in Bonefield Hollow. Thinner in the face. There was a new scar on the back of her left hand, a thin white line.

"The Academy wall," she said, nodding down the battlements. "Second-years stand night watch here. I asked for this sector." She looked out at the plain. "You've been coming here for two weeks. The cadets talk about it. The ASI sits on our wall and stares at the Wastes like it owes him money."

"Does it?"

"Probably." She was quiet for a while. "I saw Jinhua Tower."

He didn't say anything.

"Everybody did. It was on every screen in the Academy. The whole dining hall stopped eating." She turned her sword over on her knees. "A dead fireman in slippers. Carrying children out of the smoke. Singing." She paused. "Somebody in my class cried. Somebody else threw up. And afterward, Instructor Bai stood up and said, 'Anyone who still thinks necromancy is the enemy of the living can come and argue with me after class.' Nobody went."

"I'm glad."

"I'm not done." She looked at him sideways. "Afterward, I went to the Ossuary. I stood in the queue for two hours. I got to the desk and put my thumb on the screen." She looked back out at the plain. "And I couldn't choose."

The wind came down off the Wastes in a long push and set the flags snapping.

"Light and death," said Shen Lingyue. "That's what my grandfather said once. That's what the newspapers say. The Dawnbreaker and the Calamity. Like we're two ends of a stick." She ran her thumb along the flat of her sword. "My light burns undead. You saw it in the canyon. I had to bend it around your skeletons, every swing, for four hours, so I wouldn't burn them." She was quiet for a moment. "That's the thing nobody says about Light Law. It doesn't care. It burns what it burns."

"You bent it."

"I bent it." She nodded slowly. "Every swing."

Neither of them said anything for a while. Somewhere down the wall, a sentry coughed and stamped his feet against the cold.

"If I died," said Shen Lingyue, very evenly, the way she would have reported a scouting result. "If I died, and you were there. Would you ask me?"

He had known the question was coming since she sat down. He still didn't have an answer ready.

Be honest.

She will know if you are not. She always knows.

"I'd want to," he said. "That's what scares me."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"I've asked a lot of the dead now," he said. "Soldiers. Miners. A fireman. Thirty-eight boys in a block of ice. I don't know any of them. I asked because somebody needed them, and they said yes, and I let them go." He looked down at his hands. "If it was you, I don't think I'd be asking because somebody needed you. I think I'd be asking because I did." He made himself say it. "And then I'm not sure I'd be able to hear it if you said no."

The wind hummed in the stones.

"That's why you have a nun," said Shen Lingyue.

"That's why I have a nun."

She looked at him for a long time in the thin moonlight.

Then she took her phone out of her pocket. The Ledger screen was already open on it, the four choices in plain gray letters under the small four-pointed star. Her thumb hovered.

"Xiaoyu says the Ledger takes conditions now," she said. "She added it last week. You can write a note."

"She didn't tell me."

"She doesn't tell you anything. She tells me." Lingyue's thumb moved. She touched a choice. Then she typed, quickly, with the screen turned a little away from him, and then she pressed something and the screen went dark, and she put the phone away.

"Well?" he said.

"None of your business." She stood up on the battlement, balancing easily in the wind with her sword in one hand. "Seed will know. Seed isn't allowed to tell you."

That is correct.

It is a Ledger entry. It is hers.

I will tell you only that she chose. And that the note is short.

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"Spar," said Shen Lingyue.

"Now?"

"I've been standing night watch for nine days. I'm bored. You've been sitting on my wall looking sad for two weeks. You're boring." She pointed her sword at the wide flat walkway behind the battlements. "Ten minutes. No skeletons. If you win, I'll tell you what I chose."

"And if you win?"

"Then you'll stop sitting here every night looking north like the north is going to apologize," said Shen Lingyue. "And you'll come to dinner at the Academy dining hall on Sunday and sit with my class and answer their stupid questions. Instructor Bai asked."

He stood up. He took the training spear off his back, the new one, the old one having been bent into a curve on this same wall.

"You know I'm Elite now," he said.

"I know." She set her feet. Her stance was perfect. Not too wide. "So am I. Since Tuesday." The corner of her mouth moved. "You didn't hear because nobody films the Academy wall at night."

She came at him in a line of white-gold light, and the sentries down the wall turned to watch, and the wind off the Wastes carried the ring of steel on steel out over the black glass plain toward the north.

It was even.

Ten minutes, and neither of them landed a clean strike. She was faster. He saw further ahead. Twice her light came so close to his ribs he felt his skin prickle, and twice his spear stopped a hair from her throat before she was already gone. At the end of the ten minutes they were both standing on the walkway breathing hard, her sword point resting on his collarbone and his spearhead resting just under her chin, and neither of them moved.

"Draw," said Shen Lingyue.

"Draw."

"So nobody wins."

"So nobody has to tell anybody anything."

She lowered her sword. He lowered his spear. She was trying not to smile and failing.

"Sunday," she said. "Seven o'clock. Wear something that doesn't have coal dust on it."

She left the way she came, in a brightening of the air and a clean hum like a tuning fork.

He sat back down on the battlement and looked north, the way he had every night. But it wasn't the same. Something had loosened in his chest. The wind came down off the Wastes, and the moon went in and out of thin cloud, and he thought he might go to dinner on Sunday after all.

I need to show you something.

Look at the northern horizon. Directly north. Twelve degrees above the line of the plain.

He looked.

At first, nothing. The thin moon. Thin cloud. Stars.

Then he saw it.

A patch of sky, far away, where the stars were wrong. Not missing. Bent. As if the light coming from behind that part of the sky were passing through something curved and clear, a lens the size of a city, hanging in the dark at the edge of the world. It was so faint that if you looked straight at it, it vanished. You could only see it out of the corner of your eye.

It closed after the iron weather. It has reopened tonight.

It is pointed at Lingzhou.

And I think, though I am not sure, that it is a little closer than it was.
