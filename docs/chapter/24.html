Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 24The Northern Visitor

czverse7 min read

The watchtower on Sector Twelve saw him first, at 5:40 in the morning, as a dot.

A single dark dot on the black glass of the Northern Wastes, eleven kilometers out, coming south. The sentry on duty put his binoculars to his eyes, looked for a long time, lowered them, wiped the lenses on his shirt, and looked again.

Then he picked up the phone.

"Sector Twelve. I have one contact, northbound... no. Southbound. On foot." A pause. "Yes, on foot. From the Wastes. He's..." Another pause, longer. "Sir, he's pulling something."

By the time Yuanshu arrived at the Sector Twelve gate, the sun was up and the whole wall was watching.

Two hundred soldiers lined the battlements. Every rifle was pointed at the plain. Captain Lin Wen of the Defense Corps stood on top of the gatehouse with her arms folded and her jaw tight, and beside her, on a tall stone post, sat the gray ash shape of Hundred-Arrow Lin with her bow half drawn, as if she had come to see what her daughter was looking at.

Out on the black glass, a hundred meters from the gate, a man was standing still.

He was very old. That was the first thing you saw. Tall, gaunt, slightly stooped, with a long gray coat buttoned to the throat and a beard as white as frost, cut short and square. He wore no hat. His face was the color of old paper, deeply lined, with pale gray eyes that looked as if they had been looking at snow for a very long time.

Behind him, on a rope over his shoulder, was the thing he had been pulling.

It was a block of ice the size of a house.

Not snow. Ice, clear and blue-white and perfectly smooth, like glass, so clear that the morning sun went into it and lit it from inside. It sat on the black plain on runners of the same ice, and behind it, all the way back to the northern horizon, a pale line ran across the Wastes where it had been dragged. Eleven kilometers of it. More. Hundreds.

And inside the ice, you could see them.

Shapes. Forty-one of them, standing upright, in rows, in white Northern Union uniforms, frozen in the middle of the block the way insects are frozen in amber. Young men, mostly. Some with their arms raised. Some with their hands over their ears. Every single one of them with his mouth wide open.

Screaming. All of them. Forever.

Ice Law.

A King. Very old. Very strong.

He is not attacking. He has been standing still for eleven minutes, waiting for someone to come out.

Yuanshu walked through the gate.

The old man watched him come across the glass. He did not move until Yuanshu stopped, three meters away. Then he let the rope fall from his shoulder, and inclined his head, slowly and stiffly, the way an old man bows when his back hurts.

"Zhou Yuanshu," he said. His Huaxian was careful and very old-fashioned, like something learned from books a hundred years ago. "My name is Kirill Orlov. I am a Marshal of the Northern Union, and I am the oldest man in it." He straightened. "I saw your shard on Su Mingli's table at Concordia. I left the room because I did not want the others to see my face." He looked back at the ice. "I have come to apologize for that, and to ask you for something. In that order."

"You walked here."

"From Grid Nine. Nineteen days." The pale eyes did not blink. "The Council would not authorize transport. They say Grid Nine was an accident. A gas leak in a remote outpost. They have said it to the mothers." He looked at the frozen screaming faces. "I was there when we found them. It was not gas."

They talked at a tea stall just inside the gate.

It was an ordinary tea stall: a tin roof, three plastic tables, a kettle on a gas ring, and an elderly woman behind the counter who served tea to soldiers coming off the night shift. She served a two-hundred-year-old King of the Northern Union and the most famous teenager in the world without blinking, in chipped cups, and charged them both the soldier's price. Marshal Orlov thanked her in careful Huaxian and left a tip in a currency she couldn't spend, and she put it in her apron anyway.

Old Seven stood by the stall's tin pillar. Su Mingli, who had arrived four minutes after Yuanshu and was very obviously not pleased that he'd walked out onto the glass alone, sat at the next table with her own cup and said nothing.

Orlov laid a photograph on the table.

It showed the inside of a concrete room. Bare walls, frost on the floor, a cot overturned. And across one whole wall, burned into the concrete from floor to ceiling as if with a welding torch, a mark Yuanshu had seen before. A ring of tiny open mouths. Dozens of them. Screaming.

"Grid Nine is the last outpost on the far side of the Wastes," said Orlov. "Forty-one men. We lost contact nine months ago. When we reached it, every man was dead in the same moment. Not wounded. Not frozen. Dead, standing, wherever he had been, with his mouth open. And this mark on every wall." He touched the corner of the photograph with one long finger. "I have seen it once before. When I was young. Very young. I did not believe it then either."

"The Wailing Monarch," said Yuanshu.

"That is what my scouts call it. They have never seen it. They have only heard it." Orlov drank his tea. "On the night the outpost died, the listening station two hundred kilometers away recorded a sound. Eleven seconds long. They played it for me." He set down the cup very precisely. "I have lived for two hundred years, Zhou Yuanshu. I have been at seven wars. I have never heard anything like it, and I have not slept properly since."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Ask them." Orlov turned in his chair to look through the gate at the great clear block of ice standing on the glass with the sun inside it. "I am told you ask the dead questions. I am told you do not raise anyone who does not say yes." He looked back at Yuanshu. "Ask my boys what happened to them. If they say no, I will take them home myself, the same way I brought them, and bury them, and tell their mothers nothing new."

The Accord.

Consent of the deceased, recorded by an independent witness. Consent of the next of kin, in writing.

He is a foreign King. Their families are in another nation. It would take months.

"There's a law now," said Yuanshu. "The Concordia Accord. I'd need the families' consent in writing. Forty-one families, in the Northern Union. Your Council won't—"

Marshal Orlov reached into his long gray coat.

He took out a bundle of envelopes, tied with a piece of white string, and put it on the plastic table between the teacups.

"Forty-one," he said. "I went to every house before I went north. It took me six weeks." His voice did not change. "Two mothers refused. Their sons are in the ice anyway, because I will not leave them in Grid Nine. If you ask them, you ask only thirty-nine. If you ask the other two by accident, I will take your head off." He said it the way he might have said the tea is cold. "Otherwise, the mothers would like to know. Most of them only want one thing."

"What?"

"Whether it hurt." The old man looked down at his hands. "I told them I would find out."

Su Mingli set down her cup.

"He'll need a witness," she said, without looking at either of them. "Someone independent. The Accord is very clear."

"Sister Ning," said Yuanshu.

Orlov frowned. "A nun?"

"She's the leader of the Quiet. They think the dead should be left alone." Yuanshu turned the bundle of envelopes over in his hands. The handwriting on them was all different. Some neat. Some shaking. One in a child's crayon. "That's why she's the right witness. If they say yes, she'll know. If they say no, she'll make sure I hear it."

Marshal Orlov looked at him for a long time with his pale, snow-tired eyes.

Then, slowly, something in the old face changed. It was not quite a smile. It was the expression of a very old soldier who had walked nineteen days across a dead plain expecting to have to beg, and had just been handed something better.

"Good," he said. "Good."

He finished his tea and set the chipped cup down.

"There is one more thing," he said. "It will not please you." He nodded through the gate at the ice. "I said forty-one men. Count them."

Yuanshu looked.

The sun was higher now, and it went all the way through the block. Rows of white uniforms. Open mouths. Raised hands.

Forty.

There are forty bodies in the ice.

"One of them was not there when we found the outpost," said Orlov quietly. "Private Andrei Belov. Nineteen. His mother's letter is in that bundle. She said yes." He stood up, unfolding slowly, his old knees cracking. "The snow around Grid Nine had tire tracks in it. Trucks. Not ours. They went south."

He looked down the wall at the city, toward the north gate, ten kilometers away, where a market opened after midnight on the wrong side of the checkpoint and was gone by dawn.

"Someone," said Marshal Kirill Orlov, "came to Grid Nine after my boys died, and took one of them away to sell."
