Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 182You Didn't Ask Us

czverse8 min read

"Mister Zhou," said Chair Adaeze Okafor. "The last time you moved eleven thousand people, I asked you to ask the Council first. I said we'd have said yes. I said we'd have liked to be asked."

She did not raise her voice. She sat at the middle of the long black volcanic-glass table in the crater on Concordia with her hands folded in front of her, the way a judge sits, and looked down the table at the witness chair.

"Three weeks ago," she said, "you took five hundred and fifty-eight people, seven living and five hundred and fifty-one dead, up a gold road out of Lingzhou into a place above the sky that no nation on Blue Star has any jurisdiction over, to fight three things nobody on this Council had ever heard of, on behalf of a being that harvested a third of Yamato twelve hundred years ago." She paused. "We found out from Marshal Orlov's satellites. The next morning."

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

"Did it occur to you to ask us?"

He had thought about that question for three weeks. On the gold road coming down. On the vault steps of the Ossuary. On the step of the Sector Nine battlements, with the small white ring over the end of the world. He had known it was coming.

"It occurred to me," he said. "I didn't."

"Why not?"

"Because there were six days, and I was afraid you'd say no."

The crater was quiet.

Four hundred and twelve steps up from the jetty. The ring of white stone benches, open to the sky. Fifty people on them, perhaps sixty: delegations, Kings, witnesses. Orlov, with Sokolova behind him. The woman from the Southern Compact whose name was Ngozi Eze and who had been sent because there was no King to send. Elias Brandt, with his reading glasses in his hand. An empty chair for Yamato, because Ren would not leave the mountain while there were still people on it with their faces turned up. Sister Ning, with the Ledger. Lingyue, on the bench behind the witness chair, with her grandfather's sword across her knees.

And a man he did not know at the Huaxia end of the table, in a dark green uniform with a general's stars, sitting very straight.

"That is honest," said Okafor. "I'll give you that. It's also the reason Article Seven has a Commission." She turned her head. "General Han."

The man in the dark green uniform stood.

He was perhaps sixty. Lean, gray, very correct. General Han Yizhou of the Huaxia Defense Ministry, Seed said: Northern War Zone, thirty years on the wall at Heishui, no Law that Seed could read at this distance, which meant either he was not an awakener or he was a very good one. He did not look at Yuanshu. He looked at the Council.

"Madam Chair," he said. "Members. I want to be clear about one thing before I say anything else. I have read every word of the record. I have read the Western Pass. I have read Port Abiri and Sandu and the mountain in Yamato. I am not here to tell this Council that Mister Zhou is a danger to Blue Star." He folded his hands behind his back. "I am here to tell you that he is the only person on Blue Star who decides when Blue Star goes to war above the sky. And that nobody gave him that job."

Nobody said anything.

"Fifty-two thousand dead," said General Han. "Bound to one man. On the register of this Council's own Commission. Defensive only, the register says. Article Seven. The dead may defend, never conquer." He turned, at last, and looked at Yuanshu. "Mister Zhou took five hundred and fifty-one of them across a border. Into a place that is not Blue Star. To fight a war that was not Blue Star's. Because a foreign power asked him to." He paused. "Is that defense?"

"The keeper isn't a foreign power."

"The keeper is a thing above the sky that harvested human beings for longer than our species has existed," said General Han, without heat. "It asked you for help. You said yes. You did not ask anyone on Blue Star whether Blue Star wanted to go to war for it." He looked back at the Council. "This time it was five hundred dead and seven volunteers. Next time, it may be fifty thousand. And the next time something above the sky asks Mister Zhou for help, I would like it to have to ask us too."

Okafor let the silence go on.

Then she turned to the witness chair.

"Mister Zhou. Answer him."

He sat in the chair that had once had a sign on it that said THE SUBJECT, and thought about it. He did not want to answer quickly. He had learned that on a gatehouse step at the Western Pass.

"He's right," said Yuanshu.

A sound went round the benches. Not quite a murmur.

"Not about all of it," he said. "The dead I took said yes. They weren't sent. I asked fifty-two thousand and fifty-one thousand said no, and I didn't take them. The Commission has the record. Sister Ning read it in." He looked down the table at General Han. "But he's right about the part that matters. I asked the dead. I asked the living who came. I asked the keeper. I didn't ask Blue Star." He was quiet for a moment. "I've been writing a sentence for this Council for a month. Article Eleven. Whoever asks another what they want shall say, before the answer is given, what will be done with it." He looked at Okafor. "I asked the keeper what it wanted. It told me. And I didn't tell Blue Star what I was going to do with that answer. I just did it."

"So you accept the General's point," said Brandt.

"I accept that I should have asked."

"And if you had," said General Han. "And we had said no?"

The crater was very quiet.

"Then I'd have been standing in someone else's window," said Yuanshu. "Eight billion people's. And I'd have had to decide whether my yes stopped there." He looked at the general. "I don't know what I'd have done. I'd like to tell you I'd have stayed. I don't know if that's true." He held the general's eyes. "I'd rather you knew that than heard me say something I'm not sure of."

General Han looked at him for a long time.

Then, unexpectedly, the lean gray man in the dark green uniform sat down.

"Thank you," he said. "That is the first honest answer I have heard from anyone with that much power in thirty years." He folded his hands on the table. "I don't like it. I'd like it written down."

I like him.

I did not expect to.

They argued about the commas for six hours.

Okafor had said they would. She had promised. Article Eleven went through four drafts in the crater that afternoon, with the delegations passing sheets of paper up and down the black glass table and Elias Brandt putting his glasses on and taking them off and Marshal Orlov, at one point, standing up and reciting from memory a sentence of Huaxian legal grammar from two hundred years ago that he said settled the matter of the second comma forever, and was overruled.

At six in the evening, with the sun going down over the rim of the crater and the wind coming cold off the sea, Okafor read the fifth draft.

"Article Eleven. Whoever asks another what they want shall first say what will be done with the answer."

"A question asked in order to take is not a question. No answer given to it binds the one who gave it."

"This Article binds every asker: persons, the dead, the powers above the sky, and the nations of this Council. Where the answer of one would be acted upon in the name of all, all shall be asked."

She took off her glasses.

"That last sentence is General Han's," she said. "He wrote it at three o'clock. On the back of a napkin." She looked down the table. "Mister Zhou. Do you accept it?"

He read it again, in his head, the way Seed read things.

Where the answer of one would be acted upon in the name of all, all shall be asked.

"It means next time the keeper asks me," he said, "I have to bring it here."

"It means next time anything above the sky asks anyone on Blue Star for anything that would commit Blue Star," said Okafor, "they bring it here. You. Me. Marshal Orlov. The Atlantic Federation. Anyone." She folded her hands. "And if there are six days, we meet in six hours. And if there are six hours, we meet in six minutes." She looked at him. "We'd have said yes, Mister Zhou. I want you to know that. We'd have said yes to the keeper. We'd have wanted to be asked."

"I know."

"Then do you accept it?"

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

It passed five to nothing. General Han's hand was the first one up.

On the steps going down to the jetty, at dusk, Lingyue walked beside him with her grandfather's sword on her back and did not say anything for two hundred steps.

Then she said: "You told them you didn't know."

"I didn't."

"You could have said you'd have stayed. They'd have believed you. Han would have believed you."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

He thought about it for another fifty steps. Below them, at the jetty, the Council plane waited with its lights on, and the sea was going dark.

"Because the Collector asks," he said. "It asks very kindly. It says exactly the right words. It follows the rule better than anyone on Blue Star." He looked at the sea. "The only difference between us and it is what we do after. If I start telling people what they want to hear, so they'll say yes, I'm doing what it does."

Lingyue was quiet for a while.

"That's a very you answer," she said.

That is what I always say.

"I know," said Lingyue. "I've been listening to Seed for a year. It's catching."

At the bottom of the steps, General Han Yizhou of the Huaxia Defense Ministry was standing on the jetty in his dark green uniform, alone, looking out at the sea. He turned when they came down.

"Mister Zhou," he said. "I'm going to Lingzhou tomorrow. To the Ossuary. I'd like to read your wall." He paused. "I'm asking. You can say no."

"You can read it," said Yuanshu. "Anyone can. That's what it's for."

General Han nodded once, the way General Qin nodded, the way old soldiers nod at a thing that has been said correctly.

"My son was at Grid Nine," he said. "Forty-one of them. In white. I'm told they stood in the field with their eyes open three weeks ago." He looked at the sea. "I'd like to know if his name is on it."

He did not wait for an answer. He walked up the gangway into the plane.

It is.

Han Jiale. Private. Grid Nine. Nineteen.

He was one of the forty who went up.

He knelt down in the first empty ring and put his hand inside it.
