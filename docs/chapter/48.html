Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 48Gu's Garden

czverse8 min read

Gu Hanshan lived in a small gray house at the end of a quiet street on the eastern hill, and the garden was the most beautiful thing Yuanshu had ever seen.

It was not large. A walled square of earth behind the house, perhaps twenty paces across. But every centimeter of it was alive. Tomatoes climbing bamboo canes in neat rows, heavy and red. Beans. Squash with leaves as big as umbrellas. Herbs in clay pots along the wall: mint, basil, coriander, things Yuanshu didn't know the names of. A plum tree in one corner, an apricot in another. Roses. A small pond with a frog sitting on a stone. And bees. Everywhere, bees, moving lazily from flower to flower in the warm afternoon light, so many that the whole garden hummed.

On the windowsill of the kitchen, in a blue glazed pot, stood the lemon tree from his office.

A Bureau guard sat on a folding chair at the gate with a newspaper and a thermos. He looked at Yuanshu's pass, and at Old Seven, and back at Yuanshu, and said, "He's in the back. He's always in the back. He gave me a bag of tomatoes yesterday." He paused. "They were very good tomatoes."

Gu was kneeling in the dirt between two rows of beans, in an old straw hat and a pair of gardening gloves, with his gray cardigan hung over the handle of a wheelbarrow. His shirtsleeves were rolled up. His forearms were brown from the sun.

He looked up as Yuanshu came through the gate, and his face lit with genuine pleasure.

"Yuanshu! Oh, I'm glad. Nobody visits a suspended man. They're afraid they'll catch it." He sat back on his heels and pulled off one glove. "Come and sit. Mind the beans. Would your friend like a chair? I don't suppose he sits."

Old Seven did not sit. He stood just inside the gate, under the plum tree, with his spear grounded and his flame turned low, and watched.

Yuanshu sat on an upturned bucket at the end of the row.

Before he'd come through the gate, he had stopped in the street for a full minute and done what the old monk taught him. Gone quiet. All the way down. He held it now, carefully, the way you'd carry a full cup across a room. No racing thoughts. No four hundred and ten. No silver rain in the Western Pass. Just a boy on a bucket, in a garden, in the sun.

I can hear you a little. Very faintly.

Good. Keep holding it.

I am going to watch the band.

"How are they?" said Gu, going back to his beans. "The fourteen. I hear they're in the old teahouse. With the Quiet." He smiled at the soil. "That was clever. I'd never have thought of it. Silence." He pulled a weed, carefully, getting all the root. "Is the boy any better? Belov?"

"A little."

"I'm glad." He sounded it. "Truly. He was the youngest. I used to read to him in the evenings, you know. I don't know if it helped. Probably not." He dropped the weed in a bucket. "You'd think, with a doctorate in Rift biology and thirty years at the Institute, I'd know whether reading to a hollow helps. I don't. Nobody does. Most of what we know about minds would fit on the back of a seed packet."

He held up a bean pod, long and green, and snapped it, and offered Yuanshu half.

Yuanshu took it. It was sweet and crisp and tasted like summer.

"May I ask you something?" said Gu, after a while. "As a scientist. Not as a suspect." He smiled. "Although I understand I'm both."

"Go ahead."

"What do you think happens to them? The minds that go up the threads." He was weeding again, not looking up. "Everyone assumes they're destroyed. Eaten. The cores in the market are full of minds that were stripped, yes, torn apart, used as fuel. I've seen the gray crystal. I don't argue with that." He pulled another weed. "But the ones that go up. Whole. A whole person, every memory, pulled up a thread in one breath. Where do they go? What are they for?"

"You tell me."

"I don't know. I have a hypothesis." Gu sat back on his heels again and looked up at the sky, shading his eyes with his gloved hand, exactly the way the young monk had looked up at the ring twelve hundred years ago. "Look at this garden. Look at those tomatoes. Ten thousand years ago they were tiny bitter green berries on a weed in a mountain valley. Poisonous, nearly. And then someone started picking them. Saving the seeds of the best ones. Planting them again. Picking again." He smiled at the sky. "Ten thousand years of harvest. And now they're this."

The band has brightened.

From 1.0% to 1.4%.

He is talking about the sky.

"A harvest isn't only an ending," said Gu quietly. "It's how a field gets better. You take the best. You keep it. You make something from it that the field could never have made by itself." He turned to look at Yuanshu, and his kind, tired eyes were very bright. "What if the thing in the north isn't a predator? What if it's a gardener? What if every twelve hundred years it comes and takes the best minds on Blue Star, and keeps them, and makes something from them? Something larger. Better. Something no single human mind could ever be."

1.9%.

"The people it takes don't think it's better," said Yuanshu evenly. He held the quiet. He held it very carefully. "I heard three thousand of them under a bridge. They said is anyone."

"Those were the stripped ones. The fuel. The waste." Gu shook his head gently. "I'm talking about the ones who go up whole. Nobody's ever heard from them. Nobody's ever asked." He looked at Yuanshu with great kindness. "Including you."

A bee landed on the rim of the bucket between Yuanshu's feet, and walked along it, and flew away.

"You're saying it's mercy," said Yuanshu.

"I'm saying I don't know that it isn't." Gu took off his straw hat and turned it in his hands. "We are very small, Yuanshu. Very frightened, very short-lived, very cruel to each other. We forget everything. We make the same mistakes for ten thousand years. My mother died of a disease we'd had the cure for since before she was born, because she couldn't afford it." He looked at the hat. "I have spent my whole life in a laboratory trying to improve one or two minds at a time, a little, with medicine and theory. It's very slow. It doesn't work very well." He smiled. "And then I look up at the sky and think: what if something up there has been doing it properly, for a hundred thousand years? Improving minds. Keeping them. Making them better than they could ever be on their own."

2.6%.

That is the highest I have ever seen it.

And it is not his own law, Yuanshu. That is the important part. It does not feel like him. It feels like something listening through him. Like the hollows.

But fainter. Much fainter. Like a thread so thin it is almost not there.

"Without asking," said Yuanshu.

Gu looked at him. For a moment, something moved behind the kindness. Then it was gone, and he laughed softly and put his hat back on.

"You and your asking," he said fondly. "Your mother was exactly the same."

He walked Yuanshu to the gate. On the way he stopped at the kitchen window and lifted the blue glazed pot with the lemon tree in it off the sill, and turned it in the sunlight, checking the leaves.

"Lu's been watering it," he said. "On Thursdays. He brought it here himself. He sat in the kitchen and drank a cup of tea and didn't say anything for an hour and then he left." He set the pot back down. "He's a good man. He'll be all right. Whatever happens."

He reached up into the tree and picked a lemon, small and bright yellow, and held it out.

"For the boy," he said. "Belov. Put it by his bed. The smell's supposed to be good for waking. It's probably nonsense. I'm a scientist; I'm not supposed to believe it." He smiled. "But I'm also an old man with a garden, and I've stopped being sure about very much."

Yuanshu took the lemon.

At the gate, the guard had fallen asleep with the newspaper on his chest. Old Seven stepped through ahead of Yuanshu into the quiet street. The afternoon was golden and warm and full of bees.

"Come again," said Gu, leaning on the gatepost. "Before the island. I'd like to hear how it goes. I'd like to hear about the monks. Mingli says you went to Yamato." He shook his head admiringly. "Twelve hundred years of silence. Imagine that."

"I will," said Yuanshu.

He turned to go.

"And Yuanshu," said Gu Hanshan gently, behind him.

He stopped.

"Don't let the number frighten you," said Gu. "Four hundred days is plenty of time to change your mind."

He did not turn around.

He did not let anything move in his face. He held the quiet. He held it so hard that he could feel it in his jaw, in his hands, in the lemon in his fist. He nodded, once, without looking back, and walked down the quiet street in the sunlight with Old Seven beside him, at an ordinary pace, past the neat gray houses and the parked cars and a child on a bicycle, all the way to the corner.

At the corner, out of sight of the gate, he stopped, and leaned one hand on a wall, and let go.

Nobody told him.

Four hundred days. Nobody told him.

You. Me. Su Mingli. And Sister Ning, who heard you think it on a stair in an empty teahouse.

That is all. I have checked every conversation. I have checked every room you were in. It was never said aloud anywhere he could hear it. It was never written down.

Whoever's on the other end, thought Yuanshu. That's who else I said knew.

Yes.

He did not hear it from us.

He heard it from above.

He looked down at the lemon in his hand. Small and bright and perfect, smelling of summer.

Do not give that to Andrei.

I do not know what it is. I do not know if it is anything.

But do not put anything that man has touched beside that boy's bed.

He put the lemon in his pocket. He walked the rest of the way down the hill without speaking, with the sun warm on his back, and behind him, at the end of the quiet street, a kind man in a straw hat went back into his garden and knelt in the dirt among the bees and started weeding again, humming softly to himself, as if he had all the time in the world.
