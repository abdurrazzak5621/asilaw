Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 242The Other End

czverse12 min read

Dabao's mother brought the dumpling cart to the Ossuary at eight, and would not take any money, and would not let anyone else push it, and parked it on the pavement at the bottom of the steps in the summer evening with steam rolling off it into the street, and shouted at Dabao to bring more flour.

Dabao brought more flour. He had come straight from the north wall at the end of his shift, A-rank, Shield Guardian, Unbroken Wall, enormous in his gear, and the first thing he had done when he came through the bronze door was pick Xiaoyu up off the floor and hold her over his head like a trophy and shout SCRIBE at the ceiling of the banking hall until Fat Tan told him the pigeons were complaining.

"Bro," he said to Yuanshu afterward, on the steps, with a paper boat of dumplings in each fist. "Bro. Your sister told the rock what to call her. On television. My mother cried into the dough. There's going to be salt in all of these." He ate one. "There is. It's good salt."

The street was full. Not the way East Ring Road had been full, with a city waiting outside a hospital. Full the way a street is full on a summer evening when something good has happened: people sitting on steps, children running, the Mo twins arguing with a fruit seller, Hu Dabao's mother's cart with a queue down to the corner. Shi Lei and his father at the end of it, in their work clothes, the boy with a real spear now, plain steel, issued that afternoon, which he kept holding up to look at and putting down again.

Lingyue sat on the top step beside him with her grandfather's sword across her knees and a flame in her open palm the size of an apricot, pale and steady, that she had not seemed to notice was there since the hall.

"You're bigger," he said.

She looked down at it.

"Huh," said Shen Lingyue. "So I am." She closed her hand on it, and opened it again, and it was still there. "It came back when your mother stood up. I felt it. I didn't do anything." She looked at it a while longer. "I think it was just waiting for something worth being bright about."

Inside, Xiaoyu was at the wall.

She had been at it since they came home, on the stepladder, with the marker, writing the day. Not the names; there were too many names, tens of thousands, eleven halls, and Gao Min had them in her new ledger in columns with a heading nobody had ever used before. Xiaoyu was writing what they had said. The best of them. The ones that had come over the open channel and made the banking hall go quiet. SOMETHING THAT MAKES THINGS GROW. I WANT TO HEAR THE DEAD, NOT RAISE THEM. I JUST WANT TO FLY.

And at the top, at the head of the new column, before any of them, in her square black capitals:

WEN JIA. LINGZHOU. WENT FIRST.

He stood at the foot of the stepladder and watched her write it.

...the letters are different.

He looked.

They were. Not to look at. To look at they were the same black capitals Xiaoyu had written on that wall for two years. But when Seed looked through him, they were not ink. They were something written in a way nothing on that wall had ever been written, the way the three lines had hung in the air over the stone in the hall after she took her hand away. Set. Fixed. As if the plaster itself had agreed to remember.

I cannot remember her.

But I can read it. And when I read it, it is true. It is not like reading a name off a list. It is like being told by someone who was there.

That is not remembering. I know it is not. I have checked.

But it is something.

At the top of the stepladder, his sister had stopped writing, and was looking down at him, with the marker in her hand.

"It's talking about my handwriting," she said. "Isn't it."

"Yes."

"Good," said Xiaoyu, and went back to writing. "Somebody should."

Kwan came down the stairs at a quarter to midnight with the laptop held open in front of her like a tray of hot food, and he knew from her face.

"It's pulling again," she said.

The threads. Eleven fine pale lines on the screen, going up off the diagram of the Stele into the dark. Slack since midnight the night before, when eleven people from the bottom of a well had gone into eleven stones and sat down in the knots and not sent anything up. One tug, last night, all together, from the far end, like a line jumping in the hand of someone on a riverbank.

Now they were not slack.

They were taut. All eleven. Drawn straight up into the dark like the strings of a kite in a strong wind. And every few seconds, together, they jerked. Not hard. Steadily. The way a line jerks when whatever is on the end has stopped being curious and started to pull.

On the radio at Kwan's belt, from Port Abiri, through Ngozi Eze's handset, the old man who liked the sea:

...it's getting heavier.

...I'm holding it. We're all holding it. The girl in Lingzhou says hold on to the knot, not the thread.

...but someone should know. It's getting heavier.

And then, from the Lingzhou stone, three kilometers away on East Ring Road, through the staff, small and stubborn and seventeen, a voice he had not expected to hear again that night:

...captain's boy.

...there's something at the other end. On my thread. Not pulling. Something else.

...it's asking for the girl who writes.

They went at one in the morning.

Not all of them. Xiaoyu said that it was her it had asked for, and it was her window, and she would decide who came. She chose her brother, because the staff was the only way anything at the other end of a thread could be heard. And Old Seven, because he would come anyway. And Lingyue, because, Xiaoyu said, somebody should be there who could light the room.

Their mother did not ask to come. She sat in her wheelchair at the bronze door and watched them go down the steps into the warm dark of East Market Road, and said only, to her daughter, as she passed: "Don't answer anything."

"I know."

"It asks. And whatever you say, it keeps. Even no. Your brother told me. On the mountain, it asked him and he didn't say anything, and that was the only thing that worked."

"I know, Mom."

"So don't answer anything."

Xiaoyu stopped on the bottom step and looked back up at her.

"I'm not going to answer anything," she said. "I'm going to ask it something."

The Awakening Hall was dark, and the letters were still there.

Three lines of soft light, hanging in the air above the platform where she had left them that morning, in the empty bowl of four thousand seats. [Class: Scribe] [Chosen.] [Talent: — not marked —]. Under them, the black stone with its crack and its runes all pointing down, and a bright blue jacket laid over its shoulder.

The stone was warm. He could feel it from the doors.

Xiaoyu walked down the aisle between the tiers and up the three steps and stood in front of it, under her own letters, in her school jacket, with her marker in her pocket. Lingyue stood one step behind her with the pale flame in her open hand. Old Seven at the foot of the steps, facing out. Yuanshu beside him with the staff.

"Hello," said Xiaoyu to the stone. "It's me again."

...I know, said Wen Jia. ...I'm going to let it through now. If you want.

"I want."

...it's very old. Be careful. Don't tell it anything.

"I know," said his sister. "Everybody keeps saying."

It came up the thread the way it had come down the lens on the mountain.

Not a weight. Not a pressure. An attention. From further away than anything. Up out of the deep water between arms, along a thread finer than a hair, through the crown of the tree of Laws inside the stone, past a girl of seventeen sitting in a knot, into the hall.

Warm. That was the worst thing, still. Interested. Patient. The way a very old person is kind to a child who has come to visit.

Hello.

It arrived in his mind the way it had on the mountain, already finished, in his own language, like a cup set down very carefully on a table so as not to wake anyone. And in Xiaoyu's. He saw her go still.

You are the one who writes.

I heard you this morning. Everyone did. You told the stone what you wanted. You told it what to call you. It was very bright.

I did not keep it. It was not said to me.

I would like to tell you something first, before anything else. Your brother taught the keeper that. Tell first. I learned it from watching. I learn everything from watching.

Xiaoyu did not say anything.

The pulling on the threads is not me.

I want you to know that. I do not pull. I ask.

The stones are very old. Older than the gardens. We made things like them, once, a long time ago, to count. Then we stopped making things. I keep. That is all I do now.

But the one who picks still listens at the other end of the stones.

It has never felt one go quiet. Not once. Not in all the orchards. Tonight, eleven went quiet at once.

It does not ask. It never learned. Nobody ever taught it. It only knows that when the stones are bright, it comes, and when the stones are quiet, something is wrong.

It is pulling to see what is wrong.

It is going to come and look.

The hall was silent. The three lines of light hung over the platform. In Lingyue's palm, the pale flame did not flicker.

That is what I came to tell you.

And to ask you something.

I asked your brother, once, on a mountain, what he wanted. He did not answer. I said I would not ask him twice. I have not.

I have not asked you yet.

Child. You who write.

Xiaoyu took her hand out of her pocket.

She had the marker in it. Capped. She looked down at it for a moment. Then she looked up, not at the letters in the air, not at the stone, but at the place a long way off, past the roof and the sky and the keeper and the branch, where a thread finer than a hair went down into the deep water.

"Wait," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "I go first."

The attention paused.

"That's the rule," said his sister. "It's not on the wall yet but it's going to be. Whoever's asked the least goes first." Her voice was steady. He did not know how. "And nobody's ever asked you anything."

She uncapped the marker.

"Everybody asks everything now," she said. "My brother asked the keeper. The keeper asked the rings. The rings asked each other. Somebody asked the tree. Wen Jia asks every kid who touches this stone. You asked all those monks on the mountain, and the soldier behind the curtain, and the Architects, and you kept everything they said." She looked up into the dark. "But nobody ever asked you. Did they? In all that time. Since before the gardens. Nobody ever asked the one who asks."

The attention did not move.

"So I'm asking," said Xiaoyu. "I'm going to write down whatever you say. On the wall. And then I'm going to let it go. I won't keep it. It won't be mine. It'll just be written, so that nobody can say afterwards it wasn't there." She held up the marker. "That's what I'll do with your answer. I'm telling you first."

She took a breath.

"What do you want?"

Nothing.

For a long time. Longer than anything had ever been silent in front of him: longer than the keeper on the first night, longer than the rings, longer than the bottom of the well. The attention rested on the hall, warm, patient, enormous, and did not say anything at all, and he understood, standing at the foot of the steps with the staff in his hand and a dead captain beside him, that it was not waiting for her.

It was looking for the answer.

The way the first ring had looked, when the Elder's ring asked it every hour for five days. Inside. Where something used to be. The way a very old thing looks when someone asks it a question that nobody has asked it since before it was old, and it goes to the place where the answer should be, and finds that it has never once been there.

Lingyue's flame burned straight up in her open hand.

Xiaoyu stood under her own letters with the marker uncapped, waiting. She did not look in the bowl.

And then, very faint, from very far away, in a tone like a cup set down so carefully on a table that it made no sound at all:

...no one has asked me that.

The attention stayed on her a moment longer.

...I do not know.

...I have kept every want I was ever given. I do not know if I have one.

...I will look.

And then, after a long pause, so quietly that afterward none of them was sure it had been said, the oldest thing any of them had ever heard said the thing two hundred million cousins said every morning to a keeper above the sky, the thing the Elder said from inside a ring:

...ask me again tomorrow.

It went.

He felt it lift off the hall and go back down the thread, out through the crown of the tree in the stone, past Wen Jia in her knot, up through the roof and the sky and away, into the deep water. Not quickly. The way a very old person gets up from a table after a visit, slowly, thinking about something.

Xiaoyu stood on the platform for a long moment.

Then she capped the marker, and turned round, and came down the steps, very straight, past Lingyue, past her brother, past Old Seven, and sat down on the bottom step of the platform of the Awakening Hall in the dark, and put her face in her hands, and shook.

Lingyue sat down beside her and put an arm round her and said nothing at all.

...Yuanshu.

It did not keep anything.

It asked her nothing. She did not answer anything. She asked it.

And it said: tomorrow.

He stood at the foot of the steps.

Will it? he thought. Come back tomorrow?

I do not know.

Everything that has ever been asked has changed. I said that. Your sister wrote it on the wall.

I did not know if it was true for something that old.

I still do not know. But it said tomorrow.

On the radio at his belt, from Port Abiri, faintly, the old man who liked the sea:

...still pulling. Still heavy. Still holding.

...it's coming. Whatever it is. You can feel it walking along the thread. Very far. Very slow. But walking.

At four in the morning, Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder in the banking hall of the Ossuary one more time.

She did not write what the Collector had said. She had promised to write its answer, and it had not answered. She wrote, instead, at the very top of the wall, above the first column, above HUNDRED-ARROW LIN, above everything, in a space nobody had ever used because the stepladder did not quite reach and she had to stand on the top step on her toes with Lingyue holding her ankles:

THE COLLECTOR. ASKED. NO ANSWER YET. ASK AGAIN TOMORROW.

And under it, in smaller letters, which Seed read through her brother's eyes and found, as it always would now, to be true:

THE ONE WHO PICKS. COMING.
