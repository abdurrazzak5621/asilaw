Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 129The Grain Exchange

czverse9 min read

The thing that came up under the old Grain Exchange was two hundred meters long, and it came up through the building.

Su Mingli saw it from above. She had folded herself onto the roof of the department store across the market street, and she was standing on the parapet with her coat snapping in the wind when the Grain Exchange lifted. All of it. Six stories of a hundred-year-old stone building rose off its foundations like a lid coming off a pot, and hung for a second in the air, and broke apart, and fell, and out of the hole where it had been came a head.

It was a head the way a train is a head. Blunt, plated in overlapping sheets of black glass, wider than the market street, with no eyes that she could see, only rows of pale pits along its flanks that opened and closed like gills. Behind it came the body. Segment after segment, each one as broad as a bus, each one with a pair of glass legs that bit into the cobbles and pulled. It came out of the rift in the ground the way a centipede comes out from under a stone: all at once, too fast, too much of it.

"Level?" she said into the radio, very calm.

"Seed says eighty-four," said Kwan from the Institute. Her voice was shaking. "Lord, near the peak. The Defense Corps calls it a Bridge-Breaker. There's one in the records. From the Black Tide. It took down the Tongji bridge."

The Bridge-Breaker lifted its blunt head out of the ruins of the Grain Exchange and turned it, slowly, like a compass needle finding north.

Toward the river. Toward the Institute. Nine hundred meters away.

"Mingli," said another voice on the radio. Yuanshu. Strained. Far away, at the fountain in Tannery Square. "Don't kill it there."

She had not been told not to kill something since she was nineteen.

"Explain," she said, and did not take her eyes off the beast.

"It's nine hundred meters from the seed. Small embers, my Field wins at that distance. Not that one. That one's a Lord." His breath caught. Somewhere south of her, something was going through him that was not easy to carry. "Seed ran it. If it dies at nine hundred meters, the seed takes a third of it. Maybe half. That's days off the clock. Maybe all of them."

"Then where?"

"Here. Tannery Square. The Spear Corps finishes it. I take the whole ember."

Su Mingli looked down at the Bridge-Breaker.

Two hundred meters of glass and leg. A Lord-grade beast that had broken a bridge in the Black Tide when she was nineteen and cheering on a wall. It had dragged the back half of itself out of the hole now, and its legs were biting into the market street, and it had started to move. Toward the river. Not fast. It did not need to be fast. It was very large.

"You want me to carry it," she said, "two kilometers across the city, through the streets, and put it down in front of you."

"Yes."

"It weighs about forty thousand tonnes."

"Seed says thirty-eight."

She almost laughed. She did not. She took off her coat, and folded it once, and laid it on the parapet, because it was a good coat and she had had it a long time.

"Clear the square," she said.

Yuanshu had stopped counting the embers. Seed had not.

Two hundred and six.

They came downhill through the Field from nineteen crossings, without stopping now. Ashbacks. Glass herons. A pack of things like stilt-legged cats that had come out of the rift under Jinhua Road and run straight into a sergeant's nine and two of Lingyue's students. Each one a small hot grief. Each one running down the slope into him, and through him, and out the cool dark door at the bottom of the Death Law, and gone.

He was on one knee by the dry fountain, with the staff planted on the cobbles and both hands on it, because standing had become complicated. Bai Lu was kneeling behind him with both her small hands flat on his back, and green light was running down his spine into the cobbles, and every few seconds she said, very quietly and very firmly, "Breathe," and he breathed.

Three hundred skeletons stood around them in a ring, facing out.

Two hundred and thirty.

The arrays are flat. Kwan says the line has not moved since 01:58.

Nothing has died on the wrong side.

Yuanshu. You are doing it.

Is Mingli coming?

She is coming.

Look up.

Over the old quarter, the sky folded.

He had seen Su Mingli fold the world many times. A page turn. A lurch. A King stepping through ninety kilometers of space like a woman stepping through a doorway. He had never seen her fold something that was not herself.

It looked, he thought later, like a giant turning a page in a book, and there was a centipede on the page.

The air over the market streets two kilometers north creased, from the ground up into the clouds, a single vast line of light. The whole Bridge-Breaker, all two hundred meters of it, every glass plate and every biting leg, was on one side of the line. Then the line closed, and the creature was not there any more, and the sky over Tannery Square opened, and there it was.

Fifty meters up. Hanging. Upside down.

It did not fall. Su Mingli had it. She stood on the roof of the pharmacy on the north side of the square, in her shirtsleeves, with one hand raised, and forty thousand tonnes of Lord-grade beast hung in the air over the dry fountain as if it were a hill of glass on the black plain of the Wastes, and she was holding it up for Seed to look at.

Its legs thrashed. The pale pits along its flanks opened and shut, opened and shut. Its blunt head swung toward the north, toward the river, toward the call it could still hear, and strained.

She turned it right side up. Slowly. With one hand.

Then she put it down.

The cobbles of Tannery Square cracked in a ring around it. The plane trees bent. Every window in the pharmacy and the bank and the shop that sold phone cases blew inward at once. The Bridge-Breaker lay in the square with its legs bent under the weight of a gravity well that went down through it into the bedrock, pressed flat, all two hundred meters of it, curled around the dry fountain like a cat around a stove.

It could not move. It could barely breathe through its gills.

"Spear Corps," said Su Mingli, from the pharmacy roof. Her voice was perfectly calm. Her nose was bleeding. "It's yours."

Old Seven went first. He always went first.

He walked across the cracked cobbles to the beast's blunt head, with his spear in the hand that had one finger and a thumb, and stood in front of it, and the Bridge-Breaker swung its head toward him and opened the pale pits along its jaw and could not bite, because a King was standing on it from fifty meters away.

He says it is a big one.

He says the big ones break the same way as the small ones. You find the joint. You put the point in.

He says: ankle, knee, hip, spine, shoulder, elbow, wrist.

Seed. The joint.

Third plate behind the head. Underneath. There is a seam where the plates meet. Two centimeters wide.

Feeding it to him. And to them.

He felt it go out of him. Not the way the embers went out the door. The other way. Up through the staff and out across the Field, the shape of a seam two centimeters wide between two plates of black glass, and the shape of Old Seven's Spear Intent, ankle to wrist, into three hundred dead soldiers at once, the way he had fed it across a cemetery on his first night. The army learns as it fights. Three hundred spears came down to the same angle at the same moment, as if one man were holding all of them.

The Spear Corps went in.

They went in under the plates on both sides of the beast, all along its length, three hundred of them, each to the seam Seed had found and fed them, and drove. The Bridge-Breaker screamed. It was a sound like a glacier breaking. It went on for a long breath, and every rusted spearpoint found its seam, and Old Seven, at the head, put his own spear in under the third plate with a single thrust that a lifetime of practice had made.

The scream stopped.

The ember was enormous.

He felt it come loose from the body the way you feel a dam give. Not a small hot grief. A flood. A great old furious thing that had lived in the dark between the rifts for longer than Lingzhou had stood, and had been called out by a pulse it did not understand, and had walked toward the call because it could not do anything else, and had died in a strange square under a crane made of bronze.

It ran downhill.

Into the Field. Down the slope. Into him.

He did not remember falling. He was on his back on the cracked cobbles, with the staff across his chest and Bai Lu's hands on his face and Old Seven standing over him, and the ember was going through him like a river in spate, too big, too much, tearing at the sides of the cool dark door.

Hold the door.

Yuanshu. Hold the door open. Do not close it. If you close it, it stops in you.

I have you. Hold it.

"I've got the door," said Jiang Lan's voice, very sharp and very close, from the white skull on his chest. "I've been holding doors since before your grandmother was born. Breathe. Let it go through. Don't fight it. It's frightened. So are you. Let it be frightened on its way out."

He held the door.

It went.

It took a long time. Long enough that the sky over the old quarter went from black to the dull gray of the hour before dawn. Long enough that Bai Lu's green light flickered and went out and she put her forehead on his shoulder and kept her hands on his face anyway. And then it was through, all of it, down the river, wherever old beasts went, and he lay on the cobbles of Tannery Square and breathed.

Four hundred and eleven embers through the door tonight. One of them very large.

The arrays in the chamber did not move. Not once.

Yuanshu, the seed has eaten nothing since 01:58.

On the pharmacy roof, Su Mingli had sat down on the edge with her legs over the side, in her shirtsleeves, with a handkerchief pressed to her nose. She looked down at him across the ruined square.

"You owe me a coat," she said. "I left it on the department store."

Lingyue's voice came over the radio at 05:12.

"Crossing Fifteen," she said. She was breathing hard. "East side. Toward the cemetery." A pause. "They've stopped coming toward the river. All of them. The last wave, from the two rifts by the old canal. They turned."

He sat up. The world tilted. Bai Lu caught his arm.

"Turned where?"

"Up the hill," said Lingyue. "The War Memorial Cemetery. There are three hundred of them, maybe more. They're going up through the graves." A pause, and her voice changed. "Yuanshu. Why would they go there? There's nothing there for them. The seed's the other way."

He knew why.

The Spear Corps were standing around him in Tannery Square, three hundred of them, with their spears still wet. Old Seven was standing over him. And on the hill above the river, at the top of the War Memorial Cemetery, three kilometers away, inside the edge of his Field, there was one living man who had said he would stand next to an empty stone when it came, and was deaf when he wanted to be.

The beasts had not turned toward something that called them.

They had turned toward the only warm thing left on the hill.

The pulse is over. The call has stopped. They are not being called any more.

They are only hungry.

Grandpa Xu is at No. 7. He is alone.
