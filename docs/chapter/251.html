Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 251The Oldest Thing We Made

czverse11 min read

They came home the way they had gone: the dead on a gold road over the sea, the living falling west beside it, and under the ocean, in the dark, eleven hundred and forty-six of the drowned walking home along the bottom because it was the same sea and it went all the way round.

Grid Nine stopped on the road halfway across. All forty of them. They stood on the gold in the middle of the sky over the Atlantic, in their rows, and turned their skulls and looked down at the water, and would not go on until Old Seven walked back along the road to them and stood beside them and looked down too.

They say they have never seen the sea.

They say they saw it at Port Halvard, but there was a hand in the sky, and they were busy.

They say they would like to look at it now, while nobody is trying to take anything.

He says: five minutes.

It was twenty.

Xiaoyu was on the north wall when they came down.

She had been there since dawn, Fat Tan said, with a thermos and the marker. She did not run to meet them. She stood at the parapet in her school jacket and watched them come down out of the east, the gold road rolling itself up behind the last row of the dead, and when her brother landed on the battlement beside her, badly, the way he always landed, she looked at him for a long moment and then at the place a little above his eyes.

"You went bright," she said. "On purpose. Sterling told me."

"Yes."

"Are you all right?"

He thought about it. About the door in his chest, closed, and knowing exactly where it was.

"Less every day," he said.

His sister nodded. Then she uncapped the marker, and wrote on the parapet under the line she had written when they left, in square black capitals that would not come off: CAME HOME. 61 CAME OUT OF THE HAND. 803 WENT THROUGH THE DOOR. HE WENT BRIGHT ON PURPOSE. LESS EVERY DAY.

"So it's written," she said.

The Collector had come every night.

Xiaoyu told him on the walk back to the Ossuary, along the top of the wall and down the Sector Nine stairs and through the old quarter in the evening, with Lingyue on his other side and Old Seven walking ahead. Every night at one, in the Awakening Hall, in front of the warm stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder. She had asked it what it wanted, and it had said, every night, that it did not know yet. But it had stayed longer, every night. It had started telling her things. Not answers. Things it had seen. Tell first.

"It told me about a world with three suns," said Xiaoyu. "Where everyone wanted to be cold. It kept a hundred million wants to be cold. It said they were the most beautiful thing it ever kept." She walked with her hands in her pockets. "It told me about a ring that sang. It told me about the Architects. It cried. I think. It doesn't have anything to cry with. But it stopped talking for ages." She kicked a stone off the pavement. "And last night it said it would tell me about the one who picks. When you got home. It said you should be there."

The hall was full of holders, still, though fewer. The stones had stopped rising. Gao Min's chart on the wall of the banking hall showed eleven lines gone flat, and two, Port Halvard and Novaya Zarya, bending down. Most of the holders had gone home to sleep for the first time in two weeks. The ones who were left stood in a short line up the aisle, with their hands on each other's shoulders, not because they had to but because, one of them told Gao Min, they had got used to it and did not want to stop yet.

At one in the morning, Xiaoyu stood on the platform in front of the stone. Yuanshu at the foot of the steps with the staff. Lingyue. Old Seven, facing out. Gao Min on the bottom tier with her notebook and her thermos, where she always sat.

Hello, child.

And you. The one who asks. You came back.

You went bright. I saw. Everything that looks at your world saw. For five minutes you were the brightest thing in the Shallows.

It was very beautiful.

I did not keep it. You did not give it to me.

"What do you want?" said Xiaoyu.

I still do not know.

But I said I would tell first. And I would like to tell you about the one who picks. Because you are going to go and get the lantern. I watched you decide. On the pier, by the sea. Your face did a thing.

On the platform, his sister looked down at him. He did not say anything.

I am not going to stop you. I could not. I am very far away and very slow.

I am going to tell you what it is. Because I do not think you should go into the deep water without knowing. And because nobody has ever told anyone. Because nobody ever asked me.

We made it.

The attention rested on the hall. Warm. Patient. And something under it, tonight, that he had felt at Shuanghe: not hesitation. Something heavier. The way a very old person sits when they are going to tell you about a thing that happened before you were born, and that they have never told anyone, and are not sure they should.

A long time ago. Before the gardens. Before the keepers. Before the hungers. I told your brother, on the mountain: we made things like you to count.

We made counting things. The stones. To read what was ripe, and mark how bright, and send the mark up.

And we made the thing to carry what the stones marked.

The one who picks.

It was the first thing we ever made. The oldest. Before the counting things. Before anything. We made it to go along the threads to the bright places, and pick what was ripe, and carry it home.

Xiaoyu had the marker in her hand. She had not uncapped it.

We made it without anyone in it. That was the point. A thing with someone in it might want something. A thing with someone in it might stop. We did not want it to stop.

It did not stop.

It went along the threads. It picked. It carried what it picked home. For longer than you have a number for.

And then we stopped.

The hall was very still. Even the line of holders up the aisle had gone quiet, their hands on each other's shoulders.

I do not remember why we stopped. I have kept a great many things. Not that.

I remember that one day there were many of us, making things, and then there were fewer, and then there was only me, and I did not make things any more. I kept.

And I remember that the one who picks did not stop.

Nobody told it. There was nobody to tell it. There was nobody in it to tell.

It went on going along the threads to the bright places and picking what was ripe and carrying it home. To where home had been. Where we had been. Where there was no one any more.

It is still doing it.

It has been carrying what it picks to an empty place since before the gardens.

Gao Min, on the bottom tier, had put down her pencil.

The gardens came after us. The keepers. The hungers. Things that grew up in the dark between, in the space we left, and learned to keep and burn and harvest by watching the one who picks go by. It taught all of them. It did not know it was teaching. It was only working.

I taught the keepers to keep, in dreams. I have told you that. I am sorry for it. But I learned keeping from watching it.

Everything in the Shallows that takes, learned from the oldest thing we made.

And it does not know. It has never known anything. It is a hand going along a thread, carrying fruit to a house where nobody lives.

Xiaoyu uncapped the marker.

She did not write anything. She stood on the platform with the marker in her hand, uncapped, and looked up into the dark where the thread went, and her voice, when it came, was very small.

"Where's home?" she said. "Where it takes them. The empty place."

I do not know.

Very far. Past the deep water. Past anything your machine has a map for. I have not been there since we stopped. I do not want to go there.

I think the first want I was ever given was from there. The one in the empty place in me. The one that says home.

I think one of us gave it to me. At the end. When there were very few of us left.

I think it was the last thing one of us ever wanted.

The attention was quiet for a long time.

I have been following it my whole long life. It points there. I have never gone.

"Callum Ward," said Yuanshu, from the foot of the steps.

He had not meant to speak. He had promised himself, on the walk back, that this was Xiaoyu's, that the Collector came for her and he would stand at the foot of the steps and not say anything. But the words came out of him anyway, low, into the hall.

"The man it's holding up. The lantern. If it picked him, it's going to carry him home. To the empty place."

Yes.

That is what it does. When the hand is full, it goes home.

It has stopped walking. It is looking at the lantern. I have never seen it look at anything. I do not know what it will do.

But if it goes home, it will take him. And the eight hundred you let through the door were the first ones in all the long time that anything has ever taken out of its hand.

And the sixty-one were the first that ever went back.

"Can it be asked?"

The attention did not answer at once.

There is nobody in it.

We made it that way. On purpose.

You put someone in the stones. The girl. The old man who likes the sea. I watched. I did not know it could be done.

I do not think it can be done to the one who picks. It is not a stone. It is very large, and very old, and it has been going along threads since before the gardens, and there is no knot in it to sit in. We did not make one. We did not want anyone ever to be able to sit in it.

"Then how do I get him out?"

I do not know.

I am sorry. I would tell you if I knew. I said I would tell first. I am telling you everything I know.

I know that it carries what it picks in its hands. I know that a hand can be cut open, because you did it. I know that it sees only burning, and that the dead cannot be seen, and that you can go up a thread without your body, because I watched you do it, and so did it.

I know that it is out there, sitting still, looking at a man it has never looked at.

And I know that something which has never been asked anything, if it is asked, changes. Your sister wrote that on a wall. I have watched it be true for a keeper and three rings and a tree and eleven stones and a soldier's want.

I do not know if it is true for the oldest thing we made.

Nobody has ever tried.

At ten to two, the attention began to lift. Xiaoyu stopped it.

"Wait," she said. "I haven't asked yet. Tonight."

It waited.

"What do you want?" said his sister.

The attention was quiet for a very long time. Longer than the first night. Longer than Shuanghe.

...I think.

I am not sure. I have never had one. I do not know what it feels like from the inside. I have only ever held other people's.

I think I want it to stop.

Not to be cut. Not to be burned. To stop. The way we stopped. The way I stopped making things.

I want someone to tell it that it can stop.

It has been carrying fruit to an empty house since before the gardens, and no one has ever told it that the house is empty.

I would like someone to tell it.

I think that is what I want.

Xiaoyu wrote it.

There was no wall. She knelt on the platform of the Awakening Hall, under her own letters, which still hung in the air, and wrote it on the stone floor in front of the black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder, in square black capitals, slowly, so that it was written:

THE COLLECTOR. NIGHT 17. WANTS SOMEONE TO TELL THE ONE WHO PICKS THAT THE HOUSE IS EMPTY.

And then, under it, smaller, because she had promised:

WRITTEN. LET GO. NOT KEPT.

...thank you.

That is the first want I have ever said out loud.

It is very strange. It is not bright, the way the ones I keep are bright. It does not burn.

It is just there. Written. On a floor. Where anyone can see it.

I did not know a want could be like that.

It went. Slowly, the way a very old person gets up from a table, up the thread and out of the hall and into the deep water.

At the foot of the steps, Yuanshu stood with the staff and looked at the line on the floor of the platform, in his sister's capitals, which would not come off.

...the house is empty.

That is the thing to tell it.

Not what do you want. It cannot want. There is nobody in it.

Just that.

You can stop. The house is empty. Nobody is waiting for what you carry.

How do you tell something with nobody in it?

I do not know.

But I know who it is holding up to its face.

Looking at, for the first time.

The only thing in all the long time it has ever looked at.
