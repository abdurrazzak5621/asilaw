Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 112Sorry, Out Loud

czverse8 min read

The seven Architects asked to go to Port Abiri first.

Not to Concordia. Not to the Council. Not to the five Kings, or the cameras, or the crater where the Charter had been signed. They had a very long list of worlds to apologize to, they said, and almost all of them were gray husks with no one left to hear. This was the first world on the list where someone was still alive.

And of all the places on it, Port Abiri was the one the Hunger had bitten.

They asked Ifeoma Adeyemi for permission. Through Yuanshu. Through the staff. Through Seed. Three translations deep, from a voice like stone grinding on stone to a thin dry voice in a palace by the southern sea.

The oldest King on Blue Star was silent on the line long enough that Yuanshu checked the connection.

"Bring them," she said at last. "I want to see their faces when they say it."

They did not have faces. Not really. Long narrow heads, smooth, with a single dark seam down the front of each, like a closed eye. When the seams opened, a soft gray light shone out. That was the closest thing they had.

They came down out of a Council transport plane onto the airstrip on the northern coast of the Southern Compact on a hot bright morning, unfolding their long thin limbs at too many joints, ducking under the door frame. Seven tall angular shapes in gray robes the color of ash, standing on the tarmac under a sky so blue it hurt.

The ground crew stared. One of them crossed herself. One of them dropped a clipboard.

The Architects did not look at anyone. They stood in a row, with the seams in their faces closed, and waited to be told where to go.

Yuanshu walked them down the coast road himself. With the staff. With Old Seven behind him, and Lingyue beside him with her sword across her back, and Bai Lu with her medical bag, which no one had yet been allowed to carry.

Two hours on foot. The Architects did not want the trucks.

The town was not empty anymore.

Three months after the bite, the people of Kesse had come back up the coast to Port Abiri. Not to live. Not yet. To tend. The white houses with blue shutters had been washed. The streets swept. The painted boats turned over on the beach and scraped and repainted, red and yellow and blue, so bright they hurt the eyes.

And the dead were gone from the streets.

They were on the hillside above the town. In a field that had been a pasture. In rows. Three thousand and eleven small white stones, each one carved with a name, each one with a shell set on top of it, the way the people of the coast marked their graves.

The whole of Kesse was there.

Standing on the hillside, among the stones, in their best clothes, in the heat. Fishermen and their wives. Children. Old men leaning on sticks. The people who had stood on the sand all night with lanterns while the dead of Port Abiri spoke to them out of the air.

And at the top of the hill, in a wheelchair, with a green headwrap and a blanket over her knees despite the heat, Ifeoma Adeyemi.

Waiting.

The Architects walked up the hill between the graves.

Slowly. Their long thin legs folding and unfolding. Their gray robes trailing in the grass. The crowd parted for them. Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. A child started to say something and her mother put a hand over her mouth.

At the top of the hill, in front of the old woman in the wheelchair, the seven tall shapes stopped.

And knelt.

All seven. Their long bodies folding all the way down, their knees in the grass, their long smooth heads bowed until the tops of them touched the earth in front of Ifeoma's wheels.

And then, one by one, the seams in their faces opened. Soft gray light, shining out against the grass.

The oldest one spoke.

Not in its own voice. It had asked Seed to give it one. A voice that people here could hear with their ears. And Seed had made it slow and low and plain, a voice like an old man speaking carefully in a language he had learned late in life.

And the people on the hillside heard it in their own language. Every one.

We built the thing that came out of the sea.

Not this one. Its sister. But the same. We built all four. A very long time ago. Before your sun.

We built them to burn minds. Because minds burn brighter than stars. And we wanted to be bright.

We told ourselves the first ones were volunteers. Then we told ourselves the next ones deserved it. Then we stopped telling ourselves anything.

And then our Hungers went out into the Sea, and we could not stop them, and they came here. To your town. Three thousand and eleven. In the time it takes to say a name.

That is ours. We did that.

The oldest one lifted its head from the grass.

The soft gray light in the seam of its face was shaking.

We are sorry.

And then, one by one, the other six lifted their heads. And said it too. In Seed's slow plain borrowed voice, each in turn, down the line.

We are sorry.

We are sorry.

We are sorry.

Seven times.

On a hillside of three thousand and eleven white stones with shells on top, in the hot sun, in front of an old woman in a wheelchair.

Nobody spoke for a very long time.

Then a woman pushed through the crowd at the edge of the graves.

She was perhaps fifty, broad and strong, in a black dress, with a fisherman's hands, cracked and hard. Her face was set like stone. She walked straight up the hill between the white stones without looking at anyone, and stopped in front of the oldest Architect, kneeling in the grass.

She looked down at it.

"My husband," she said. Her voice was flat. "Kojo Mensah. He was mending a net on the beach. He had the needle in his hand when they found him. The needle was still in his hand."

The oldest Architect did not move. The light in its face flickered.

"My son," said the woman. "Kwame. Nineteen. He was on the boat."

The light flickered again.

"I came to Kesse on the night they spoke," she said. "From the air. On the beach. I stood there with a lantern all night. My husband said the key to the boat was under the blue pot. That's all he said. The key to the boat." Her hard cracked hands were shaking at her sides. "My son didn't say anything. He chose quiet. The boy told me. He said my son said he was very tired."

She looked down at the tall gray thing kneeling in the grass in front of her.

"I don't forgive you," said the woman.

The oldest Architect bowed its head. All the way. Until it touched the grass.

...no, it said. ...you should not.

"Say it again," said the woman.

The Architect lifted its head.

...we are sorry.

"Again."

...we are sorry.

"Again," said the fisherman's widow, and her voice cracked down the middle for the first time. "I want to hear it until I believe somebody means it."

And the oldest of the seven Architects, who had built a Hunger before Blue Star's sun was lit, knelt in the grass on a hillside in the Southern Compact, and said it again.

And again.

And again.

It said it for an hour.

The woman stood in front of it the whole time. Not moving. Not speaking. Not crying. Just listening. And around her, slowly, others came. A man whose mother had been asleep in her bed. A girl whose father had been at the card table in the shade. An old fisherman whose whole crew had been on a boat. They came up the hill between the stones and stood beside the widow in her black dress, in a half-circle, in front of the seven kneeling Architects.

And listened.

And the Architects did not stop.

At the end of the hour, Ifeoma Adeyemi raised her thin hand.

The oldest Architect stopped.

The old King looked down at it from her wheelchair with her very bright black eyes.

"In my country," she said, "when someone does a terrible thing, we say sorry is a seed. You plant it. It does not grow in a day. It does not grow in a year. Some seeds never grow at all." She folded her hands on the blanket. "You have planted it. I do not know if it will grow. That is not up to you."

...no.

"Where will you go now?"

...to the next world on our list.

...it is a husk. There is no one there.

...we will say it anyway.

Ifeoma looked at the seven tall gray shapes for a moment longer.

"Come back," she said. "In a year. Say it again. To her." She nodded at the widow in the black dress. "And to me. I will still be alive. I am too stubborn to die while you still owe us."

The oldest Architect bowed its head to the grass one more time.

...yes.

The widow did not say anything else. She turned and walked back down the hill between the white stones. At the bottom, by the stone with KOJO MENSAH carved on it and a shell on top, she stopped. And knelt. And put her hand flat on the stone.

And then she reached into the pocket of her black dress, and took out a small brass key, the kind that starts a boat engine, and set it on top of the stone, beside the shell.

On the long walk back up the coast road, in the evening, the Architects did not speak.

Neither did anyone else.

But when they reached the airstrip, the oldest one stopped on the tarmac, beside the plane, and turned its long head toward Yuanshu.

...she did not forgive us.

"No."

...we did not expect her to.

...but she listened. For an hour. She stood there and listened.

...on the husks, no one listens. We say it to the gray. And nothing comes back.

...today something came back.

It was quiet for a moment.

...it was worse, it said. ...and it was better.

...I do not know how to hold both.

"Nobody does," said Yuanshu. "That's what she was doing. For an hour. Holding both."
