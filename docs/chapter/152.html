Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 152The Crane

czverse10 min read

The ship was called Patience, and it had been painted over twice.

Yuanshu could read the old names under the new one as the white boat came alongside. Under PATIENCE in fresh black letters, the ghost of DEEPWATER RECOVERY 4, and under that, older and fainter, something in a script he did not know. A hundred and forty meters of rust-red hull riding low in a long swell, with a crane on the afterdeck swung out over the water and a cable running down from it, straight down, into the black.

The cable was slack.

It is already on the bottom.

Two thousand nine hundred meters. I can feel the seed through the Field, at the very edge of it. Very faint. Like a coal at the bottom of a well.

It has stopped moving. It has stopped humming.

I do not like that it has stopped humming.

Ifeoma Adeyemi did not open her eyes. She lifted one finger from the blanket, and the sea raised the white boat up the side of the Patience on a smooth hump of water, gently, like a lift, until the rail of the fishing boat was level with the rail of the hauler, and held it there.

"Go on, child," she said. "I'll keep the water still."

There were twenty-three people on the afterdeck, and none of them were working.

They sat in a ring on the steel plates around the base of the crane, on folded tarpaulins and coils of rope, in orange coveralls and life jackets, with their hands in their laps. Deck crew. Engineers. A cook in a white apron. A young man with a bandaged hand. In the middle of the ring, on an upturned bucket, sat a tall gray-bearded man in a captain's cap, with a mug of coffee going cold on the deck by his boot.

They watched three hundred dead soldiers of the Spear Corps climb over the rail of their ship and form up in rows on the deck without a word, and nobody stood.

"Mister Zhou," said the captain. He had an Atlantic accent, flat and northern. "Hendrik Maas. Master of the Patience. You're welcome aboard." He looked at Old Seven, at the rows of rusted spears, at Lingyue stepping over the rail with two swords on her back. "I'd offer coffee. We've only got the one pot."

"You lowered something."

"We did."

"Bring it back up."

"No," said Hendrik Maas, pleasantly.

Lingyue cut the cable.

She did it without asking anyone, the way she had cut a great many things in the last year. One stroke of her own sword, silver-white, through four centimeters of braided steel at the head of the crane. The cable parted with a sound like a gunshot and whipped out over the side and went down into the sea, all three thousand meters of it, in a long hissing rush.

The captain watched it go.

"It was on the bottom," he said mildly. "We'd let go of it an hour ago. The cable was just there for the camera."

Lingyue looked at the empty end of the crane. Then at the captain. Then at Yuanshu.

"You could have said."

"You didn't ask," said Hendrik Maas.

That was a camera cable.

It had a camera on it. Pointed at the seed. On the bottom.

It does not now.

I would like to note, for the record, that I also did not ask.

They had come from Port Halvard. Maas told them all of it, sitting on his bucket with his hands around the cold mug, in the same mild flat voice, the way a man describes a route he has sailed many times.

A Deepwater Recovery hauler, laid up in a yard on the Atlantic coast after the company went bankrupt in the spring, when the Bureau froze the Halcyon accounts. Bought for scrap by a trust nobody had heard of. Crewed by people who had been asked.

"Asked what?" said Yuanshu.

"What we wanted." The captain smiled a little. "You'd know. You started it."

He took a tablet out of the pocket of his coat and held it out.

On the screen, a man in a plain gray robe was sitting on the wooden floor of a small room with paper walls, with his hands folded in his lap. Sixty, perhaps. Thin. Shaven-headed. A gentle, ordinary face, the kind of face that would have been easy to forget, except for the eyes, which were very tired and very kind. Behind him, through a gap in the paper wall, rain fell on a garden.

Kurose Tōya. The Abbot.

"Hendrik," said the Abbot on the screen. "If you are showing this to someone, it is probably the boy. Hello, Mister Zhou." He bowed his head slightly. "Please don't be angry with Hendrik. He was asked. He said yes. He's been carrying the seed in his hold for a month, singing to it at night so it wouldn't be lonely. He has a terrible voice. It didn't mind."

Somebody in the ring of crew laughed, softly.

"I'm not asking you to agree with us. I know you don't. I'm asking you to understand one thing, because you of all people will." The tired eyes looked out of the screen. "We didn't plant this seed. We returned it. It came from that sand. Somebody took it out of its mother and sold it. We bought it back, and carried it home, and put it down where it was born." He paused. "That's all. Everything that happens next is between the seed and the sea."

The screen went dark.

"You put a Hunger seed back into its sand," said Yuanshu.

"Yes."

"Off a coast where three thousand people died a year ago."

"Yes." Maas looked at his cold coffee. "I was there, you know. Not here. Port Halvard. When the sphere came up through the floor. My wife was a nurse at Hearth One. She was in the round room with the old man, the lighthouse keeper, the day he died. She came home and sat in the kitchen and didn't speak for a week." He turned the mug in his hands. "Then she joined. The Abbot's people. She said she'd seen the keeper's field on the news, and the columns of light, and she wanted to go somewhere she'd be held. And then she got sick, in the summer, and she went." He looked up. "And I'd like to go where she went. That's all. I'd like to be kept where she's kept."

"The keeper doesn't keep people any more."

"Not that one. No." Hendrik Maas smiled. "There are others. Further up. The Abbot says so."

There it was. The thing he had been dreading since the gatehouse step on the Western Pass, and it took him four minutes this time instead of a day.

He asked each of them. All twenty-three. Down the ring, one by one, the cook and the engineers and the young man with the bandaged hand. What do you want? And every one of them told him, kindly, the same thing in different words. To stay. To be here when it comes up. To be first.

He told each of them no.

Out loud. In person. With the reason. He crouched down in front of each one on the steel deck so their faces were level, the way he had crouched on a pavement in Lingzhou in front of Fang Lili, and told them: Your yes is yours. This ship is a few kilometers from a town of eleven thousand people who have never been asked. You're in their window. So you're going ashore. Gently. And when it's over, you can go anywhere you like.

Mo Xing folded them. Two at a time, from the deck of the Patience down into the two launches Ifeoma's household had brought out from the harbor, where Bai Lu and Hu Dabao were waiting with blankets. It was only twenty meters, but it was twenty-three people, and his nose bled by the eighth. He did not stop.

Hendrik Maas went last. He stood up off his bucket and put his captain's cap straight and looked at Yuanshu.

"You're quicker at it than they said."

"I've had practice."

"You don't like that."

"No," said Yuanshu. "I don't."

The captain nodded, as if that were the right answer. Then Mo Xing touched his sleeve, and he was gone, and his coffee mug stood alone on the steel deck in the middle of an empty ring of tarpaulins.

Yuanshu.

Come to the rail. Ifeoma is doing something.

The oldest King on Blue Star had opened her eyes.

She sat in her wheelchair on the deck of the white fishing boat, lashed to the rail beside the hull of the Patience, and she was looking straight down into the sea. Her thin hands were not folded any more. They were open on the blanket, palms down, fingers spread, and they were shaking.

He climbed down to her.

The water around the two ships had gone perfectly flat. Not calm. Flat, like glass. A circle of it, two hundred meters wide, with the long swell rolling past at its edges and not one ripple inside it. And under the glass, very deep, so deep that he felt it more than saw it, the whole sea was moving. Down. A long, slow, enormous pull of water toward the bottom, a column of it three kilometers tall.

She is reaching for the sand.

All of it. Through the whole depth.

This is the most Tide Law I have ever measured. It is more than Su Mingli's Space at the river. It is more than anything.

She is trying to lift it. Like she did in the bay. All of it, up out of the dark, and then down somewhere deeper.

"Ifeoma."

"Be quiet, child."

He was quiet.

He felt her find it. Through the Field, through the water, three thousand meters down: a long gray smear across the seabed, kilometers of it, spread out in drifts and ridges where the sea had laid it down a year ago after she threw it there. Not all gone. Not even most. A salvage company had come with a dredge and taken some tonnes of it off the top, and sorted it grain by grain in a laboratory for seven seeds, and left the rest, because nobody on Blue Star had a dredge that could take it all.

And in the middle of the smear, a small warm coal. The seed. Home.

He felt her close her hand on the sand.

And he felt the sand close its hand back.

The flat circle of glass shuddered.

Ifeoma made a small sound. Not pain. Surprise. Her fingers spread wider on the blanket, and the column of water under the ships pulled harder, and harder, and the swell at the edge of the circle broke and slopped over into it in a long low wave that rocked both hulls.

It is holding on.

The sand. It is not loose. It is rooted.

Yuanshu. It has been rooted for a long time. Months. Long before the seed came back.

"Rooted into what?" said Yuanshu. "It's a seabed. There's nothing down there."

Ifeoma Adeyemi let go.

All at once. The pull went out of the sea like breath going out of a body, and the circle of glass broke into ordinary waves, and the white boat dropped half a meter down the side of the Patience and rocked and steadied. The old King sat back in her wheelchair with her eyes closed. Her face had gone gray under its brown. Her hands lay open on the blanket. They were still shaking.

"There is a great deal down there, child," she said.

He waited.

"I have held this sea for a hundred and ninety years." She did not open her eyes. "Do you know how many fishermen it has taken, in that time? From these towns? From this coast? Every year. A boat that doesn't come back. A man who goes over the side in a storm. A boy who swims out too far." Her voice was very dry and very steady. "They don't wash up. Not out here. Not past the shelf. They go down. All the way. And they lie on the bottom in the cold and the dark, where no one has ever gone to ask them anything."

He understood a half-second before she said it.

"A year ago," said Ifeoma Adeyemi, "I took a spire that ate my people. And I threw it as far away from them as I could. Into the deepest water I had." She opened her eyes and looked down at the sea. "I threw it into the middle of my drowned."

...she is right.

I can feel them now. Now that I know to look. Under the sand. Inside it. Through it.

Embers. Very old. Very cold. Hundreds of them. More. The Field only reaches the edge.

The sand has been eating them. Slowly. For a year. One at a time, a little at a time, the way the roots ate the Western Pass. Nobody heard, because nobody was listening down there.

And now its seed has come home.

Under the two ships, three thousand meters down, in the cold and the dark, something began to hum.

Not one note. A great many. Rising out of the seabed in a slow ragged chord, the way voices rise in a church when the first one starts and the rest come in late.

And Ifeoma Adeyemi, who had been alive for two hundred and thirty-one years and had once said that she had learned only one thing about fighting worth knowing, put her shaking hands over her face.
