Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 164Twelve Hundred and Two

czverse8 min read

Yamato came up out of the sea at dawn, green, the way it had the first time, and Lingyue was waiting on the jetty with her arms folded.

"You're two days late," she said.

"The frigate was slow."

"You can fly."

"I can go up," said Yuanshu. "I'm still working on along."

She looked at him for a long moment. At the staff in his hand, and the three hundred dead soldiers of the Spear Corps filing down the gangway of the Adeyemi behind him in their rows, and Old Seven at the rail, and Hu Dabao being helped down the gangway by Bai Lu with a face the color of old porridge. Then back at him.

"You look different," she said.

"I'm a Lord."

"Not that." She frowned. "You look like you slept."

"I didn't."

"Then you look like you're going to." She hit him on the arm, not very hard, and turned toward the car at the end of the jetty, where Shirasagi Ren was standing in her white robes beside a small old car with a cracked windscreen, the same car, he thought, that had driven him up the mountain a year and a half ago with Su Mingli in the back seat and Old Seven's knees against the dashboard. "Come on. You need to see it before he knows you're here."

"He knows I'm here."

"I know," said Lingyue. "I said it anyway. It made me feel better."

Ren drove. Nobody talked. The road went up into the mountains the way it had before, so narrow and so steep that Yuanshu shut his eyes twice, and the cedar forest closed in on either side, dark and wet, and the cloud came down until the trees were gray shapes in it.

He used the time to feel the mountain.

The Field went out from him now nearly ten kilometers. It still surprised him. He had spent a year with a slope of five, and his sense of it had grown up around five the way a hand grows used to a cup, and now the cup was twice as big and he kept reaching for the edge and finding more. It went up the mountain ahead of the car. Into the forest. Up the long path through the black gates, to the clearing at the top, where the cedars were as wide as cars.

And under the clearing, under the hall, under the stone, something hummed.

That is it.

It is not like the others. The others were hungry. They were eating. Even the sea seed, sitting in its heap, was eating the drowned, slowly.

This one is not eating.

It is warm. It is awake. It is not growing. It is listening.

It feels like the scout did, under the Institute. In the round chamber. Before it moved.

Like something holding very still, so as not to miss a sound.

He opened his eyes.

"Ren," he said. "When the Sleepers sat in the hall. Did they feel like that?"

Shirasagi Ren did not take her eyes off the road.

"Yes," she said. "That's why I asked you to come."

There were three thousand four hundred and eighteen of them on the mountain, and they were all in white.

He saw them from the gravel clearing at the bottom of the path, where the road ended. On the slopes above, among the cedars, where the forest had been cut back into old terraces long ago, for tea or rice or nothing anyone remembered. They sat in rows on the terraces in the wet gray morning, on mats and blankets and bare stone, under the dripping trees. Men and women. Old and young. Some of them Yamato people. Many of them not. A great many had come a long way.

Nobody was praying. Nobody was chanting. They sat in the rain with their hands in their laps, facing up the mountain, toward the top of the path, the way people sit outside a hospital room where someone is being born.

"More every day," said Ren quietly. "They come up the road on foot. Some of them have walked from the coast. Some flew here from the Atlantic Federation. There was a family from the Northern Union yesterday with three children." She looked up at the terraces. "They're very polite. They bring their own food. They clean up after themselves. They've swept the path every morning since they came." A pause. "He taught them that. Sweeping."

"Has anyone tried to move them?"

"The prefecture asked them to leave. They said no. Kindly." Ren did not look at him. "There's no town within twenty kilometers. There's nobody behind them. I've read your Article Ten, Mister Zhou. I read it four times." She folded her hands into her sleeves. "They're not in anyone's window."

They went up through the gates.

The same black wood, worn gray at the edges, moss on the crossbeams. The same stone steps, hollowed in the middle by a thousand years of feet. The same falling bird-note, somewhere in the cedars, again and again. Yuanshu walked under them with the staff in his hand and the Field folded over the whole mountain and Old Seven a pace behind him, and the Spear Corps waiting at the bottom in the clearing, because he had asked them to, and because three hundred skeletons walking up through three thousand people in white was not something he wanted to do yet.

The people on the terraces watched him go by. Nobody said anything. A few of them bowed. One old woman, sitting under a cedar with a blanket over her head against the rain, smiled at him the way you smile at a nephew.

He counted the gates without meaning to. It was something to do with his mind. Two hundred. Five hundred. A thousand. They went on up into the cloud.

At the top, he stopped.

Twelve hundred and two.

There were twelve hundred when we came. Ren said one is added every year.

There are two new ones. At the top. The wood is new. Unpainted. Pale.

He looked at them. Two plain gates of new cedar, standing at the top of the path, a step apart, before the clearing. Nobody had painted them. Nobody had carved them. There was a small wooden tablet hung from the crossbeam of the last one, the kind people hang at shrines with a wish written on it.

He turned it over.

There was one line on the back of it, in brush, in a careful, ordinary hand.

For the year the Sleepers stopped keeping watch, and the year somebody else started.

"He put them up," said Ren, behind him. "Last year. Before he left. I didn't know, until I came back." She looked at the tablet. "He swept the gates every morning for forty years. He'd know how to put one up."

The Abbot was sitting on the step of the hall.

The same hall. Small, plain wood, a heavy roof of dark thatch green with moss. A single wooden step up to a single sliding door made of wood and paper. The door was shut. And on the step, in the gray robe of the mountain shrine, with a straw rain hat on his shaved head and his hands folded in his lap, sat a thin man of sixty with a gentle, ordinary face.

He looked up when they came into the clearing.

He did not stand. He did not need to. He looked at Yuanshu with very tired, very kind eyes, and smiled, the small sad smile from the recordings, and said:

"Mister Zhou. You came by sea. I'm glad. I was afraid you'd fly, and miss the gates."

Yuanshu did not answer.

"I'm sorry," said Kurose Tōya. "That was a foolish thing to say. You've just come from the south. From the old Queen of the Tide." He bowed his head, slightly. "I heard. I'm very sorry. I met her once. She told me I was a sentimental fool. She was right."

"She drowned your seed."

"She did." The Abbot did not seem troubled. "She did what she thought was right. So did Hendrik. So did I." He looked up at the gray sky above the clearing. "It doesn't matter now. The word went from the sea to here before the seed died. The way it went from Lingzhou to the sea. We have all of it now. Everything the scout wanted to say." He looked back at Yuanshu. "It isn't going to shout it. You're thinking of the Western Pass. Of poor Zhuo. This isn't that."

"What is it?"

"It's a window," said the Abbot simply. "Not to shout through. To look through." He folded his hands. "Something has wanted to look at this world for a very long time. It's asked me to leave the window open, so that it can see." He smiled. "And it asked me to wait until you came. So that it could see you."

Yuanshu.

He is telling the truth. Or he believes he is. I cannot tell the difference in him. That is the problem with him. There is no difference.

And the seed under the hall.

It is not a voice. He is right. It is not a cup facing the sky.

It is a lens.

"Who asked you?" said Yuanshu.

"The one who keeps." The Abbot looked at him calmly. "You'd call it the gardener. I don't. A gardener pulls things up. This one doesn't pull anything. It only keeps." He was quiet for a moment. "It spoke to me first on this step, in a dream, the night after your keeper refused us. It's spoken to me many times since. It told me where the scout was. It told me about the seeds. The first time, it told me that the old keepers never ask. That they keep everything, and never ask." He looked down at his hands. "Lately it asks. It says it learned. It says it watched your keeper learn, and thought it was beautiful."

"What did it ask?"

"Last week?" The tired kind eyes came up. "It asked me if I knew the way to the world that asks. I said I was standing on it. It said it knew that. It said it had found someone who could point." The Abbot paused. "It said: thank you anyway. It's very polite."

The rain dripped off the edge of the thatch.

Yuanshu stood in the clearing at the top of twelve hundred and two gates and felt the Field go cold, all at once, from the top of the mountain to the sea.

Liang Fu, he thought. Behind the curtain. Who knew the way home.

...yes.

"It wants to see you," said the Abbot. "That's all. It's been looking for the one who asks for a long time. It isn't angry about the keeper, or the field, or the scout. It isn't angry about anything. It only wants to see you, through the window, and ask you a question." He bowed his head. "The same question you ask everyone, Mister Zhou. You'll know it when you hear it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no," said Kurose Tōya. "And I'll sit here on this step until it closes. It isn't mine to open. It isn't yours to shut. It's the seed's, and the sea's, and the word's." He looked up at the sky. "I only sweep the path."
