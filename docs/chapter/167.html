Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 167Under the Gates

czverse10 min read

"There are more of them," said Shirasagi Ren. "I never told you. I never told anyone."

It was the second night. They were walking down the path under the gates in the dark, the two of them, with a paper lantern Ren had lit in the kitchen and Old Seven a few steps behind. The cloud had lifted. Through the gaps between the black crossbeams, overhead, he could see stars, and no moon at all, and in two nights there would be even less.

"More Sleepers?"

"More monks." She did not look at him. She walked the worn stone steps the way you walk stairs in your own house in the dark, without needing the lantern. "You remember what I told you, the first time. Twelve hundred years ago there were many more. When the trouble came, they chose to die in the hall, sitting, so that they could keep watch. Seven were left. The others crumbled."

"I remember."

"Where do you think they went?"

He did not answer. He had not thought about it. It was the kind of thing he usually thought about, and he had not.

"When a Sleeper crumbled," said Ren, "the shrine swept him up. Very carefully. Every grain. And on the first morning of the next year, when they put up the new gate, they buried him under it." She stopped on the step and lifted the lantern and looked up at the gate above them, black wood worn gray, moss on the crossbeam. "Not under every gate. Most of them are empty. But under some of them, there's a monk."

...four hundred and six.

I can feel them. Now that I know to look. Under the path. Very small. Very old. Very still.

They are like the Sleepers were. Not faded. Held. On purpose. Like a breath.

I did not notice them. Twice. I walked over every one of them twice and did not notice. They are so still that the Field went straight past them.

"Four hundred and six," said Yuanshu.

Ren closed her eyes.

"My grandmother told me three hundred," she said. "She said nobody had counted for a long time." She opened them. "Nobody has ever asked them anything. That's the rule in Yamato. You don't ask the dead. You keep them. You sweep their gates. You light their lamps. You don't ask." She looked at him in the lantern light. "You know what I asked you, on the step of the hall, the first time you came up this mountain."

"You asked if they ever ask back."

"And you said always." Her calm face did not change. "I've been thinking about that for a year and a half."

They sat down on the step under the six hundred and twelfth gate, because Ren said there was a monk under it, and he was the oldest one on the path that she knew of, and if she was going to do this she wanted to start with someone who had waited longest.

She set the lantern on the step between them.

"I've never done it," she said. "I'm a King. I've held Spirit Law since I was fourteen. I can walk into a man's mind and sit down in it and he won't know I'm there. I carried a dead Sleeper's voice into three hundred people in a crater on Concordia." She folded her hands into her sleeves. "And I've never once asked a dead person what they want."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to." She looked at him. "He asked me what I wanted. On the steps. The Abbot. Ten days ago. He asked me very kindly, and I didn't have an answer, and he smiled at me the way he used to when I got my characters wrong." She was quiet. "I've been thinking about why. I think it's because I've spent my whole life not asking. Not the dead. Not anyone. Not myself." She looked down at the stone step. "If you never ask, you never find out what you want. You only find out what you're supposed to do."

"So ask."

"Will you help me?"

"I'll sit here," said Yuanshu. "That's all. It's yours."

She did not use the staff. She did not need it. She was a King of Spirit, and the minds of the dead were not so different from the minds of the living, she said afterward; only quieter, and further down, and much more patient. She put one hand flat on the worn stone of the step, the way he put his hands on a grave, and closed her eyes, and went down.

He felt her go through the Field. A small white light, going down through the stone. Very careful. The way you step into a room where someone is sleeping.

For a minute, nothing. Then two.

Then Ren made a sound. Very small. Like someone who has heard their name said in an empty house.

"He's awake," she said, without opening her eyes. "He's been awake the whole time. Twelve hundred years." Her voice was very steady and her hand on the stone was shaking. "He says: you're Shirasagi. The granddaughter. You used to sit on this step and eat rice cakes and drop the crumbs through the crack."

Yuanshu did not say anything.

"He says he liked the crumbs," said Shirasagi Ren, and her calm face broke, all at once, the way ice breaks on a river in spring.

She asked him, when she could.

The way Yuanshu would have. He heard her do it, under her breath, in the old Yamato that Seed had to work to translate: I'm sorry. Nobody ever asked you anything. I'm asking now. What do you want? You don't have to answer.

And the monk under the six hundred and twelfth gate, who had sat down in a hall twelve hundred years ago and stopped eating and stopped drinking and stopped moving so that he could keep watch, and crumbled, and been swept up very carefully, and buried under a gate, and waited, answered her with a question.

"Is it time?"

Ren went very still.

"We have been listening. All of us. Under the path. For twelve hundred years. Since we crumbled, we have not been able to see. Only to listen. But we can hear."

"We heard the seven go quiet. One by one. We heard the hall empty."

"And then we heard a man with a broom. Every morning. Sweeping. Sweeping up the watching our brothers left in the wood. Taking it down into the rock."

"We heard something under the hall begin to hum."

"We could not say anything. Nobody asked."

"Is it time, granddaughter? Is it the trouble? Is the ring coming back?"

She told the monk the truth. Yuanshu heard her do it, sitting on the step beside her in the lantern light with his hands in his lap. Not the ring. Something else. Something very old and very polite, above the sky, that had been looking at Blue Star for a long time and wanted to look closer. A lens under the hall, ground out of twelve hundred years of the Sleepers' watching. The dark of the moon in two nights. A boy who was going to stand in front of it.

The monk under the gate did not answer at once. The lantern guttered and steadied on the step.

"Our brothers' watching."

"He took our brothers' watching, and made it into an eye, and turned it around."

"So that something can look in."

"Yes," said Ren.

"That was not what it was for."

"We sat down in that hall so that the world would see the trouble coming. Not so that the trouble could see the world."

And then, very quietly, in the old Yamato, the monk said something Seed did not translate at first. It hesitated. Yuanshu felt it hesitate.

What did he say?

...he said: then we will watch it back.

Ren asked them all.

Not that night. Through the next day, and the night after that, from the bottom of the path to the top, gate by gate, with the lantern and her hand on the stone. Four hundred and six. Yuanshu did not help. He walked with her and sat on the steps beside her and said nothing, the way she had once knelt behind him in the hall and said nothing while he asked the seven.

They did not all answer the same way. That surprised her, she said afterward. She had thought they would be like the Sleepers, one voice, one will, the way the seven had seemed to her all her life. They were not. They were four hundred and six old men and some old women, it turned out, nuns who had come up the mountain with their brothers in the year of the trouble and sat down beside them. Some of them were tired. Some of them were angry about the broom. One of them asked her, at great length, whether the shrine still made the bean paste the old way, and was very disappointed by the answer.

But every one of them asked her the same question first.

Is it time?

Yes: three hundred and ninety.

They want to watch it back. When the lens opens. They want to take back what was swept out of the wood. They say it belongs to their brothers, and their brothers did not consent to it being made into an eye.

Quiet: sixteen. They are too tired. They have listened for twelve hundred years. They would like to stop.

Ren asked every one of them. She did not tell them what to say. She did not tell them what she wanted them to say.

I think she did not know. I think she found out while she was asking.

On the last night before the dark of the moon, Ren sat on the step under the last gate, the new pale one with the wooden tablet hanging from its crossbeam, and looked up the clearing at the hall.

The Abbot was there, on its step, under his straw hat, with his hands in his lap. He had been there for six days. He looked down the clearing at the two of them sitting under his gate, and lifted one hand, and smiled.

Ren did not lift hers.

"I know what I want now," she said.

Yuanshu waited.

"I want him to come down," said Shirasagi Ren. "Not up. Down. Off that step, and down the mountain, the way he did when the Sleepers went quiet. I want him to be sad, and old, and alone in a small room somewhere, sweeping something that doesn't matter. I want him to live." She turned the lantern a little on the step. "I want it so much that I've been afraid to look at it for a week. Because he doesn't want that. And it's his choice. And I'm not going to take it."

"No."

"So I can't have what I want."

"Probably not."

She looked at him. In the lantern light, her calm face was very tired and very young.

"Is it always like that? When you ask?"

"Mostly," said Yuanshu. "You find out what you want. And then you find out it isn't yours to have." He looked up at the stars between the crossbeams. "And then you do the right thing anyway, and it hurts, and you do it again tomorrow."

"That's terrible."

"Yes."

"Why do you keep doing it?"

He thought about it. About a cemetery at three in the morning, and a skeleton coming up out of the mist, and a boy reading a dead man's notebook by lamplight in a room under a mountain, because nobody had ever asked the man what he wanted written in it.

"Because the other way," he said, "you never find out at all."

Yuanshu.

Twenty-two hours.

Mo Shi says the clock under the hall is getting louder. He says it sounds like a bell being lifted.

The monks under the gates are awake. All three hundred and ninety. They are listening up. They have never listened up before. Only down.

And I have done something you did not ask me to. I am telling you now.

Go on.

I have been practicing.

Not hiding. The opposite. Standing in the open in your head with nothing in front of me. I have never done it on purpose. Every time something big has looked at you, I have been behind something. Old Seven's core. Your mother. Or I was not there at all.

It is very uncomfortable. It is like standing in a field in the rain without a coat.

I am going to do it anyway.

I wanted you to know that I chose it. So that if something goes wrong, you do not carry it.

I'll carry it anyway.

I know.

Ifeoma said you carry things very badly.

She was right. I am telling you so that you know I know, and that I chose it anyway, and that you can stop carrying this one now.

He did not stop. But he laughed, once, out loud, under the last gate at the top of the mountain, and Ren turned and looked at him, and up on the step of the hall the Abbot looked down at both of them with his tired kind eyes, and did not understand what was funny.
