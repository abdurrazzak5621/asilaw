Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 166The Lowest Terrace

czverse10 min read

She sat there all night, and nobody asked her anything until dawn.

Yuanshu watched from the kitchen window until midnight, and then went down himself and sat on the steps under the gates, a long way above her, where he could see the first terrace through the cedars and not be seen. He did not go closer. She had not asked him to. Old Seven came and stood on the step behind him in the dark, and after a while Seed stopped saying that it was watching and simply watched.

The rain stopped at two. The cloud stayed. The people in white sat on the old terraces among the dripping trees with their hands in their laps, and Lingyue sat among them on the wet stone with her two swords on the ground beside her and her arms around her knees, and the old woman with the blanket over her head sat beside her, and neither of them said a word.

At dawn, the old woman said, without turning her head: "You're the girl with the swords. From the television."

"Yes."

"You cut the birds out of the sky. The glass ones. Over the canal in Lingzhou. With all those children holding swords."

"That was two months ago."

"You say it like it was a long time." The old woman pulled her blanket closer. "You're very young for a long time." She looked at Lingyue sideways. "Why are you sitting here?"

"Because you are," said Lingyue.

It went like that, after.

Not quickly. Not with speeches. People on the first terrace had seen her sit down at dusk and still be sitting there at dawn, wet through, not asking anyone anything, and by the middle of the morning, one by one, they started asking her. Why are you here. Are you with the boy. Are you going to make us leave. Did the old Queen really die. What was she like.

She answered all of them. Bluntly. The way she answered everything.

"Yes, I'm with him. No, I'm not going to make you leave. Neither is he, unless you're in someone's way. Yes, she died. She held the sea down for nine hours so that eleven thousand people on a river road could get to the hills, and then she pulled a seed the size of a house out of the bottom of the ocean, and she died doing it, and she kept doing it after she died." She picked at the moss on the stone. "She was rude. She called everyone child. She called me girl. She told me not to be soft."

"Were you?" said the old woman.

"Sometimes."

The old woman's name was Hara Setsuko, and she was eighty-one, and she had come up the mountain because her husband had died in the spring after fifty-eight years and she did not know how to be in a house where he was not. She told Lingyue this at noon, over a rice ball she shared without being asked.

"I want to be with him," she said. "That's all. The Abbot says the one who keeps will keep us together. He says it keeps everything." She looked up the mountain at the hall. "Is that wrong? To want that?"

"No," said Lingyue.

"You're not going to tell me I'm wrong?"

"I don't think you're wrong. I think you want your husband." Lingyue was quiet. "My grandmother died when I was eleven. Rift fever. It took four days. I wanted to go with her for a year." She looked at her hands. "I'm not going to tell you that goes away. It didn't for me. It just got... further down."

Hara Setsuko looked at her, chewing slowly.

"Downhill," she said.

Lingyue turned her head.

"Things go downhill," said the old woman. "Water. Grief. My husband used to say that. He was a carpenter. He said everything settles, if you give it long enough. It goes to the lowest place and sits there." She pulled the blanket closer. "He said that's why you build a house on a hill."

Yuanshu went down at noon to bring her food, and found her sitting with a family from the Northern Union.

A father and a mother, both tall and fair and very tired, in white clothes that did not fit them, bought somewhere for the journey. And three children. A girl of about twelve, sitting very straight. A boy of perhaps nine, curled against his mother's side. And a smaller girl, six, maybe seven, on her father's knee, with a runny nose and a cardboard book about trains.

They came from Kirovsk.

The father's brother was a soldier on Orlov's wall. He died in the spring. The mother's parents died in the winter before. The father says they have lost everyone except each other. He says the Abbot promised that if they are kept, they will be kept together. All five. Forever. Nobody left behind ever again.

He has a paper. The Abbot's people give everyone a paper. The consent. Signed. Witnessed. In the style of Article Three.

He signed for all five.

Yuanshu stopped on the step.

Lingyue was sitting on the wet stone in front of the family with the father's paper in her hands, reading it. She read it twice. Then she folded it and gave it back.

"You signed for them," she said. Not angry. Flat. "For the children."

"I'm their father."

"Yes." She looked at the girl of twelve, sitting very straight. At the boy against his mother's side. At the small one with the train book. "Did anyone ask them?"

The father opened his mouth, and closed it.

"The Abbot asked us," said the mother quietly. "He asked us very carefully. He said a family should stay together."

"He asked you," said Lingyue. "Your yes is yours. I'll defend it to anyone. If you want to stay on this mountain and be kept by whatever's coming, that's your choice." She did not raise her voice. "But you can't say yes for them. Nobody can. That's the whole point of asking. You don't get to answer for someone else's mind."

It was the only time on the mountain he saw her ask anyone anything.

She turned to the girl of twelve. Not to the parents. To the girl. And crouched down on the wet stone so their faces were level, the way he did, which he had not known she had noticed.

"What's your name?"

"Katya."

"Katya. I'm going to ask you something. You don't have to answer. Nobody will be angry if you don't." Lingyue's voice was very steady. "What do you want?"

The girl of twelve looked at her father. At her mother. Then back at Lingyue.

"I want Uncle Misha back," she said. "And I want to go home. To our flat. And I want Papa to stop crying in the bathroom when he thinks we can't hear." Her chin was shaking. "I don't want to be kept. I don't know what it means. Nobody will tell me what it means."

The boy against his mother's side had his eyes shut. He said, into her coat, "I'm cold."

The small one with the train book did not say anything. She was asleep.

Article Ten, Yuanshu thought, standing on the step above them with a bowl of rice in his hands. No consent, however freely given, extends to harm done to those who have not consented.

He had read it in the crater. He had written the sentence it came from. He had applied it to a sit-in on a pavement, and to a major on a spire, and to a sect on a beach, and to an old woman on a hill, whom it did not touch.

He had never once thought about it like this. A father with a paper. Three children who had not been asked.

They are in their parents' window.

The parents can choose for themselves. They cannot choose for the children. The children cannot consent to being kept. They are too young to know what it means, and nobody has told them, and the one who is old enough says no.

This is the clearest case of Article Ten I have ever seen.

And it is the hardest. Because it is a father.

Lingyue stood up.

"The children are going down the mountain," she said. "Today. To the village. Ren's people will look after them." She looked at the father. "That's not a question. I'm telling you, to your face, with the reason. They didn't say yes. Katya said no. The others are too young to say anything. Your yes stops at them."

The father stood up too. He was a head taller than her. His face was gray.

"You can't take my children."

"I'm not taking them. I'm taking them down a hill." She did not step back. "You can come with them. Or you can stay. That's yours. I won't touch it." She looked at the mother. "Either of you. Both of you. But they go."

Nobody moved for a long moment.

On the terrace around them, three thousand people in white had gone quiet, watching. Yuanshu could feel it through the Field: not hostility. Something stranger. A kind of held breath. The Abbot had told them, all of them, many times, that the boy who asked had a limit, and that one day they would see it. And here it was, on the first terrace, in front of a family from Kirovsk. Not the boy. The girl with the swords.

The mother moved first.

She did not look at her husband. She stood up, slowly, with the boy still against her side, and picked up the sleeping one with the train book from her father's knee, and held out her free hand to Katya.

"Come on," she said. "We're going down."

"Lena," said the father.

"I said yes for me, Dmitri." She did not look at him. "I meant it. I still do. I'd still like to be kept. With you. With all of them." Her voice cracked. "But she said no. She's twelve. She said no to my face. I heard her." She held the sleeping child tighter. "I'm not going to sit on a mountain and be kept by something while my daughter walks down it."

She went down the steps. Katya went with her, holding her hand. The boy went with them, wiping his nose on his sleeve.

The father stood on the terrace while the rain began again, in his white clothes that did not fit, with his paper in his hand.

Then he folded the paper very small, and put it in his pocket, and went after them.

Lingyue sat back down on the wet stone beside Hara Setsuko, and picked up her two swords, and laid them across her knees, and looked at nothing.

"That was hard," said the old woman, after a while.

"Yes."

"You did it anyway."

"Yes." Lingyue did not look up. "He would have. Yuanshu. He'd have crouched down in front of her the same way. I've watched him do it a hundred times." She turned her grandfather's sword over on her knees. "I always thought he was being kind. When he crouches. So their faces are level." She was quiet. "He isn't being kind. He's making sure that when he says no, he's not saying it from above them."

Hara Setsuko looked at her for a long time.

Then the old woman reached over and patted her hand, twice, the way you pat the hand of a granddaughter who has done something difficult, and went back to looking up the mountain at the hall.

By dusk, two hundred and nine people had gone down the mountain.

Nobody had moved them. Nobody had told them to. They had watched a family from Kirovsk go down the steps under the gates, a mother carrying a sleeping child and a father following with a paper in his pocket, and some of them had children in villages and cities a long way away, and some of them had parents, and some of them had simply sat on the terrace all afternoon thinking about the girl of twelve who said she did not know what kept meant, and found that they did not either.

Three thousand two hundred and nine stayed.

I have decided.

Yuanshu was on the step under the last gate, the new pale one, at dusk, watching the people go down.

You asked me what I want. I said I needed until tomorrow. It is not tomorrow, but I know.

I watched Katya.

She did not know what kept means. Nobody told her. She said no because nobody would tell her what she was saying yes to.

I do not know what is going to look at you through that lens. Nobody will tell me. If I hide, I am saying yes to not knowing. Forever. I am saying: it can look at you, and I will never know what it saw.

I do not want that.

I want to stand next to you and see it. And if it sees me too, then it sees me.

That is what I want. You can say no. You will not.

No, thought Yuanshu. I won't.

Down on the first terrace, in the dusk, Lingyue was still sitting beside the old woman with the blanket over her head. She had not moved. She did not look up at the hall.

But she lifted one hand, without turning, toward the top of the steps where she knew he was sitting, and held it up for a moment, open, the way you hold up a hand to say I know you're there, and put it down again.
