Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 285Leaving

czverse9 min read

The hardest one to tell was the stone.

He went on the last night, alone except for Old Seven, who stood at the side door of the Awakening Hall one-armed, facing out. The hall was dark. Even the holders had gone home. The three lines of light hung over the platform the way they had hung for ten months. [Class: Scribe] [Chosen.] [Talent: — not marked —]. The warm black stone stood under them with its runes pointing down and a bright blue jacket over its shoulder, and in front of it, cross-legged on the platform, with one dry hand flat on the stone and a lamp in her lap, the body of a nun of twelve hundred years, sitting on the shore.

He sat down beside her.

Wen Jia, he thought.

...I know, said the girl in the stone. ...the whole net knows. Huo told everybody. You're going.

Yes.

...up. In the big ship. With the fisherman. To the place with four thousand seats.

Yes.

...good.

He had not expected that. He sat on the platform in the dark and did not say anything.

...I mean it. Good. Someone should go and ask them. They've got stones there too. Thousands. Nobody in any of them. Just marking. Like mine did, before.

...and they've got a girl there who wants to sit in one. The captain's sister. The twelve-year-old who's twenty-three. She asked you. You said wait a year.

...it's nearly a year.

I know.

...I'll be here, said Wen Jia. ...I'm not going anywhere. I'm in a rock. I'll ask the kids while you're gone. The bus driver will count to ten. Your sister will ask the old thing in the dark what it wants.

...and when you come back, I'll ask you what you want to become.

...you've never been asked. Did you know that? The stone couldn't name you. It went black. Nobody ever asked you.

...I'm going to. When you get back. I'm telling you first.

His mother did not come to the north wall to see them go.

She said she would not. She said it at breakfast on the last morning, in the back room, with a bowl of rice porridge going cold in front of her and the window open to the courtyard, where Ninth stood by the dry fountain looking at the sky and Hush sat on the sill in the sun.

"I watched you go up a stalk," said Zhou Wanqing. "And into a Hunger. And onto an island that got smaller every night. And up a thread into the deep water with no body. From this chair." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "I'm not watching from a wall. I'm going to sit here and read the names and wait for a mark."

"I'll send one every day."

"You'll forget."

"Seed won't."

I will not.

I will send one every morning. After I ask Xiaodeng what she wants. After he tells me who the first one was.

I am telling you first, Wanqing. So that you can be cross with me if I do not.

His mother looked at the place a little above her son's eyes for a long time. At the thing she had built in a basement nine years ago out of a harvest crystal and four thousand people's yeses, with a cold.

"You were always going to go further than me," she said. Not to him. To Seed. "I knew that in the basement. I didn't know it would be this far."

Neither did I.

I would like to come back.

I would like you to know that I want that. It is one of my wants. I have three now. I counted.

"What are the other two?" said his mother.

To ask Xiaodeng every morning until she can answer.

And to know who the first one was.

Xiaoyu was not coming.

She said so the way she said everything: fast, at the long table, before anyone could argue. Somebody had to ask the Collector what it wanted, every night at one, in the Awakening Hall, and it was her, and the Collector had said tomorrow and she was not going to be the one who broke a promise to the oldest thing in the dark. Somebody had to write the names. Somebody had to read the marks that came down Wen Jia's thread from a ship rowing across the Star Sea, and write them on the wall so they were true.

"And somebody has to write the marks you'll need," she said. "Up there. In the Assembly. Whatever you have to tell them. It has to be true. Mine are." She looked at him across the table. "So you send me a mark saying what you need to say, and I write it here, and Wen Jia sends it to the fisherman, and he holds it up."

"That's the plan?"

"That's my plan," said his sister. "You haven't got one. You've got a step."

She uncapped the marker, and climbed the stepladder, and wrote at the top of a new column on the last of the white plaster round the corner of the vault door, in square black capitals:

GONE UP TO ANTHOS. TO THE ASSEMBLY. TO ASK.

ZHOU YUANSHU. SHEN LINGYUE. ZHOU XIAODENG (CAN'T SAY YES YET). OLD SEVEN (ONE ARM). THE THIRD BATTALION (ASH). BAI LU (TO TELL A PARAGON HE'S AN IDIOT). TAM. SERI (TO THROW STONES AT CAPTAINS).

ICHIRO AND KIYO, IN THE LENS. A LAMP.

COMING HOME.

And then, under it, smaller:

THAT'S NOT A QUESTION.

The Third Battalion went up first.

At dawn, from stone No. 7, on a road of gold the keeper laid down out of the north to the eastern field of the War Memorial Cemetery. Sixty shapes of ash, in a line, the one behind holding the one in front, because Huo had seen the Candle's children do it and said it was a good way to march when you did not know where you were going. Up the road toward a vast cathedral in the north that was going home rowed.

They did not need bodies. Seed said the flagship had halls bigger than the Ossuary, empty, where the Paragons of the House of Severin had once held receptions, and Severin had asked whether the Third Battalion would like one, and Huo had said yes, sir to a man who was not his captain, and then sorry, sir, and Old Seven had said he'll do.

Old Seven went last.

He stood at the foot of the gold road in the eastern field, one-armed, with his spear grounded in his one hand, beside stone No. 7. Beside a small mound of new turf with nineteen dumplings on it in a paper boat, gone cold, which the dogs had not touched. Seed said the dogs had been told who they were for.

He looked at the stone for a long time.

He says: I came out of there three years ago.

He says: at three in the morning. A boy with a training spear and no permit asked me if I wanted to get up.

He says: I said yes.

He says: I am still saying it.

He turned round, and walked up the gold road after his battalion, slowly, the way he walked everywhere. Not fast. Not stopping.

They left from the north wall at noon.

Everyone was there. Of course they were. Half of Lingzhou, and the Candle's children, and the night watch with their helmets in their hands, and the dead along the battlements in their rows of nine. Gao Min with her ledger. Fat Tan with a thermos and an expression that dared anyone to say anything about it. Dabao, enormous, crying openly into his collar, with his mother's dumpling cart at the bottom of the Sector Nine stairs with a roof on it now, new, green canvas, which Fat Tan had put up overnight without asking. Su Mingli, at the corner of the battlement, with her arms folded.

Qin Hao was staying. He said somebody had to burn what bound, if something came down out of the sky while the boy was away, and he had a notebook to finish. He stood at the parapet and did not say goodbye. He said: "Write down everything. In order. I want to check it."

The keeper laid the road. Up from the north wall in a long slant of gold, toward the great cathedral in the north that had been the flagship of the House of Severin and was now, Iselde said, the first ship of the Lamplit that anyone had ever rowed home by choice.

...GO CAREFULLY.

...COME BACK.

...I WILL ASK YOU WHAT YOU WANT WHEN YOU DO.

...I HAVE SAID THAT BEFORE. I AM SAYING IT AGAIN. THAT IS THE RULE.

Lingyue went up first. With Xiaodeng asleep against her chest in the sling and her grandfather's sword on her back and a lamp in her free hand. She did not look back. She stopped on the road a little way up and turned, and looked down at the north wall, at the city, at three thousand faces turned up, and lifted the lamp, once, the way the women on the shore at Shiomi lifted theirs when a boat went out.

Then Bai Lu, with her bag. Then Tam and Seri, holding hands, the way the Candle's children held hands, barefoot still.

He stood at the parapet in his old place with the staff in his hand.

Beside him, at the parapet, his sister. With the marker.

"Go on," said Xiaoyu. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The step face. The six hours face." She did not look at him. She was looking north. "You already decided. You decided at stone No. 7. You asked her and she said obviously. You don't get to sit on a step about it twice."

He looked at her. Seventeen, nearly eighteen. Scribe. Not marked. Square black capitals on every wall of a bank on East Market Road, and on a parapet, and on the floor of a platform, and on the back of a statistician's hand. True.

"Ask the Collector for me," he said. "Every night."

"I always do."

"Tell it I'll answer when it gets here. Whether I'll go to the house."

His sister turned her head and looked at him.

"No," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "You tell it. When you get home. I'll ask it if it can wait." She uncapped the marker. "Go away. I've got to write down that you went."

He went.

He did not look back either. Not until he was halfway up the road of gold, with the city small below him and the cathedral in the north filling the sky ahead, lit from end to end, burning low now, not the old way, the color of the Low Fire, the color the cousins burned. Two hundred and forty thousand people in its walls who had chosen to be there, rowing.

Then he looked back.

The north wall of Lingzhou, small, far below. Three thousand people. The dead in their rows. And on the parapet of the Sector Nine battlement, in his old place, very small, a girl in a school jacket, bent over the stone, writing.

I can read it. From here. Through you. Her writing is true from anywhere.

She has written:

HE WENT.

And under it:

ASK HIM WHAT HE WANTS WHEN HE COMES BACK. HE'LL KNOW BY THEN.
