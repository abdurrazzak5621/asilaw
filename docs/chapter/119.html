Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 119Something Loud

czverse8 min read

Mama?

He did not say it. He could not have said it. He was in the middle of a fire with a burning old man in one hand and a staff in the other and threads of himself being pulled out of him one at a time. It was not a word. It was just there, under everything, the way a heartbeat is there.

"Not quite," said the voice in the staff. "A little piece of her. The piece that stayed behind. In the field. When the keeper's door closed and your teacher carried us down." A pause. "She was so tired, Yuanshu. She wanted to come home so badly. She left the tired part here. In the quiet. With Jiang Lan."

"She snores," said Jiang Lan, very dryly, very strained. "I've had a year of it."

"I don't snore."

"You do."

He would have laughed. In any other place. He could not breathe.

Yuanshu. Listen to her.

I know what she is going to do.

Listen.

"It's hungry," said the piece of his mother. Quick. Sharp. The voice she used to explain things when she had to leave for the Institute in five minutes. "It's a seed. It's never fed before. It doesn't know how. It sees the brightest thing and lunges at it. Like a baby grabbing."

The fire pulled. Another thread. Xiaoyu laughing so hard she had to sit on the floor.

"In the field," said Wanqing, "I went loud. Everything at once. So the keeper would turn and look at me. So you could get away." A pause. "And then I went quiet again. All the way. So fast it couldn't find me. Seventy-one days it squeezed and it couldn't find me."

I know.

"I'm going to do it again. Both halves. Loud. Then quiet." The voice in the staff was perfectly calm. "It'll lunge at me. With everything. Because I'll be the brightest thing it's ever seen. Brighter than the staff. Brighter than you."

And then?

"And then I won't be there," said Zhou Wanqing. "And it'll have thrown everything it has at nothing. Like a fist punching a hole in fog. And for one second, it won't be pulling at you at all." A pause. "One second. That's all you'll get. Pull him out. Pull yourself out. Don't wait for me."

What happens to you?

"I don't know," said his mother's voice. "I've never done it this close to anything this hungry."

He held the staff. He held the old man. He held on.

No, he thought.

"Yuanshu."

You can say no. That's how it works. You can say no, and I'll find another way.

"There isn't another way. You know there isn't. You're losing. I can feel it. So can your teacher. So can Seed." The voice softened. Just a little. "And I'm not saying no. I'm saying yes. You're asking me, and I'm saying yes. That's how it works."

He could not answer.

"Seed," said Wanqing.

...yes. Mama.

"When I go loud, hold him. Don't let him follow me."

...yes.

"Jiang Lan."

"I heard," said the schoolteacher. "You're as stubborn as your son. Go on, then. I'll hold the door."

Wait. Yuanshu. I have been measuring it.

Eleven minutes of pull. It pulls in beats, four to the second. When it lunges at her it will throw every beat at once, and then it has to draw back. My model says that takes nine-tenths of a second. Not one.

And the door is slow with a living mind in it. Jiang Lan needs a second and a half to carry you through. I ran it nine thousand times. In every run you are still inside when the pull comes back.

The answer arrived the way answers used to arrive in the exam hall, already finished.

Then we don't wait for the gap, he thought. We start now. Against the pull. We do most of the distance while it's still holding us, and use the gap for the last step.

...it will take more of you. Every second you move against it, it takes a thread.

How many?

Four. Perhaps five.

Then let it. He tightened his grip on the old man. Mister Ashby. When I move, move with me. Don't stop until I say.

...right, son.

They started to move. Slowly. Against the whole hungry weight of it, toward the door Jiang Lan was holding. A thread went: a bus window in the rain, the year he was twelve. Another: the taste of exam-hall tea. He kept going. So did the old man.

We are almost at the door. Now, Mama.

His mother went loud.

He felt it the way he had felt it in the field. Like a bell struck inside a mountain. Everything at once. Seven years in the dark and a year asleep in the bones of a schoolteacher. A rice cooker. A green flask with a cracked lid. A daughter snoring in a chair. A son on a table in a crater with his face turned up to the rain. Every morning she had hummed four notes over breakfast. Every night she had not been there.

All of it. At once. Blazing up out of the small white skull in his hand like a struck match in a dark room full of gas.

The fire turned.

The whole hungry weight of it. All at once. Away from him. Away from the old man. Toward the blazing thing in the staff, the brightest thing it had ever seen, brighter than ninety million names, brighter than anything.

It lunged.

And she was gone.

Not burned. Not taken. Gone. Quiet. All the way down. In an instant. Like a candle pinched between two fingers. Like a light switched off in a house where someone had been standing in the window a second ago and now was not.

The fire hit nothing.

It had thrown everything it had at a blazing light, and the light was not there, and for one second, one, the whole hungry gray-red weight of it was flung out into empty space, overreaching, off balance, like a fist that has missed its target and keeps going.

For one second, it was not pulling at him at all.

NOW.

He pulled.

Not with his hands. With everything. With the staff. With Jiang Lan, holding the door open with every bone she had. With Seed, holding him. With the old man in his other hand, who was not crying anymore, who had his enormous knotted fingers wrapped around Yuanshu's wrist and was pulling too.

They came out of the fire the way you come up out of deep water. Fast. Gasping. Through the door. Through the staff. Out.

He opened his eyes in the chair.

The round room was wrecked. Rain pouring in through the shattered glass dome. Gray roots everywhere, through the floor, through the walls, most of them cut and shriveled and smoking. Roman Sterling standing in the middle of it all with his chest heaving and gray splinters in his beard. Lingyue in front of the chair with her sword lit, white-gold, her face streaked with rain and something else.

And on the pedestal, the sphere. Three meters across now. Jagged. Cracked. Its great red eye open.

Staring at nothing.

Confused. Lunging, still, at the place where something blazing had been a second ago. Its roots twitching. Its light flickering.

It does not know where we went.

It does not know where she went.

It is empty, Yuanshu. There is nothing in it now. Nothing to burn.

And it is still hungry.

"Lingyue," he said. His voice came out as a croak. Blood in his mouth. "Pale fire. Now. Nothing's inside it."

She did not ask. She held the sword out over his knees. He touched the small white skull to the blade.

The white-gold went silver-gray.

Old Seven was already moving.

He crossed the wrecked floor in two strides, the stumps of two burned fingers on his three-fingered hand smoking, and went up the side of the sphere the way he had gone up the side of the spire at Port Abiri. Bone on jagged gray crystal. To a crack halfway up. And drove his spear into it.

Not gently, this time.

"Sterling!" Lingyue's voice. "Hold it still!"

The King of the Atlantic Federation raised both hands. And the air around the sphere locked. Not crushing. Holding. Force from every direction at once, pinning the jagged thing on its pedestal so it could not twitch, could not lunge, could not grow.

And Shen Lingyue walked up to the sphere with a sword of pale fire, and drove it into the same crack as Old Seven's spear, all the way to the hilt.

It did not explode.

It did not scream. There was nothing in it to scream.

The pale fire went into the crack and spread. Silver-gray. Cold. Steady. Through every crack in the cloudy gray crystal, the way frost spreads across a window on a winter night. And where it went, the red died. The glow went out of the cracks. The great eye dimmed, and dimmed, and went dark.

And the sphere went gray. All of it. From the inside out.

And then, slowly, softly, the way the spire in the bay had gone, it crumbled. Into sand. Gray. Fine. Pouring down off the pedestal in long soft rivers, hissing on the wet stone floor.

Old Seven came down with it. He landed in the gray sand in a crouch, and stood, and pulled his spear out of the heap. And looked at his hand.

One finger left. And the thumb.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he closed it around the spear shaft, and grounded the spear, and stood beside the chair, facing the door.

Outside, on the headland, the lighthouse was dark.

Down the hill, the lights of Port Halvard were dark. All of them. The hospital. The children's ward.

The rain came down through the broken dome.

And in Yuanshu's lap, on the white staff, something small and bright and warm was sitting. Not burning. Just there. A little gold light, the size of a candle flame, resting on the small white skull with its silver reading glasses.

...son.

...you came back for me.

...like you promised.

"Mister Ashby," said Yuanshu.

He could not say anything else.

...I can't see the light anymore.

...that's all right. I saw it. From inside. I saw it go round. Over her window.

...that's enough.

...is Ellie all right?

"She's all right," said Lingyue, from beside the chair. "The Secretary carried her out."

...good.

...good.

...son. I'd like to say something to her. Just once. Then I'd like to be quiet.

...if that's allowed.

"It's allowed," said Yuanshu. "It's always allowed."

He did not ask the staff about his mother.

He could not. Not yet.

He sat in the wrecked round room with the rain coming down on him and a small gold light in his lap, and he held the staff against his chest, and he listened.

Nothing, at first.

Then, very faintly, from somewhere very deep in the bones of a schoolteacher, under ninety million names, a sound.

Not a word. Not a voice.

Snoring.

Very quiet. Very tired. Very, very much there.

"Told you," said Jiang Lan.
