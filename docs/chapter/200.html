Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 200The Road North

czverse8 min read

At dawn, the keeper laid six roads.

Not one. Six. Out of the small white ring over the end of the world, down the gray morning sky, across Blue Star, the way threads had once come down out of a lens to cut a field. Gold. Wide enough for nine to walk abreast. Laid across the morning like bridges of warm light, from the end of the world to every place on Blue Star where the dead had said yes.

One came down to the Western Pass.

General Qin Zhenyue was standing on the first terrace when it touched the mountain, in his greatcoat, with his one hand behind his back and his cap on, beside his son's stone in row two hundred and twelve. Around him, in their rows among the forty-one thousand three hundred and six small gray stones, twenty-five thousand dead soldiers of the Western Pass stood in the frost, banked to steady gray. At the gate, a thousand who had lain down a year ago and stood back up when they were asked again, holding the pass. And at the front, at the foot of the gold road, a short broad skeleton with a sergeant's chevrons and a pick over his shoulder, counting.

Sergeant Ma Dashan looked back at the old general once.

He says: we'll bring them home, sir.

"I know," said General Qin. "That's not yes. That's what you want." He did not move his hand from his son's stone. "Go and get it anyway."

The Western Legion went up the road into the sky.

One came down to the sea off Kesse.

Akosua Mensah saw it from the hill above Port Abiri, sitting on the grass beside two stones with shells on them, with her basket. A road of gold coming down out of the north into the water two kilometers offshore, past the point, where the bay turned dark. And up it, out of the sea, streaming water, forty shapes. Fishermen. Sailors. A harbor pilot's chosen forty, who knew how to watch the water in the dark.

They walked up the road into the morning, dripping, and the light went through them.

Akosua Mensah did not stand up. She lifted one cracked fisherman's hand off her knee, and held it up, open, the way you hold up a hand to a boat going out, and put it down again.

One came down to a mountain in Yamato, and did not touch it.

It stopped above the top of the path, above twelve hundred and three gates, a meter over the new pale one with its tablet, and hung there in the dawn, gold, and did not come down. Nobody on the mountain was going. Nobody had said they would.

It came anyway. So that they could see it.

Shirasagi Ren stood under the gate with her hands in her sleeves and looked up at it. Beside her, a thin old man in a gray robe leaned on a bamboo broom.

"What is it?" said Kurose Tōya.

"A road," said Ren. "They're going to bring some people home."

The old man looked at it for a long time.

"I'd like to know," he said slowly, "if they come back."

Ren turned and looked at him.

"That's a want," she said.

"Is it?" He looked at his broom. "Then I have one." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "Ask me tomorrow if I still have it."

Under the six hundred and twelfth gate, an old monk said he liked the way the light came down through the cedars. Under the three hundred and fortieth, an old woman said nothing at all, and listened, very hard, to a bell.

One came down to the cemetery hill above Lingzhou, where a captain stood in a stone gate facing out and a director lay under a stone that said QUIET, and six thousand dead of the cemetery companies went up it past them, quietly, so as not to disturb them.

One came down to the river gate, where Ma Lianhua stood with her rusted halberd at the head of seventeen thousand five hundred and fifty-three.

And one came down to the north wall of Lingzhou, to the battlements of Sector Nine.

They were all there.

The whole city. He had not asked them to come. Nobody had. They came anyway, at dawn, up onto the wall, out of the streets, into the floodlights that nobody had turned off after the walkers, in their hundreds of thousands, along the whole north wall from the river gate to Crossing Six and back, and stood. Not talking. The way the coast road had stood when an army truck went north with a sheet in the back.

Su Mingli at the top of the stairs, with her arms folded. Marshal Orlov beside her, with his gloves off. General Han, in his dark green uniform, with his cap under his arm. Captain Lin Wen in the watchtower above Crossing Six, standing in two shallow hollows in the floor by the north slit. Sister Ning with her candle. Fat Tan, crying openly, not bothering to hide it. Principal Yue. Hendrik Maas, who had driven a bus from the station.

And at the very front, at the foot of the gold road where it came down onto the battlements, in her wheelchair, with a blanket over her knees and a microphone in her lap, his mother. And behind her, holding the handles of the chair, in a hoodie too big for her, with a marker in her pocket, his sister.

The Spear Corps went first.

Two hundred and seventy-two of them, in their rows, rusted spears grounded and then lifted, up onto the gold road and away up into the morning sky, because Old Seven had not asked them and they had not asked him. Then Adjutant Xu, with his cane, at the top of the Lattice, humming. Then the forty from Grid Nine in their white, who had stood in the field once with their eyes open, and one of them, passing General Han on the battlements, slowed, and turned his skull, and the general put his hand out, very carefully, into the air beside him, where a shoulder would be.

Then the living.

Mo Shi and Mo Xing, side by side, elbows touching, with one pack between them. Bai Lu with her bag. Hu Dabao with his shield on both arms. Kwan with a new list, forty-one pages. Qin Hao with his bandaged arms and his notebook in his breast pocket, who stopped at the foot of the road and turned round and looked at the crowd on the wall and said, loudly, to nobody, "I'm writing it down," and went up. The Elder, tall and pale, with the gold thread running through her into the road under her feet.

Lingyue, with her grandfather's sword.

She did not go up. She stood at the foot of the road and waited.

He knelt beside his mother's chair.

"I'm going to ask you," he said.

"I know how you ask, Yuanshu."

"Anything you want me to bring back?"

Zhou Wanqing looked up the gold road into the morning sky for a moment. Then down at him.

"You," she said. "And the child. And the soldiers. And the corporal who wants to know where home is." She took his face in her thin hands. "And when you're back, you're going to tell me what you wanted. The whole time. I've never once heard you say."

"I'll tell you."

"That's not yes," said his mother. "That's what you want."

"I know," said Yuanshu. "I'm still learning the difference."

She let go of his face. And lifted the microphone off her lap, and pressed the button, and said, in a quiet, sharp, slightly hoarse voice that went out of every speaker on the north wall of Lingzhou and every street behind it:

"Lanterns."

And all along the wall, in the gray dawn, hundreds of thousands of people lit a light.

Candles. Torches. Phones. Oil lamps from kitchens. A child's nightlight shaped like a rabbit. Matches. Along the whole north wall of Lingzhou, from the river gate to Crossing Six, a long line of small lights in the dawn, held up. Not dimming. Not hiding. The opposite. A city on a shore, lighting the lamp.

Xiaoyu, behind the wheelchair, had not lit anything. She had the marker in her hand.

"Go on," she said. Her voice was rough. "I'll be here."

He went up.

Old Seven in front of him, with his spear. Lingyue beside him, close, her shoulder against his arm. The staff in his hand, with a schoolteacher awake in it and his mother's fragment asleep. And behind them on the gold road, and on five other gold roads rising up out of Blue Star into the morning from a mountain and a sea and a cemetery and a river gate, converging, fifty thousand dead of Blue Star, banked to steady gray, walking north into the sky after their own.

At the top of the road, at the end of the world, the small white ring was open, and in it, waiting, a hundred and seventy-five gold columns who had asked if they could come.

I am going to tell you the numbers. All of them. You asked me to, once, on the first night, and you have never told me to stop.

[Zhou Yuanshu. Lord. Level 69.]

[Death Law: 39.2%. Field: 9.8 kilometers.]

[Seed v1.6. Parameter: "Open."]

I changed it myself. Last night. I did not ask anyone. I am telling you now.

Fifty thousand two hundred and thirty-one of the dead, who said yes.

Eight of the living, who said yes.

A keeper who is afraid and is carrying us anyway.

Thirty-nine raft-days to the cold fire. Something pruning along the arm toward it. Someone ringing a boat bell from the deep water, coming quietly, a year behind.

Sixty-one soldiers. A child of a world that asks. The Sixth. The cousins. Two hundred and eighty-one million. A corporal who wants to know where home is.

I do not have a probability.

I have stopped needing one for everything.

Yuanshu. Let us go and get them.

At the threshold of the ring, before they went through, the keeper sent one thing ahead of them. Very quietly. Along its attention. Into the dark. The way you hand something to someone in a room where you cannot see them.

...WE ARE COMING.

And for a long moment, nothing.

Then, very faint, very far, across thirty-nine raft-days of cold dark Sea, the long way, through the echo, so small that only Seed caught it, and only because it had been listening every month for four hundred and twenty-nine days:

We can see you.

I still have the queen of hearts.

Tell the captain. — H.

Old Seven, at the front of fifty thousand, on a gold road at the end of the world, did not stop walking.

But his gray flame came up. Slowly. Steady. Warm.

And they went through.
