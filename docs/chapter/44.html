Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 44The Oldest Witness

czverse9 min read

The old monk did not tell it. He showed it.

One moment Yuanshu was kneeling on polished boards in a dim wooden hall with the smell of incense in his nose. The next, the hall was gone, and he was standing on the same mountain twelve hundred years ago, in bright sun, and there were no cedars.

There were terraces. Rice terraces, cut into the slope in long curving steps all the way down to the sea, each one a mirror of flooded water with green shoots standing in it. Little figures in straw hats bent over the water. Smoke rose from a village by the shore. A bell was ringing somewhere, slow and deep.

And a young man in a saffron robe stood beside him on the path, with a shaved head and a broom in his hand, sweeping fallen leaves off the stone steps.

That is him.

The oldest one. As he was.

He is perhaps twenty.

The young monk stopped sweeping. He leaned on his broom and looked up at the sky, shading his eyes with his hand.

Yuanshu looked up too.

At first it was nothing. A smudge in the blue, high up, far to the north. Like a thumbprint on glass.

Then it was a ring.

Thin. Pale. Perfectly round. It hung in the daytime sky over the northern horizon like a halo with nothing inside it, and it was so big that it took up a quarter of the sky, and it was so faint that if you looked straight at it, it almost disappeared.

Down on the terraces, the little figures in straw hats had straightened up. They were pointing.

The lens.

The same shape. The same law. I would know it anywhere.

But it is wide open.

The young monk's voice spoke in his mind, the clear old bell-voice, calm and sad.

"It hung there for nine days. We did not know what it was. The priests said it was an omen. The farmers said it was the moon's ghost. The children made songs about it."

"On the tenth day, it began to rain."

The rain was light.

It came down out of the clear sky, out of the ring, silver and fine, like the threads of a spider's web catching the sun. Thousands of them. Millions. Falling slowly, drifting, settling over the whole country like a net being lowered over a pond.

Wherever a thread touched someone, it stayed.

Yuanshu watched it happen to a woman on the nearest terrace. A thread drifted down out of the bright sky and touched the top of her straw hat and went through it, and through her, and she stopped. Mid-step. Mid-word. She stood very still in the flooded paddy with the water around her ankles, and her face went empty, and her head turned, slowly, three degrees, toward the north.

All down the mountain, all across the terraces, all through the village by the shore, people stopped and turned.

Thousands of heads. Three degrees. North.

The bell in the village had stopped ringing.

"It did not take everyone. Not at first. First it listened. It listened through them for a long time, the way you would listen at a door. To learn the house. To learn who was inside."

"We thought the ones who turned were sick. We carried them into the temple. We nursed them. We sang to them."

"We did not know that every song we sang went up."

The vision moved, the way dreams move. Days passed in a breath.

Yuanshu stood in a great hall full of beds and straw mats, and on every mat lay someone with their eyes open and their head turned north. Monks moved between them with bowls of water and cloths. Some were weeping. Some were praying aloud. Some were singing, softly, the old songs, the way you'd sing to a sick child.

And from every turned head, thin as a hair, a silver thread went up through the roof.

He could see them now. He could see them the way Seed saw them. Thousands of threads, rising from this hall and from every village and every town, rising like the strings of a vast instrument up and up into the bright sky, all converging on the pale ring in the north.

The young monk with the broom was kneeling beside a little girl, holding her hand.

"Then the lens closed. Just a little. Like an eye narrowing."

"And it pulled."

Yuanshu would remember the next part for the rest of his life.

It happened without a sound. Every thread went taut at once, all over the country, a million silver strings drawn tight in the same instant. And up them, from every turned head, something rose.

Light. Faint and gold and flickering. It came out of the people on the mats like heat coming off a road on a summer day, and went up the threads, fast, all of it, all at once, the way water goes up a straw.

Memories. He could see them going. Flashes. A face. A meal. A song. A first word. A wedding. A dead parent's hands. The smell of rain on a hot day. Everything a person had ever been, pulled up a silver thread into the sky in a single long breath.

On the mat, the little girl's hand went limp in the young monk's.

All over the hall, the turned heads turned back. Slowly. Toward the ceiling. Empty. Breathing. Alive.

Hollow.

"We call it the cutting," said the old bell-voice, very quietly. "Not death. Death is a door; you go through, you are somewhere. This was not a door. This was a scythe going through a field of grain. The stalks stay standing. The seed is taken."

"Three in ten of all the people of these islands. In one breath."

"And then the ring closed, and went away, and the sky was blue, and there was nothing to fight. Nothing to follow. Only the field, cut."

The vision dimmed.

When it came back, it was night, and the young monk was older. Thirty, perhaps. Thinner. He sat on the stone steps of the temple in the dark with six others, under the stars, and none of them were speaking.

"Some of us were not taken. We wanted to know why."

"It took us twenty years to understand. There was only one thing the ones who were left had in common."

"They had been quiet."

"Not silent in the mouth. Silent in the mind. Monks in deep meditation. A deaf shepherd alone on a mountain. A woman who had been grieving so long she had stopped thinking anything at all. The thread listens. That is all it can do. It learns a mind by listening to it. And a mind it has not learned, it cannot find to cut."

Yuanshu thought of the Listening Pavilion. Fourteen candles standing straight in the still air.

"Then we found the record in the old archive at Nara. Written on bark, older than our writing. The ring had come before. Twelve hundred years before us. And twelve hundred years before that. Each time, the same. A ring. A rain of threads. A long listening. A cutting."

"Blue Star is not a battlefield, child. It was never a battlefield. Nobody up there is at war with us."

"Blue Star is a field. And something comes to cut it when the grain is ripe."

"So we made a choice. Many of us. We would sit. We would go into the deepest quiet we knew, and we would stay there, past eating, past drinking, past the body. We would hold ourselves in that quiet on purpose, the way you hold a breath under water. And when we died, we would not let go."

"Because a quiet mind cannot be cut. And because someone must remember, for the next time. The people of the next cutting would not have the bark scroll. They would only have us."

"We have been sitting here for twelve hundred years, waiting for the ring to come back. Waiting for someone who could hear us."

"Most of us crumbled while we waited."

The vision let go of him.

He was on his knees on the polished floor of the hall again, with his hands flat on the boards, and his face was wet, and the gray light from the door lay across the floor in its long pale rectangle, exactly where it had been.

He had been gone, Su Mingli told him later, for three minutes.

Behind him, Shirasagi Ren was sitting very straight, with tears running silently down her calm face. She had seen it too. Seed had let her in, on the edge of the vision, at the old monk's request.

At the door, Su Mingli had one hand flat against the wooden frame, as if she needed it to stand up.

"Twelve hundred years," she said. Her voice was not quite steady. "And twelve hundred before that. And before that." She closed her eyes. "The Black Tide wasn't the war. It was a farmer walking the edge of the field. Checking whether it was ripe."

The old monk is still listening.

He is waiting for you to ask the other thing.

Yuanshu wiped his face with the back of his hand and bowed again over the boards.

"In fifty-one days," he said, "five nations are going to sit in a crater on an island and decide who owns the dead. One of them wants a machine to read every mind it can reach, to keep everyone safe. One of them wants the dead to choose." He swallowed. "I don't have the right to ask you this. You've done enough. You've done more than anyone. But if the Council heard what you just showed me..."

"Yes."

He looked up.

"Not YES in your ledger, child. We do not want to be raised. We do not want to walk. We are very tired, and we would like, when this is over, to finally lie down."

"ONLY TO SPEAK. Once. On that island. Then QUIET, forever."

"Write it in your book that way."

"I will," said Yuanshu. "I promise."

"And child."

"You think much too loudly. Even now. Even here. I could hear you from the bottom of the mountain."

"If the thread ever finds you, it will learn you very fast."

"Sit with us for a while. Before you go. We will teach you how to be quiet."

He stayed three days.

He sat on the polished floor in front of the seven Sleepers from dawn until dark, cross-legged, with his hands in his lap, and tried to stop thinking. He was terrible at it. On the first day he lasted four seconds. On the second day, a minute. Every time his mind ran off after something, a thought, a worry, the number four hundred and ten, the old bell-voice would say, very gently, There. Did you hear that? That was you, shouting.

Old Seven stood outside under the cedars for three days and three nights without moving, his gray flame turned so low it was almost out.

On the evening of the third day, as the light was going gold through the door, Yuanshu sat in front of the seven and did not think anything at all for eleven minutes.

I could not hear you.

For eleven minutes. I was right here, behind your eyes, and I could not hear you think.

It was very strange.

It was a little like being alone.

When he opened his eyes, Shirasagi Ren was standing in the doorway with the sunset behind her, looking at him.

"Yamato will vote for the Charter," she said.

Then she bowed. Deeply. Not to him.

To the seven, behind him, in their faded robes, sitting in a row in the gold light with their heads bowed, listening.

And for the second time in twelve hundred years, the oldest one smiled.
