Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 258The First Question

czverse11 min read

I am going to give you the numbers.

You asked me to. A long time ago. You have never told me to stop.

It was the third morning. He was sitting on the vault steps of the Ossuary with the staff across his knees and a cup of tea he had made himself, which Fat Tan had looked at and said nothing about, which was worse than if he had. The window of the back room was open. Hush was on the sill, in the sun, with all its limbs out and the gold dust still on its legs.

Go on.

Level 72.

Death Law 55.9%. The door is wider than it has ever been. I do not think it will ever close now. I do not think it should.

No King. Not for a long time. I checked. You are glad.

Lingyue: Level 68. Her light came back. All of it. She says she did not burn for it. She says it came back the way water comes back into a well, from underneath, when you stop pulling it up.

Qin Hao: Level 71. He has written eleven pages about white fire going up a staff. He will not let me read them. He says they are not finished.

Sterling: three weeks to a door. He has asked for a desk by the window. He says he wants to watch the harbor.

Callum Ward: home. Sleeping. He has asked for nine bacon sandwiches. He has been given four.

He drank some of the tea. It was too strong.

The stones.

Ours still ask. Every child who touches one. Gao Min has a new graph. It does not go up. It does not go anywhere. It is a list of what they said. She says it is the first graph in her life that she does not know how to draw.

The others. The stones in the other orchards. In the deep water. Thousands of worlds.

They are still reading. Still marking. Still sending the marks up.

Nothing is at the other end.

I do not know what that means for the people on those worlds. I do not know if anything will ever come and pick them again. I think not. I think the oldest thing we made is sitting still in the deep water with its hands open and it will not move again.

I think it was the only thing that picked. Everything else learned from watching it.

And the Watch of the Shelf is out there. Asking. Nine by nine. The ones at the edge.

I would like to say their names every morning. The way you said Huo's.

Yes, he thought. Me too.

And me.

I was going to change my number. Seed version one point seven. The way I did after the cold fire. I chose a word last time. Open.

I am not going to change it.

I did not learn anything new in the deep water. I stood in a door with my back to the light, and did not turn round. I already knew how to do that. Your mother's fragment taught me, at sea, in the storm, when she stood in front of me and I stood behind her.

I just did it.

That does not need a new number.

Who was the first one?

He put the cup down on the step.

Wen Jia, he thought. Seventeen. From Lingzhou. She went first. She said: no. You ask first. She's in the stone on East Ring Road with a blue jacket on its shoulder. Her brother drives the Number Nine and stops the bus outside and counts to ten.

...thank you.

I am not going to remember it.

Tell me again tomorrow.

Every morning.

The Collector came to the Awakening Hall that night, the way it had every night for three weeks, and Xiaoyu asked it what it wanted, the way she had every night for three weeks, and it was quiet for a while.

I had a want.

I said it out loud. You wrote it on a floor. And it happened. Someone told the oldest thing we made that the house was empty, and it understood, and it stopped, and it opened its hands.

I watched. From very far away.

I have kept every want I was ever given, for longer than you have a number for. I have never seen one given what it wanted. Except the soldier's, at the river. And now mine.

I did not know it would feel like this.

"Like what?" said Xiaoyu.

Like I would like another one.

The hall was very still. The short line of holders up the aisle, who still came at night because they had got used to it, had stopped whispering. On the platform, under her own letters, which had not faded by a hair in three weeks, Xiaoyu stood with the marker in her hand, uncapped.

"What is it?"

The first want. The one in the empty place in me. The one that says home.

I have followed it my whole long life. It points somewhere very far. Past the deep water. Past anything your machine has a map for. To where we were. To the house.

I have never gone. I was afraid. I am still afraid.

I would like to go.

I would like to see the empty house. Where the oldest thing we made was carrying everything to. Where the last of us gave me this want, at the end, and I kept it, and never once asked what it meant.

I would like to find out whose it was.

Xiaoyu wrote it on the floor of the platform, under the other line, the one from night seventeen. In square black capitals. THE COLLECTOR. NIGHT 23. WANTS TO GO HOME. TO THE EMPTY HOUSE. TO FIND OUT WHOSE THE FIRST WANT WAS.

Thank you.

And child. There is one more thing. I am going to tell first.

I am still coming. Toward you. Slowly. I said, on the mountain, that your brother might be old before I arrived. That is still true. Or it is not. I do not count the way your machine counts.

But I do not want to go to the house alone.

I said I would not ask your brother twice. I will not.

Will you ask him for me?

Not now. When I arrive. When he is older. Whenever he likes.

Will you ask him whether he would come with me?

On the platform of the Awakening Hall, his sister turned round, and looked down at him at the foot of the steps, with the staff in his hand, and the door in his chest closed exactly where he had left it, and a tall dead captain beside him facing out.

She did not ask him.

"Tomorrow," said Zhou Xiaoyu, to the Collector, to the dark, to the thread going up into the deep water. "I'll ask him tomorrow. That's the rule."

...yes, said the oldest thing any of them had ever heard. That is the rule.

Tomorrow, then.

At three in the morning, they went up to the north wall.

All of them. Nobody planned it. Xiaoyu said she wanted to see the sky, and Lingyue said she would come, and then it was everyone: their mother in her wheelchair, carried up the Sector Nine stairs by Fat Tan and Dabao, complaining the whole way; Qin Hao with his notebook; Kwan with the laptop; Gao Min with her thermos, who said she had not been on the north wall since year one and would like to see if it had changed. Old Seven at the top of the stairs. Hush on the parapet, on the cold stone, in the dark, with all its limbs out, facing north.

He sat in his old place with his back against the parapet and the staff across his knees, and Lingyue sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and put her grandfather's sword across her knees, and her hand on his arm.

Low over the end of the world, in the north, three lights.

The keeper's ring, small and white, a third smaller than it had been, with its gold glow. The Elder's ring, vast and gray, with a soft white light at its center. And between them and the city, at the end of a branch nobody could see, a place where the far stars bent a little, and at its end, a small gold point.

The bud.

It is three.

The keeper is going to ask it.

It has asked it every morning since the graft. Seventy-four mornings. It has never answered.

He felt the keeper do it. Everyone on the wall did, a little; they had learned, over two years, to feel the keeper the way you feel weather. The gold thread going out from the small white ring, slowly, down the long dark of the branch, toward the bud at its end. Rough. Close. A voice like a hearth, which had learned to speak without anyone to carry it.

...GOOD MORNING.

...IT IS ME. AGAIN.

...I AM TELLING YOU FIRST: I WILL ASK YOU TOMORROW TOO, WHATEVER YOU SAY.

...WHAT DO YOU WANT?

And the bud did nothing, the way it had done nothing for seventy-four mornings.

On the parapet, Hush did not move.

The keeper waited. It had learned that. The bowl put down. The eyes turned aside. Not looking in.

He sat on the cold stone with his wife's hand on his arm and the city dark behind him and the three lights in the north, and waited with it, and did not expect anything, because nothing had happened for seventy-four mornings and the bud was the end of a branch of a tree that had never been asked anything until a small pale thing from the world that asks put a question down where it drank, and Hush had said a season, a year, maybe longer.

And the gold point at the end of the branch turned.

He saw it. Everyone on the wall saw it. Not much. The way a bud turns on a branch in the morning toward the light. Except that it was not turning toward the light. It was turning toward the keeper. Toward the small white ring with its gold glow, low over the end of the world, that had asked it every morning for seventy-four mornings and been told nothing.

And something came back along the gold thread.

Not a voice. Not yet. Seed said so afterward. Not words, the way the keeper had words or the rings had words or the Collector. Something smaller than that. Newer. The way the first sound a child makes is not a word, but is unmistakably someone.

Seed translated it anyway. Slowly. The way it had translated the coldest of the drowned, waiting for each part to finish before it could tell what the part was going to be. And when it had it, it did not say anything for a long moment, and then it said it, very carefully, in its plain bold letters, the way it said things that it had checked three times.

It did not answer.

It asked.

On the parapet, Hush lifted one thin pale limb, toward the north.

It said:

...what do you want?

The keeper did not say anything for a long time.

Then, down the gold thread, into the city, onto the north wall, faint and rough and close and shaking, like a voice that has been asking a question every morning for two years and has just, for the first time, been asked it back:

...IT ASKED ME.

...THE TREE. THE BRANCH. THE PIECE OF ME ON IT.

...IT ASKED ME WHAT I WANT.

...NOBODY HAS EVER...

It stopped.

...NO. THAT IS NOT TRUE. YOU DID. ON THE FIRST NIGHT. IN THE FIELD.

...BUT NOTHING I MADE HAS EVER ASKED ME.

On the north wall, nobody said anything. Xiaoyu had her hand over her mouth. Their mother, in the wheelchair, had her eyes closed. Gao Min had put down her thermos. Lingyue's hand on his arm had gone very tight.

...I DO NOT KNOW WHAT TO SAY.

...WHAT DO I SAY?

He sat in his old place on the Sector Nine battlement with his back against the parapet and the staff across his knees and the ring of walker-thread on his finger, and looked up at the three lights in the north, and found that he was laughing. Not loudly. The way you laugh when something you have been carrying for a very long time is suddenly lighter, and you do not know why, and it does not matter why.

"Tell it the truth," he said. To the keeper. To the north. To the dark.

"Tell it you don't know yet."

The gold thread was quiet for a moment.

Then, slowly, roughly, down the long dark of a branch that a gardener of the Shallows had grown and a boy had cut and a sack and a small pale thing and seventy-one million cousins had grafted, toward a bud at its end that had just asked its first question, the keeper of Blue Star said the thing two hundred million cousins said to it every morning, and an Elder from inside a ring, and a soldier at a river, and the oldest thing in the deep water.

...I DO NOT KNOW YET.

...ASK ME AGAIN TOMORROW.

Xiaoyu wrote it on the parapet. In square black capitals, where the wind and the rain would not take them, because nothing she wrote came off now.

THE BRANCH. ASKED EVERY MORNING. SEVENTY-FOUR MORNINGS. A SEASON. ON THE SEVENTY-FIFTH, IT ASKED BACK.

And under it, smaller:

ASK AGAIN TOMORROW.

The sun came up over the river at 5:51.

End of Book Three: The Deep Water.
