Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 111The Doorway

czverse7 min read

They buried Captain Zhao Gangfeng standing up.

It was Zhao Mei's idea. She came to the wall at Sector Nine the morning after the Mouth left, in her nurse's scrubs under a winter coat, with her hair pulled back and her face very calm, and stood in front of the raft for a long time. In front of the broad old skeleton at its rim, leaning on an empty shield, facing out.

Su Mingli stood beside her. As she had promised.

Zhao Mei did not cry. She had read the pages in the ledger the night before. All of them. Every word Yuanshu had written down in the cold place, about a doorway and a fire and a shield going white. She had read them three times, Su Mingli said, sitting at the kitchen table in her small flat by the hospital, and then she had folded them very carefully and put them in an envelope and put the envelope in her coat pocket, where it was now.

"He touched my head," she said finally. To Yuanshu. Not looking away from the skeleton. "In the cemetery. The first night you raised him. I was nine years old when he died. I don't remember him. I only know him from one photograph my grandmother kept. And he walked across the grass in the dark and touched my head, like he was checking I was still there."

"I remember," said Yuanshu.

"He held a gate for eleven hours in the Black Tide. And a door in the middle of a fire until it was done." She looked at the shield. "He doesn't need a grave. He needs a doorway."

So they built him one.

On the hill above the old cemetery in Lingzhou, where the city fell away to the river and you could see the whole north wall running east and west into the haze. Not a gravestone. A gate. Two pillars of gray stone and a crossbeam, plain, the height of a tall man. Fat Tan paid for it and would not let anyone know he had. The stonemason was a man whose father had been on the north gate in the Black Tide and had walked home afterward when the wall came down, because a captain with a shield had held it long enough.

They stood Captain Zhao in it.

Facing out. Toward the city. Toward the wall. His bony hands on the rim of his empty shield, the way he had leaned on it at the end. The bones wired together by a doctor from the Institute who had asked for the job and would not take money for it either.

And at his feet, in a half-circle, kneeling, facing inward toward him, the ten soldiers of the Third Battalion who had gone quiet in the throat of the first ring. Their skulls bowed. Their rusted rifles across their knees.

A captain in a doorway. His men around him.

Facing the right way.

The whole city came up the hill that afternoon. Not all at once. In ones and twos and families, all day, until dusk. They did not bring flowers. Sister Ning had said nothing about flowers. But somebody had brought a candle, the first person up the hill at dawn, and set it on the step of the gate, unlit. And somebody else had brought one, and set it beside the first. And by dusk, the step was covered, and the ground around it, and the slope below, thousands of unlit candles, all the way down to the cemetery wall.

At dusk, Zhao Mei lit the first one.

And the next person lit theirs from it. And the next. Down the hill. Until the whole slope was gold.

Old Seven did not come up the hill until the last of the light was gone and the crowds had gone home.

Yuanshu was there. He had stayed. He was sitting on the grass a little way down the slope, among the candles, with the white staff across his knees, when he heard the footsteps. Heavy. Slow. Bone on stone.

The old spear captain came up out of the dark from the cemetery below and walked through the candles without disturbing a single one, and stopped in front of the gate.

He stood there. The candles around his feet burned straight up; there was no wind.

He did not cuff Zhao's skull this time. He looked at the broad old skeleton leaning on its shield in its doorway, and at the ten kneeling soldiers at its feet. His men. Some of them. The ones who had stayed.

And then he looked down at his own left hand.

Three fingers. A little shorter than the right. A little grayer. Scars on the fingers from a spear shaft, from years of holding it the way a captain taught.

He is counting.

Not out loud. Inside.

One hundred and forty-one, in the river. Fifty-three laid down. Four on a sled, home to Lingzhou with three strangers. Twelve walking a kind man south. Ten in the throat of the first ring. Sixty-two into the Mouth with Hush.

Eighty-eight said yes to walk with their captain.

Today, one of his captains stands in a doorway, and ten of his men kneel at its feet. Sixteen stand somewhere on the north wall among nineteen thousand. And sixty-two are in the fire.

He is very quiet, Yuanshu.

Yuanshu stood up.

He walked through the candles and stood beside Old Seven at the foot of the gate, shoulder to shoulder, both of them facing the same way. The way they had stood in his room at Tianshu, a long time ago, the night he read a dead man's notebook out loud.

He did not say anything for a while.

"Huo said they'd burn low," he said at last. "Hush taught them how. In the fire, they're doing what Zhao did. Standing guard. Over the ones who haven't been asked yet."

The old soldier did not move.

"Seed heard Hush say hello," said Yuanshu. "Inside it. Before it was too far. And someone answered." He looked at the gate. "That's sixty-two of your men in there, making sure the ones who answer have someone standing beside them."

Old Seven was very still.

Then, slowly, he raised his left hand. Huo's hand. And laid it, very lightly, on Zhao's shoulder, over the rag of the gate captain's coat.

And left it there.

He says: they are where the captain should be.

He says: he is where the boy is.

He says: both are right.

He says: it is still very quiet.

They stood there until the candles began to burn down.

Xiaoyu found them on her way home.

She came up the hill in his old hoodie with the sleeves pulled down over her hands and the brass key on its red string around her neck, and stood at the edge of the candlelight, looking at her brother and the old skeleton standing side by side at the gate.

She did not say anything.

She walked up and stood on Yuanshu's other side. Shoulder to shoulder. Facing the same way.

The three of them stood there in a row. A girl, a boy, and a dead soldier with someone else's hand.

"I was horrible to him," said Xiaoyu, after a while. "Zhao. When you first raised him. I said he looked like a wardrobe. I said it in front of him. He heard."

"He didn't mind."

"He minded a little." She was quiet. "He used to stand outside the Ossuary every night. On the stool by the door. When I locked up, I'd say goodnight. He never said anything back. Obviously. But he'd tilt his head." She pulled her sleeves further down over her hands. "Last night I locked up and the stool was empty and I said goodnight anyway."

Yuanshu put his arm around her.

She let him.

On the way down the hill, in the dark, among the guttering candles, she stopped.

She lifted the red string over her head. And held out the small brass key. Palm up.

"Here," she said.

"You said when you'd decided."

"I've decided." She did not look at him. She looked back up the hill, at the gate, at the broad old skeleton leaning on its shield in the candlelight, facing out. "People should have a key to go home. Even if they're the kind of people who go away all the time." She pushed it into his hand. "Especially if they are."

He put it around his neck.

Behind them, at the top of the hill, Old Seven had not moved. He was still standing at the foot of the gate with his three-fingered hand on a dead captain's shoulder, and his gray flame burning low and steady in the dark.

He says go home.

He says he will stand a little longer.

He says someone should stand the first night with a new door. In case it is lonely.
