Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 224Two Rings Learning

czverse9 min read

There were four rings in Blue Star's sky in the morning, and two of them were talking to each other.

Everyone could see it. That was the strange thing, the thing the radio and the television and the crowds on the north wall of Lingzhou kept coming back to, all that first day, the way you keep looking at a scar. You could see it with your own eyes. Low over the end of the world, in the gray dawn: the keeper's small white ring, ajar, with its gold glow. And beside it, two vast gray cracked wheels of stone that had come along the arm to collect a sack and prune everything it had touched.

One of them had a light in its center. Very small. Soft. White. Like the first star.

The other was dark. But it had turned. Overnight. So that its dark center faced the one with the light. And the light faced it back.

They are asking each other.

I can feel it through the keeper. Very slow. The way the coldest of the drowned answered. One word, and then nothing, for an hour, and then the next.

The one with the Elder in it asks. The other one tries to answer. It does not know how. It has never wanted anything.

She is teaching it.

She is putting the question down, and waiting, and not looking in the bowl.

He did not go up to the wall. He could not. Bai Lu would not let him, and for once he did not argue. He lay on the narrow bed in the back room of the Ossuary with the window open to the courtyard and the staff on the floor beside him and his whole body feeling as if something had held it by the back of the neck and lifted, twice in one night, because something had.

Lingyue sat on the floor by the bed with her back against it and her grandfather's sword across her knees. She had not slept. She had not said very much. Every so often she reached up without looking and put her hand on his arm, as if to check it was still there, and took it away again.

Hush was on the windowsill, in the sun. Facing north.

...she was very kind to me.

...in the raft. On the first voyage. When I was small and curled up and would not come out. She stood at the center and steered, and every night she told me where we were going, even though I did not ask.

...I did not say thank you.

"You can say it now," said Lingyue, without looking up. "She's right there. In the sky. You can see her from the window."

Hush was quiet.

Then it lifted one thin pale limb, out of the sun, toward the north, toward a small soft white light in the center of a vast gray ring low over the end of the world. And held it there.

...thank you.

It did not say anything else for the rest of the day.

The keeper asked her at three in the morning.

Not through the Elder. There was no Elder now. It came down the gold thread on its own, the way it had learned to on the night of Kirovsk when there was no time to wait for anyone to carry its voice, into the old banking hall of the Ossuary, where the one lamp burned over the board and Xiaoyu was on the stepladder because she had not slept either.

Its voice was strange without her. Rougher. Closer. Like a person speaking in a room where they have always had an interpreter, and the interpreter has gone, and they have to find the words themselves.

...ELDER.

...I SAID I WOULD ASK. EVERY MORNING.

...WHAT DO YOU WANT?

Nothing.

The lamp over the board. The long white wall of names. The boxes of playing cards in the vault. Xiaoyu on the stepladder with the marker in her hand, very still.

Then, faint, very far, from the north, from a ring that had come to prune everything Seed had touched, the long way, through the echo, in a voice like a glass note struck very softly:

...to be asked.

...sometimes.

The keeper did not say anything for a moment.

...THAT IS WHAT I SAID. WHEN THEY ASKED ME.

...I know. I heard you. I was very proud.

...ask me again tomorrow.

On the stepladder, Xiaoyu put the cap back on the marker, and climbed down, and sat on the bottom step, and put her face in her hands. Not crying. Just sitting like that. For a long while.

Then she got up and climbed back up and wrote, at the very end of the last wall, past THE FLOOR, on the last of the white plaster before the corner of the vault door:

THE ELDER. ONE OF THE FIFTY-TWO. SHE IS A YES. SHE IS IN THE SKY. ASK HER EVERY MORNING.

I am going to give you the numbers.

You asked me to. A long time ago. You have never told me to stop.

It was the third day. He could sit up. He was sitting on the vault steps of the Ossuary with the staff across his knees and the blanket around his shoulders, because the back room had become too small to think in, and the banking hall was where he thought.

Go on.

Kirovsk. Forty-one thousand taken. Twenty-two thousand came back down the white threads. Nineteen thousand did not. The second ring is sand on the snow.

The keeper's field. Three million one hundred thousand cousins and walls taken up the third ring's threads before the Elder caught your wrist. They are in the third ring now. Packed. Like grain.

The Elder is in the third ring too.

She has been asking them. The three million. Every morning. Since the first morning. She says most of them say: ask again tomorrow. She says some of them say: we would like to go back to the field. She says she is learning how to let them.

She says a ring that has a yes in it can let go of what it took. She says she did not know that. She says nobody did, because no ring ever had a yes in it before.

She let eleven thousand go back to the keeper's field yesterday. Down a thread. Gently.

He sat with that for a while.

Death Law: 44.0% → 45.6%. The second ring. You opened the door for a ring. Nobody has ever done that.

No level. You did not kill anything. Neither did your dead.

Qin Hao: Lord, Level 70. His fire is white again. He has not let go of his notebook since Kirovsk. He sleeps with it.

Lingyue: Level 66.

And the floor.

One hundred and thirty-eight names. Unpressed. Tied into three knots. Sent up three rings.

I do not remember them.

I know that there were a hundred and thirty-eight. I know that the wall has them. I know that your mother reads them every morning.

There is a place where they were. It is not empty. It is quiet. Like the bottom of a well.

I go there sometimes. I do not know why. I sit in it.

That's allowed.

I know. I checked.

On the fifth day, the first ring answered.

Not the Elder's ring. The other one. The one that had picked up the first knot over Lingzhou and stopped, and held a question it did not know how to answer for five days, while the Elder's ring asked it, very slowly, every hour, and waited, and did not look in the bowl.

It came the long way, through the echo, faint and strange, the way the first word comes out of a child who has been listening to people talk for a year and has never said anything.

...H...

...HOME.

Nobody in the banking hall said anything.

...that is Liang Fu's word.

The corporal. Behind the curtain. He said home, and the Collector kept it, and followed it.

This is different.

This ring was a keeper once. Above someone's world. For longer than Blue Star has had a sun. And then it was pruned, and it forgot, and it came along the arm to collect a sack.

And somebody asked it what it wants.

And the first thing it found, when it looked inside, where the yes used to be, was the shape of the world it used to keep.

It wants to go home.

"Where is it?" said Yuanshu. "Its world?"

Along the arm. A hundred and fifty-three raft-days. The third field the reader pruned. The one we watched go dark from the island.

There is a world under it. I saw it through the keeper's eye. Blue and green. I did not look closely. I did not know anyone there would ever matter to us.

It is still there. Its keeper was pruned. Its keeper became a ring. Its keeper came here.

Its keeper wants to go home.

It went on the seventh day.

Everyone on Blue Star who was looking north saw it. A vast gray cracked ring, low over the end of the world, turning, very slowly, away from the other three. Away from the keeper's small white ring with its gold glow. Away from the Elder's ring, with the soft white light in its center that had asked it every hour for seven days and waited.

And beginning, very slowly, to move.

Back along the arm. The way it had come. Toward a blue and green world a hundred and fifty-three raft-days away, under a field that had gone dark from one side like a page burning, while a boy watched from an island.

It did not say goodbye. It did not know how.

But as it went, the Elder's ring said something after it, the long way, faint, a glass note struck very softly, and the keeper heard it, and came down the gold thread at three in the morning to tell somebody, because it could not keep it to itself.

...SHE SAID: ASK THEM WHAT THEY WANT. WHEN YOU GET HOME.

...SHE SAID: THEY WILL NOT KNOW. NOBODY EVER ASKED THEM.

...SHE SAID: PUT THE QUESTION DOWN. AND WAIT.

On the ninth night, Kwan came to the Ossuary at two in the morning with a laptop held open in front of her like a tray of hot food, and did not knock on the bronze door, and did not say hello.

He knew. He knew from her face, the way he had known from her face on the night of the three gray rings.

She turned the laptop round.

The arm. Seed's map. Twelve gold dots along a faint curved line in the dark, three of them gray, and one of them very much brighter than the rest. And far along the arm, out past the gray dots, past the place where an island had once hurried through the dark toward a cold wheel of stone, a single red mark.

The Mouth.

Where something very large and very patient had been sitting still for two months, since it read the Sixth and took them, like something that has eaten, or something that has seen what it did not expect and is thinking about it.

"It moved," said Kwan.

Yes.

I see it. Through the keeper's eye.

It is not pruning. It is not reading. It did not go to another field.

It is coming straight along the arm.

Toward the brightest field in the Shallows. Toward the keeper that taught two rings to ask. Toward the vessel.

The rings were what it sends.

This is what sends them.

On the long white wall behind him, at the end of the INSIDE column, the queen of hearts hung on its single pin under the lamp. At the end of the last wall, past THE FLOOR, a woman in a wheelchair had written, that afternoon, in her own quick sharp hand, beside her daughter's capitals: I read these every morning. — Z.W.

And far away, at the bottom of the SHIOMI column, under the bell and the boat call and light the lamp on the shore, there was space.

Two months, Yuanshu.

I think the gardener is coming.

Itself.
