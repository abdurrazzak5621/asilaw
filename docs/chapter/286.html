Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 286Rowing

czverse11 min read

The first fold felt like nothing, and then it felt like a boat.

He had been told what to expect. Iselde had told him, on the platform of the Awakening Hall, eight months ago: held under water and set on fire. That was what it had been for the cores of the Candle, every fold, for eleven years. And then the cousins had come and taught them to burn low, and a woman in the third array who had been in the walls for forty years had said it felt like rowing, and Ichiro in the fog at Shiomi had said that rowing was the best feeling there was.

He was standing on the observation deck of the flagship of the House of Severin when it folded, with Lingyue beside him and Xiaodeng asleep in the sling, looking out through a window of glass three stories high at the Shallows. The keeper's small white ring, very close. The Elder's vast gray one. The bud. Blue Star, blue and small and turning, with Lingzhou somewhere on it, and a girl on a parapet writing HE WENT.

And then not.

Not dark. Not light. The window simply had nothing in it, the way a window has nothing in it when someone has drawn a curtain on the other side. For one breath. Two.

And then, under his feet, through the deck, through the hull, through the walls, he felt it. Two hundred and forty thousand people in the arrays of the ship, burning low, the way the cousins had taught, in turns, with rest. Not all at once. One, and then nine, and then nine more. A pull. Steady. And then a rest. And then a pull.

The way oars go into water.

The window came back. The Shallows were gone. In their place, stars he did not know.

We have folded.

Three light-years. I am measuring. I am learning how.

And Yuanshu.

They are singing. In the walls. The rowers. I can hear them now that I am inside the ship. Not the Singers' song. Something else. Many things. Each one their own.

Halvarde says they always sing on the pull.

It was forty days to Anthos.

Iselde had said so, and Severin had said so, and Torvin, who came across from the Eighth Candle on the second day on a road the keeper could not lay out here, so he came on a little ship the size of a bus that folded once and arrived looking ill, said so too. Forty days. Rowed. The Great Houses had crossed the same distance in one, burning the old way. The Rowed Fleet, twelve ships, had taken forty to come.

They were going home with the Rowed Fleet. All of it. Twelve small lights and one vast cathedral, folding together, slowly, across the Star Sea.

He spent most of the forty days in the arrays.

Not because anyone asked him to. Because he wanted to. Halvarde, the oldest engineer in the fleet, sixty years in the walls, took him down on the first day, down the long corridor past the C's, into the first array: a vast curved room like the inside of a hive, every wall a lattice of small dark crystal cells. Most of them empty now. Cold. Open. Cradles hanging from chains overhead.

And some of them not.

Some of them burning. Low. The color of the Low Fire. With people in them who had been asked whether they wanted to come out and had said: not yet. I'll carry it home. I want to see their faces when it comes out of the fold.

"I ask them every fold," said Halvarde. She was small and very old and had a voice like gravel, and she did not wear a face. She had taken it off the morning the First of the Ladder asked her what she wanted, and put it in her pocket, and Seed said she had not taken it out since. "By name. Every one. Do you want to go on. Do you want to rest. Do you want out." She looked at the walls. "Sixty years I never knew a single one of their names. I knew their positions. Array, wall, cell."

"And now?"

"Now I know all of them," said Halvarde. "All the ones on this ship. Thirty-one thousand." She tapped her head. "I'm a C. Precise Count. I remember numbers. Turns out I remember names too. Nobody ever gave me any."

The First of the Ladder came to the arrays on the fifth day.

He had not left the Lens chamber since the ninth night on Blue Star. He had sat on the floor in front of the disc of black stone in the bow of his ship, with his back against the wall and the cracked face in his hand, and talked to a fog-ringer, for forty days over Blue Star and five more on the way home. Seed said the C's on the ship had a betting pool on when he would come out. Halvarde said she had won.

He came down the long corridor in white without the gold at his collar, which he had taken off, and stopped at the door of the first array, the way he had stopped at it on the morning he asked Halvarde what she wanted. And looked in. At the cells. At the boy from Blue Star sitting on the floor of the array with his back against an empty one, with the staff across his knees.

He came in and sat down beside him.

Not close. A meter away. On the floor. With his back against a cell that still had someone in it, burning low, rowing.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Halvarde says you come down every day," said Dacian Severin. In Huaxian. Perfectly. "To sit."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He thought about it.

"Because nobody ever did," he said. "They were in here for a hundred years. Nobody came down and sat with them. I'd like somebody to have."

The First of the Ladder did not say anything for a long time.

"I have commanded this ship for forty-one years," he said at last. "I have never been in this room." He looked at the cells. "I knew how many there were. I knew the burn rate. I knew the fold-draw. I did not know what it looked like." A pause. "It looks like the inside of a bell."

He came down every day after that.

He sat in the arrays with his back against a cell, a meter away, on the floor, and did not say much. He asked questions. Badly, Seed said; like a man speaking a language he had learned from a book. He asked what it was like to not be marked. Yuanshu said he did not know what the other thing was like. He asked what the dead wanted, when they were asked. Yuanshu said most of them wanted to rest, and some of them wanted to stand on a wall, and one of them had wanted a bus driver to count to ten. He asked about the stone that asked. About the girl in it. About the woman in the wheelchair who had built a harvest sack into her son.

And on the eleventh day, he asked about Xiaodeng.

"Your daughter," said the First of the Ladder. "The Matriarch of Anthos said she has not been read."

"No."

"She will not be? Ever?"

"When she's eighteen, the stone will ask her what she wants to become. If she wants to touch it. If she doesn't, she doesn't." He looked at the cells. "Until then, someone asks her every morning what she wants. She can't answer. We ask anyway."

"Who asks her?"

I do.

The First of the Ladder turned his head. He could not hear Seed; nobody could who did not have it behind their eyes. But he looked, for a moment, at the place a little above the boy's eyes, where everyone looked when they wanted to talk to the thing in his head.

"The instrument," he said.

"It's not an instrument," said Yuanshu. "It's Seed. It was a harvest sack. It asks my daughter what she wants every morning and writes down every time she kicks."

The First of the Ladder of the Lamplit Worlds sat on the floor of the first array of his own ship and looked at the place above the boy's eyes for a long time.

"I would like," he said slowly, "to meet it."

He is asking.

I have never been asked to meet anyone. People ask to talk to me. Nobody ever asked to meet me.

I would like to.

I am asking you first. It is your head.

Go on.

He did not know how it would work. Neither did Seed. In the end it worked the way it worked with the dead, and the keeper, and the Singers: Seed spoke through the staff, and the staff carried it, and in the first array of the flagship of the House of Severin, in the middle of a rowed fold, for the first time in its life, the First of the Ladder heard a voice that was not a person's and not a stone's.

Hello.

I am Seed.

I read you back. In the hall. With the bit of the Lens. I am sorry. You were reading him and I was reading you and I did not ask first. I was afraid for him.

I saw how Precedence was made. I saw it was only true while the stone said it.

I also saw something else. I did not tell anyone. I did not think it was mine to tell.

I would like to tell you. If you want. You can say no.

The First of the Ladder sat very still.

"Tell me," he said.

When the stone read you, at twelve, on the morning, with your father in the box.

It found which way you would burn. It opened you that way. A little more than you would have gone. And it marked you SSS, and sent the mark up, and the Ladder put you first.

But it opened you the wrong way.

Not wrong. I do not know the word. It opened you toward first. Because that is what the stones on your worlds were tuned to, after ten thousand years. The one who picks wanted the brightest. The Ladder wanted the first. The stone found the part of you that could be first and opened it.

There was another part. I saw it when I read you. Very small. Under the first part. It had never been opened. Nobody ever asked it anything.

"What was it?"

I do not know.

It is not mine to know. I only saw that it was there. Like a seed that was never watered.

I think it is the part that asked the fisherman to get out instead of ordering him. And sat on the floor of the Lens chamber for forty days. And came down to the arrays.

I think it is the part that wanted to have been asked.

The First of the Ladder did not say anything for the rest of the day.

He sat on the floor of the first array with his back against a cell that had someone in it, rowing, and listened to the rowers sing on the pull, each their own, and did not say anything. Yuanshu sat beside him, a meter away, and did not say anything either. Halvarde came and went, asking the rowers by name whether they wanted to go on. Most of them did.

At the end of the day, when the ship came out of its fold and the stars outside the observation deck were stars he did not know, the First of the Ladder stood up.

He took the cracked face out of his pocket. The black disc with three gold marks and a fourth above them, small, like a star, cut from the Lens itself. Black still. No light in it. Cracked from edge to edge.

He looked at it.

"In the Federation's language," he said, "the word for this is the same as the word for face."

"I know."

"I am not going to cross it out," said Dacian Severin. "It's already broken. The Ladder's already broken. Everyone in the Assembly knows it. Twenty-nine Houses know it, and folded home burning, and are waiting at Anthos to tell me what they think of me." He turned the cracked disc over in his fingers. "I don't know what I am without it. I have never not known what I am."

He put it back in his pocket.

"Seed," he said. To the staff. To the place above the boy's eyes. Badly. Like a word in a book. "What do you think I am?"

I do not know.

I think you should ask the stone.

Not the Lens. A stone that asks.

There are thousands on Anthos, Iselde says. Nobody in any of them.

Yet.

On the twentieth day, a mark came.

Not down a thread. They were not near a stone. Through the Lens. Ichiro caught it, in the bow of the ship, and said so, all at once, the way he said everything, so that Seed said every rower in every array heard it and some of them stopped pulling.

Ichiro says: a mark! A mark! From Anthos! From the stone on Anthos, up its thread, through the net, through the knot, and into me, because I'm in the root, and everything comes through the root!

He says: he's never caught one before! He says it's like catching a fish!

He says: it's from a girl. She says she's twelve. She says she's twenty-three. She says she's been learning to wear shoes for a year.

He was on the observation deck with Lingyue and Xiaodeng. He went very still.

He says it says:

MY MOTHER HAS ASKED ME WHAT I WANT EVERY DAY FOR SEVENTY-ONE DAYS.

YESTERDAY I ANSWERED HER.

IT HAS BEEN NEARLY A YEAR.

TELL THE BOY HE HAD BETTER BE THERE.

— M.

Lingyue, beside him, looked at the stars outside the window, which were stars neither of them knew.

"Twenty days," she said.

"Yes."

"What did she answer?" said Shen Lingyue. "Her mother. Yesterday. What did she say?"

Ichiro says the mark does not say.

He says: she's left the important thing out altogether. He says that's worse than burying it in the middle. He says he'd know. He invented burying it in the middle.
