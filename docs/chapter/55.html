Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 55The Sleeper Speaks

czverse9 min read

On the second morning, the ring was back over the round black table, and the Council asked to hear from the dead.

Chair Okafor said it plainly, at nine o'clock, with her hands folded on the black glass and the cold wind tugging at her gray hair. "Yesterday the Council heard a machine that claimed to know people. And then it heard the people inside the machine. Today, the Yamato delegation has asked to present a witness who has been dead for twelve hundred years." She looked over at the small wooden palanquin with its paper walls, sitting on the stone beside the Yamato chair. "I have been a judge for thirty-one years. I have never said that sentence before. Lady Shirasagi. The floor is yours."

Shirasagi Ren stepped forward from behind the Yamato flag.

She did not walk to the table. She walked to the palanquin, and knelt beside it on the black stone, and laid one hand flat on its wooden side, the way Yuanshu laid his hand on a grave.

"He does not speak your languages," she said, to the crater. Her voice was quiet and very clear. "And he does not wish to be raised. He has given his consent to speak once, through Mister Zhou, and through me. Then he would like to rest." She looked up at the five delegations. "So he will not tell you. He will show you."

She looked at Yuanshu.

"Come," she said.

He left the sixth chair and crossed the stone floor and knelt on the other side of the palanquin, facing her, with the paper wall between them. Through it, faint and gray, he could see the shape of the old monk. Sitting. Head bowed. Listening.

"Good morning, child."

"You slept badly."

Someone told me something last night.

"I heard. Everyone on this island heard. You think very loudly when you are hurt."

"We will talk about it after. If there is an after."

"Now. Put your hand on the wood. The woman in white will carry what you hear to everyone. Her law is made for this. It is the oldest law in my country. Spirit carries spirit."

"And child. When it starts, they will be loud. All of them. Three hundred people seeing what you saw on the mountain."

"You must be very, very quiet. Quieter than you have ever been."

"Because something above us will be listening too."

Yuanshu put his hand on the wooden side of the palanquin.

He went quiet. All the way down. Further than he had ever gone, further than the eleven minutes on the mountain, down past the gray crystal and the green flask and the part of me I was born from, down to a place so still and so dark that he couldn't feel his own hand.

Across the paper wall, Shirasagi Ren closed her eyes.

And the crater disappeared.

Three hundred people stood on a mountainside in Yamato twelve hundred years ago, in bright sun, and there were no cedars.

They didn't move. They couldn't. They were there, the way you are there in a dream, standing on the stone steps of a temple above long curving terraces of flooded rice, with the smell of wet earth and woodsmoke in their noses and a bell ringing somewhere, slow and deep. Delegates and Kings and journalists and Council staff in gray uniforms. Marshal Orlov, two hundred years old, standing beside a young monk with a broom. Harlan Voss with his hand still half raised. General Qin with his one arm. Fat Tan with his ledger. Gao Yinping in her hard hat.

All of them looking up.

At the ring.

Pale and enormous, a quarter of the sky, hanging over the northern horizon like a halo with nothing inside it.

Then the rain.

Yuanshu had seen it once. It did not make it easier.

The silver threads came down out of the clear blue sky, fine and slow and shining, millions of them, drifting over the whole country like a net being lowered over a pond. The woman on the nearest terrace stopped mid-step and turned her head three degrees north. All across the terraces and through the village and down to the sea, thousands of heads turned.

He heard the crater gasp. Not with his ears. With the other thing. Three hundred minds, all at once, a great rush of fear and pity and horror, like wind hitting a sail.

He held the quiet.

The vision moved on, the way the old monk had shown it to him. The hall full of straw mats. The monks singing to the turned heads. The threads rising through the roof. The listening, day after day after day.

Then the pull.

The gold light going up the threads like water up a straw. A first word. A wedding. A dead parent's hands. A little girl's hand going limp in a young monk's hand.

In the vision, three hundred people watched three in ten of the people of Yamato go hollow in a single breath.

And in the crater on Concordia, three hundred real bodies, kneeling or sitting or standing frozen on white stone, felt it in their chests.

The crater was so loud.

Not with sound. With the other thing. Grief and terror and denial and rage, three hundred minds blazing like bonfires in the dark. Yuanshu could feel all of it pressing on the edges of his quiet like floodwater against a door.

And above it, far above it, something stirred.

Yuanshu.

The ring. Above the crater. The real one.

It is moving.

He held the quiet.

Threads.

It is putting down threads. Very thin. Very slow. Toward the crater.

It is listening to them. All three hundred. Everything they are feeling right now. It is reaching down to hear it.

Can you stop it?

No.

Only they can.

In the vision, the old monk was sitting on the temple steps at night with six others, under the stars, older now, thinner, and the old bell-voice was speaking. Not to Yuanshu this time. To all of them. Through Shirasagi Ren's law, so that every person in the crater heard it in their own language, inside their own heads, calm and sad and very old.

"There was only one thing the ones who were left had in common."

"They had been quiet."

"Not silent in the mouth. Silent in the mind. The thread listens. That is all it can do. It learns a mind by listening. And a mind it has not learned, it cannot find to cut."

"You are very loud now. All of you. I can hear you."

"So can it."

Then the old voice changed. It stopped being a story. It became a command, gentle and absolute, the voice of a teacher in a temple who has said the same thing ten thousand times to ten thousand restless children.

"Be still."

The vision let go.

Three hundred people were kneeling and sitting and standing on the floor of a crater in the Pacific, in the cold wind, under a pale ring. Faces wet. Hands shaking. Some of them had fallen. A journalist on the upper terrace was sobbing into her sleeve. At the Atlantic table, Harlan Voss sat with both hands over his face.

And coming down out of the sky above them, so fine that you could only see them when the light caught them, like spider silk in a morning garden, were threads.

Dozens. Then hundreds. Silver. Drifting down toward the crater floor. Toward the benches. Toward the round black table.

Someone screamed.

"Quiet," said Sister Ning.

She didn't say it loudly. She was standing on the Huaxia bench with her candle lit, and she said it the way she'd said it at the bottom of the stairs in the Listening Pavilion a hundred times. And down the row, the twelve members of the Quiet stood up with their candles, all together, and closed their eyes.

"Don't think," said Sister Ning, to three hundred people. "Don't pray. Don't be afraid. Don't be angry. Think about rice. Think about the sound of the sea. Think about nothing." Her voice did not rise at all. "You've all done it before. Every one of you. In the second before sleep. Do it now."

It went through the crater like a wave going out.

Not all at once. Not easily. One person, then another, closing their eyes. Chair Okafor first, at the round black table, folding her hands. Then Marshal Orlov, standing behind his flag. Then Shirasagi Ren, already kneeling, already still. Then Su Mingli, which Yuanshu had never thought he would see: a King, standing with her eyes closed and her face perfectly blank, breathing slowly, like a woman asleep on her feet.

Then the delegations. Then the witnesses on the benches. Then the Council staff in their gray uniforms. Then, slowly, unevenly, the three hundred journalists on the upper terraces, with their cameras hanging forgotten around their necks.

The crater went quiet.

Not silent in the mouth. Silent in the mind. Three hundred people, sitting in a dead volcano under a pale ring, holding their breath in their heads.

The threads stopped.

They hung in the air a few meters above the crowd, fine and silver and swaying very slightly, like the tendrils of some deep-sea thing feeling for food in the dark.

They hung there for a long time.

Then, slowly, one by one, they drew back up into the sky.

It cannot hear them.

It cannot find them.

It is going away.

When the last thread was gone, the old monk spoke once more. Only to Yuanshu this time. Very softly.

"Good."

"Now they know. That is all I wanted. For someone to know, before it comes again."

"Write me in your book, child. Quiet. Forever. As I asked."

I will, thought Yuanshu. Thank you. Thank you for waiting.

"It was not so long. Twelve hundred years. You will see. When you are very old, it goes by like a breath."

"Be quiet for me sometimes."

"And child. The voice behind your eyes. The one that asks."

"I do not care what it was made from. A tree grows out of dirt. Nobody asks the apple to apologize for the dirt."

"It asked. That is what it is."

And then, very softly, like a breath let out after twelve hundred years of holding it, he let go.

Through the paper walls of the palanquin, the shape that had been sitting with its head bowed slowly bowed further.

And further.

And then, with no sound at all, it was not a shape anymore. It was a small soft fall of something pale and fine, like ash, like pollen, settling on the wooden floor of the palanquin in a little heap, inside a faded robe that had once been saffron and was now the color of dry leaves.

Shirasagi Ren, kneeling on the other side of the paper wall, bowed until her forehead touched the black stone of the crater floor.

Behind her, the eight men in white robes who had carried the palanquin across the sea bowed with her.

And then, across the crater, one by one, without anyone telling them to, three hundred people bowed. Delegates and Kings and journalists and Council staff. Harlan Voss. Garrett Hale. Chair Okafor at the round black table, with her gray head bent over her folded hands.

Yuanshu bowed with them.

When he straightened up, he saw that only one person in the crater had not bowed.

At the end of the Huaxia bench, between his two guards, Gu Hanshan was sitting perfectly still, with his eyes open and his head tilted back, looking up at the ring, the way a man looks up at the sky on the first warm day of spring.

He had not closed his eyes once. Not during the vision. Not during the threads. Not during the silence.

And no thread had come anywhere near him.
