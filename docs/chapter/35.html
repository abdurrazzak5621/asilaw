Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 35First Contract

czverse10 min read

Jinhua Tower was thirty-four floors of cheap apartments on the east side of Lingzhou, and at 11:52 on a Thursday night, its eighteenth floor exploded.

Gas, they said later. A kitchen. Somebody's old stove.

It didn't matter what it was. What mattered was the cladding. The outside of Jinhua Tower was covered from top to bottom in panels of a cheap gray composite that had been illegal for ten years and that nobody had ever paid to remove, and when the fire on the eighteenth floor found the panels, it went up the side of the building like a match flaring up the side of a matchbox.

In four minutes, the top half of Jinhua Tower was a torch.

Yuanshu saw it from the Tianshu observatory roof. A column of orange in the east, so bright it lit the undersides of the clouds. He was already moving before the sirens started.

The world folded. Su Mingli had taught him a crude version of it in the last week: not the clean page-turn of a King, but a lurching, stomach-dropping jump of a few kilometers at a time, like falling sideways down a flight of stairs. He came out of it on the roof of a noodle shop on Jinhua Road with his ears ringing and his knees buckling, and the heat hit him like opening an oven.

The street below was chaos.

Fire engines in a half circle, their ladders reaching up into the smoke and falling short, far short, at the twelfth floor. Defense Corps awakeners in silver fire-coats, Water Law users, standing in a line on the pavement with their arms raised, pouring great twisting ropes of water up into the flames. The water hit the burning cladding and turned to steam and was gone. Police tape. A crowd, hundreds of people, faces lit orange, phones held up. And above all of it, the tower: a black spine of concrete, with fire pouring up its east face in a solid roaring sheet from the eighteenth floor to the roof, and in the windows above the fire, tiny figures. Waving.

Floors nineteen to thirty-four. My estimate: one hundred and sixty people above the fire.

Both stairwells are full of smoke above the sixteenth floor. Temperature in the east stairwell: over 400 degrees.

A living human cannot go up those stairs.

At the edge of the crowd, a woman in a silver fire-coat was shouting into a radio.

She was in her thirties, with soot on her face and her helmet pushed back, and both her hands were glowing blue: a Water Law user, pouring everything she had into a twisting column of water that went up to the fourteenth floor and stopped there, as if it had hit a ceiling. Her arms were shaking. Her nose was bleeding.

"I can't get it higher! I can't get it higher!" She dropped her arms and the column fell, splashing, and she turned and shouted at someone behind her. "Where's the aerial unit? Where's... Dad!"

Yuanshu looked where she was looking.

A man was lying on the pavement by the nearest fire engine, on his back on a silver thermal blanket. An older man, sixty or so, big and broad, in a faded fire-service jacket with no insignia on it, the kind retired men wear to remind themselves. Two paramedics were kneeling over him. One of them was doing chest compressions. The other was holding a pair of paddles.

The paddles came down. The man's body jerked.

The paramedic looked at the monitor. Shook his head. Did it again.

The woman in the silver coat dropped to her knees on the wet pavement beside him.

"Dad. Dad, you idiot, you're retired, you're retired..."

Fang Tiezhu. Sixty-one. Retired chief of the Eastern District Fire Service. Thirty-four years.

He lives two streets from here. He heard the explosion. He ran here in his slippers and tried to go into the west stairwell by himself. The firefighters pulled him back out at the sixth floor. His heart stopped on the pavement.

That was four minutes ago.

The paramedic with the paddles sat back on his heels. He looked at the monitor for a long moment. Then he looked at his watch and said a time, quietly, to his partner, who wrote it down.

The woman in the silver coat did not make a sound. She just knelt there with her hand on her father's chest and stared at nothing.

I need to tell you something.

Eight days ago, Fang Tiezhu stood in the queue outside the Ossuary for three hours.

He chose YES.

He recorded a note.

Yuanshu walked through the police tape.

A policeman started toward him, then saw his face in the firelight and stopped. Behind him, the fold of the world opened twice more in quick succession. Old Seven came out of it already walking. Then Iron Wall Zhao, his empty black shield on his back. On the roof of the noodle shop, a figure of gray ash crouched with a bow across its knees.

Yuanshu knelt on the wet pavement across from the woman in the silver coat. The heat from the tower was enormous, even here, a dry pressure on his face like standing in front of a furnace door.

"Fang Yu?" he said.

She looked up at him. It took her a moment to recognize him. When she did, something moved across her face that was not quite hope and not quite horror.

"Your father signed the Ledger," Yuanshu said. "Eight days ago. He chose yes. He left a message." He kept his voice steady. "Do you want to hear it?"

She stared at him.

Then she nodded. Once.

Playing.

Yuanshu said the words out loud, exactly as Seed gave them to him. A big rough voice, cheerful and gruff, recorded in a quiet vault on East Market Road with a box of tissues on the table.

"Fang Tiezhu. Thirty-four years, Eastern District. If I'm any use when I'm gone, use me. Don't wait for a committee. Don't wait for my daughter to stop crying, she'll cry anyway. If there's a fire, and there's people, and you can get me there, get me there. And tell Yu her stance is too wide. She'll know what I mean."

Fang Yu put both hands over her mouth.

Then she laughed. A terrible, cracked, sobbing laugh.

"It is," she said through her fingers. "It's too wide. He's said it every day for ten years." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist and left a long streak of soot across her cheek. "The Accord. You need the family. That's me. I'm the family." Her voice cracked and steadied. "Yes. God help me. Go. Get him there."

There was no candle. No Quiet. No time.

Across the street, a young paramedic, the one who had written down the time, stepped forward. She was twenty at most, white-faced, with blood on her gloves.

"I'm a witness," she said. Her voice was shaking. "I don't know any of you. I heard her say yes. I heard the recording." She swallowed, and pulled a small burned-down paper-cup candle out of the pocket of her uniform, crushed flat, the kind you could find all along the gutters of East Market Road. "And I'm Quiet. My whole family is. I was at the bridge." She held it up. "Does that count?"

"It counts," said Yuanshu.

"Do I need to sign something?"

"Later," said Yuanshu.

He put his hand on the old fireman's chest.

The echo was right there. Not faint. Not far down a well. Right at the surface, fierce and bright and furious, like a man who has been told to sit down at the side of a fire and is having none of it.

Chief Fang, Yuanshu said. There are a hundred and sixty people above the eighteenth floor. Both stairs are full of smoke. The living can't go up.

The answer came before he'd finished asking.

He says: "What are you waiting for?"

[Calamity Raise]

Fang Tiezhu sat up.

He was not a skeleton. He was four minutes dead, warm still, a big gray-haired man in a faded jacket and slippers with his eyes open and a pale gray light kindled in them. He sat up on the silver blanket, and looked at his daughter, and reached out one big hand and pushed her helmet straight on her head, the way he must have done a thousand times.

Then he stood up, and turned to the tower, and looked up at it the way a carpenter looks at a job.

He does not need a Law Core. Not tonight. He needs to do one thing.

He knows this building. He inspected it in 2014. He wrote a report saying the cladding would kill everyone in it. Nobody read the report.

He knows where the fire doors are. He knows which landings have windows. He knows which way the smoke moves.

The dead do not breathe.

Fang Tiezhu walked into Jinhua Tower through the main doors, in his slippers, and the smoke swallowed him.

Old Seven went in after him.

Iron Wall Zhao went in after them both.

From the pavement, nobody could see anything.

Only the smoke pouring out of the lobby doors. Only the fire roaring up the east face. The crowd went quiet. The water mages lowered their arms. Fang Yu stood at the tape with her fists clenched and her face lit orange, not moving.

Yuanshu stood beside her with his eyes closed.

He could see it. Not with his eyes. Through three threads of gray light running into the burning building, he felt them climb.

The west stairwell. Black smoke, so thick it was like climbing through ink. Heat that would have killed a living man in a minute. Three dead men going up the stairs two at a time, not coughing, not slowing. Fang Tiezhu in front, one big hand on the wall, counting landings out loud though nobody could hear.

The sixteenth floor. A fire door jammed with heat. Iron Wall Zhao put his shoulder into it, and it came off its hinges.

The twenty-first floor. A corridor with a family huddled at the far end with wet towels over their faces. A mother, two children, a grandmother. Fang Tiezhu picked up both children, one under each arm, the way firemen do. Old Seven picked up the grandmother. The mother wrapped her towel around her face and took hold of the back of Iron Wall Zhao's ribs, and the dead captain lifted his empty shield over her head against the falling ash, and they went down.

And came back up.

And went down.

And came back up.

The first people came out of the lobby doors at 12:31.

Out of the smoke, onto the pavement, into the lights of the fire engines: a dead fireman in slippers with a child under each arm. The crowd made a sound Yuanshu would never forget. Not a cheer. Something lower and bigger than that, something like the sound the sea makes.

Paramedics ran. Blankets. Oxygen. Fang Tiezhu set the children down on the silver thermal blanket he had died on, and turned around, and went back in.

They came out every four minutes after that.

Old Seven with a man over each shoulder. Iron Wall Zhao walking backward out of the smoke with his shield up and six people sheltered under it, holding on to his ribs, his arms, each other. Fang Tiezhu with an old woman in his arms, wrapped in a curtain, singing to her. Seed said afterward that he had been singing the whole time. An old fire service song. Badly.

On the roof of the noodle shop, Hundred-Arrow Lin shot out the windows of the burning upper floors one by one, so the smoke could vent and the trapped people could breathe, and never once touched a person behind the glass.

One hundred and forty-one.

One hundred and fifty-three.

One hundred and sixty.

That is all of them.

At 2:10, Fang Tiezhu came out of Jinhua Tower for the last time.

He walked out of the smoke alone, slowly, his slippers gone, his jacket scorched, his gray hair singed. He walked across the wet pavement to where his daughter stood at the tape, and stopped in front of her, and looked down at her for a long time with the pale gray light in his eyes.

Then he put his hands on her shoulders, and turned her slightly, and nudged her left foot in with the side of his bare foot. Ten centimeters.

Too wide.

Fang Yu broke.

He held her while she cried, there at the tape, in front of the tower and the crowd and the cameras and the hundred and sixty people sitting on the pavement wrapped in silver blankets. He held her for a long time.

Then he looked over her shoulder at Yuanshu.

He says he's done.

He says: "Good job, everybody. Let me lie down now."

Yuanshu nodded.

Rest. Lie down. You can stop now. It's over.

The pale light in Fang Tiezhu's eyes went out, gently, and his daughter caught him as he fell, and lowered him down onto the silver blanket, and knelt beside him on the wet pavement with her hand on his chest.

The young paramedic was standing beside Yuanshu with a clipboard, crying. She held out a pen.

"Where do I sign?" she said.

First Ledger contract fulfilled.

YES. Witnessed by a stranger. Family present.

One hundred and sixty living.

I would like to record his note somewhere permanent. He was right about the stance.
