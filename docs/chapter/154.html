Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 154Three Kilometers Down

czverse10 min read

The first problem was that sound does not go straight down.

Adjutant Xu found that out at midnight, standing at the end of the harbor wall with his cane, humming into the sea.

He had been a Signaler when he was alive. Sound Law, eleven percent. The kind of small, useful law that let a radio operator hear a set through a wall and make himself heard across a battlefield without shouting, and that had become, when Seed and Yuanshu forged it into a Law Core, the Ninefold Relay: the thing that carried a question down the Asking Lattice from one soldier to the next nine and back up again. On land it reached twenty-five thousand dead from a thousand kilometers away.

In the sea, it reached about forty meters, and then it came apart.

It scatters.

Sound goes four times faster in water than in air. That is good. But the sea is not one thing. It is layers. Warm on top, cold below, salt and less salt, currents going different ways at different depths. Every layer bends the sound a little. By two hundred meters it is going sideways. By a kilometer it is everywhere and nowhere.

Adjutant Xu says it is like shouting down a well full of curtains.

"Can you correct for it?"

I can calculate it. Every layer. I have a model of the water column now, thanks to Ifeoma.

But calculating where a sound will go is not the same as making it go there.

Ifeoma Adeyemi had been sitting at the end of the wall in her wheelchair the whole time, with her blanket over her knees, watching the black water. She had not said a word since dusk. She had promised to teach him, and she had said she would be slow, and she was being slow on purpose; he could feel it. She was waiting for him to get as far as he could get by himself.

He got as far as this.

"I can't make the sea simpler," he said. "But you can."

"Can I?"

"You made a circle of water flat as glass yesterday. Two hundred meters wide. Not one ripple." He looked down at the sea. "Not calm. Still. You told the water inside the circle to stop doing everything it was doing." He turned to her. "Could you make it narrower? A meter wide. And deeper. All the way down."

The old King looked at him for a while with her very bright black eyes.

"A pipe," she said.

"A pipe of still water. Three kilometers long. No layers. No currents. Nothing to bend the sound." He was working it out as he said it. "Adjutant Xu sings into the top. The question goes down the pipe. At the bottom, the Field's waiting, and Jiang Lan, and the drowned can hear it."

"And how do they answer?"

"The same way. Up the pipe."

Ifeoma Adeyemi was quiet.

"Do you know what you're asking me to do, child?"

"No," said Yuanshu. "You're going to tell me."

"I'm going to tell you." She folded her hands. "Holding a circle of water still for a minute, on the surface, is nothing. I can do it asleep. Holding a column of water still from the surface to the bottom of the shelf is holding up three kilometers of sea against everything the sea wants to do. Every current. Every layer. The morning tide coming up underneath, which is the one thing you most need, because that is what your thing is riding." She looked down at the black water. "For how long?"

To ask four thousand one hundred and nine. One at a time, the way he asks. With answers.

About six hours. If they answer quickly.

The dead do not usually answer quickly. The cold ones answer slowest.

"Six hours," said Ifeoma. Her mouth moved at one corner. "I have not done anything for six hours since I was a hundred and fifty." She put her thin hands flat on the blanket. "Bring the old man and his cane. We'll start at one."

It took them until three to get the pipe right.

The first one went down crooked. Ifeoma made it, a column of perfectly still water a meter across going straight down from the end of the harbor wall into the black, and Adjutant Xu hummed into it, and the question went down a kilometer and a half and hit the morning current coming up off the shelf and bent away sideways like smoke in a wind.

The current is moving the bottom of the pipe. Not the top. The top is where she is. The bottom is three kilometers away from her and the sea is pushing on it.

The second one went down straight and broke at two kilometers, because Ifeoma's hold on it thinned out the deeper it went, the way a voice thins out the further it travels, and the sea came in through the thin place.

The third time, Seed did the thing it had learned on the Western Pass, with the Banked Field: it did not try to hold anything. It gave Ifeoma the shape.

It laid the water column out in its head, every layer from the warm surface to the cold dark bottom, and showed her the places where the sea pushed hardest, and the places where it barely pushed at all, and the exact depth at which the morning current would hit the pipe and from which side. Not numbers. She did not want numbers. A picture of the water, from inside, the way Seed saw it. Where to be strong. Where she could be lazy.

The old King looked at it for a long moment with her eyes shut.

"Oh," she said. "That's very clever, child."

It is your idea. I only drew it.

"That's what clever is," said Ifeoma Adeyemi, and made the pipe.

At three in the morning, Adjutant Xu sang the question down three kilometers of still water to the bottom of the sea.

Not words. The Relay did not use words. It used the shape of a question, the shape Yuanshu had given it and a tired necromancer on a wall had given Grandpa Xu sixty years before, carried on a single low note that the old man held in his empty chest without breathing, because he no longer needed to. At the bottom of the pipe the Field caught it, and the staff in Yuanshu's hands at the top of the wall caught the Field, and Jiang Lan's dry voice said, All right. I've got it. I'm going down, and went.

I'm sorry nobody came before. Something is eating you. It came back last night. It's going to rise up through the sea and bite the towns you came from. I want to stop it, at the bottom, where it is. Will you help? You can say no. You can say quiet. You can say you only want to speak. Nobody will ask you twice.

Then they waited.

The answers came up the pipe very slowly. One at a time, out of the dark. Cold dead answered slowest, Seed had said, and these were the coldest dead Yuanshu had ever asked; some of them had been lying on the bottom of the sea for a hundred years in the cold and the dark, and their embers were so small and so slow that Seed had to wait for each one to finish a word before it could tell what the word was going to be.

The first answer took four minutes.

Yes.

A fisherman. From Kesse. He went over the side in a storm forty-one years ago. He says his name is Yaw.

He says: is it the thing that ate the bay?

Yes.

He says: then yes.

They came faster after the first hour, the way water comes faster through a crack once it starts.

Fishermen. Mostly fishermen, from Port Abiri and Kesse and Sandu and the small villages between them that did not have names on the chart. Some had gone down alone, in storms, off small boats. Some had gone down together, whole crews, the boat and the nets and all. A cargo ship's engineer from somewhere north, who did not speak the language of the Southern Compact and had to be asked twice. A girl of fourteen who had swum out too far on a dare in the year the Steles fell, and who said she wanted to help because I was a very good swimmer, actually, everyone said so.

And the ferry.

Sixty years ago, in a storm off Kesse. Two hundred and twelve people on board. A wedding party going home. The captain had gone down with it, at the wheel, and two hundred and twelve embers had lain together on the bottom of the sea in the cold, in the shape of a ship that had long since rotted away around them, waiting for someone to come and ask them anything.

They answered together. All two hundred and twelve, in one slow chord up the pipe, the way the wedding party had probably sung on the deck that afternoon before the storm.

Yes. Most of them. A hundred and eighty-one.

Thirty-one say quiet. The bride's grandmother is one of them. She says she has been dancing for sixty years, in the dark, and she is tired.

The captain says yes. Her name is Captain Efua Asante. She says she should not have sailed. She says the harbor master told her the glass was falling and she went anyway, because the bride's father was paying double.

She says: let me do something useful with that.

He did not keep count. Seed did. The answers came up the pipe one by one through the small hours of the morning, and Seed wrote each one down, and Adjutant Xu held his note, and the oldest King on Blue Star sat in her wheelchair at the end of the harbor wall holding three kilometers of sea perfectly still with her eyes shut and her thin hands flat on the blanket, and did not move.

At half past five, with the sky going gray in the east, Seed went still.

Yuanshu.

There is one asking for her.

He looked at Ifeoma.

She had heard. He knew she had heard, because the pipe of still water at the end of the wall shivered, for the first time since three in the morning, along its whole length, the way a held breath shivers.

He says he went over the side off the point, here, outside the bay. A hundred and forty years ago. In the dry season. A squall came down off the hills very fast and the boat went over and came up again and he did not.

He says the boat went home on its own. On the current. He says it always knew the way.

He says his name is Emeka.

He says: Ify. You kept the boat.

The old King did not open her eyes.

She sat at the end of the harbor wall, in the gray light, with her blanket over her knees and the green headwrap pulled down against the dawn wind, holding three kilometers of still water in her hands, and she did not let go of it. Yuanshu could feel that through the Field. She did not let go. The pipe went on standing in the sea, straight down to the bottom, to the place off the point where a man she had married when she was eighty years old had gone over the side of a white fishing boat in a squall, and lain in the cold for a hundred and forty years, four hundred kilometers from her palace, and never once been asked anything by anyone.

"I looked for you," said Ifeoma Adeyemi.

Her voice did not shake. It was dry and level and very old.

"I looked for you for a year. I held the whole sea still, every morning, for a year, and listened. I couldn't find you. I thought the current had taken you north." She was quiet. "I didn't know you were here. Right here. Off the point."

He says: it's very dark down here. You'd never have found me. You were looking up.

He says: is that boy asking me what I want?

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

He says: I want to stay with the others and fight the thing that ate the bay. He says he has been lying next to it for a year and it has been eating his friends.

He says yes.

He says: Ify. Don't come down. It's cold.

The pipe of still water stood in the sea for another minute.

Then, very gently, the old King let it go. The column of still water at the end of the harbor wall dissolved into the ordinary sea, ripple by ripple, the way a sugar cube dissolves in tea, and the morning swell came in over the place where it had been, and the sun came up over the hills behind Port Abiri on the second day.

Yes: two thousand eight hundred and forty-six.

Only to speak: six hundred and three. I have all of it. It will take a day to read out.

Quiet: six hundred and sixty.

Everyone in the Field has been asked.

Forty-nine hours until the first trunk surfaces.

Ifeoma Adeyemi sat back in her wheelchair and opened her eyes and looked out at the sea, at the place past the point where the water turned dark.

"He was always telling me what to do," she said. "For eleven years. Ify, don't stand there. Ify, it's cold. Ify, you'll catch your death." She folded her shaking hands on the blanket. "I was ninety-one when he died. I was already a King. I could have drowned the whole coast if I'd wanted to." Her mouth moved at the corner, very slightly. "He still thought I needed telling."
