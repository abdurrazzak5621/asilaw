Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 68Three Hundred and Twelve

czverse10 min read

The sound hit the plateau like a wave hitting a seawall.

It came out of Mercer's split face, out of the rows of teeth and the throat of cloudy crystal and the hundred small mouths inside it. Not the Monarch's long slow wail. Something higher. Sharper. A shriek like metal tearing, so loud that the fire went flat, so loud that the frost on the glass leapt up off the ground in a glittering cloud, so loud that Yuanshu felt it in the bones of his face.

It was trying to make them think.

And all at once, out from under the hem of the very good gray coat, out of the sleeves, out of the split collar, the threads came.

Not drifting. Not falling like rain. Lashing. Hundreds of them, silver, fast as whips, cracking out across the plateau toward every warm thing in the firelight.

"Down!"

Su Mingli's voice. The air bent. A wall of crushing gravity slammed down between the fire and the living like a pane of invisible glass, and fifty threads hit it and bowed and slid sideways, sparking, into the frozen ground.

But not all of them.

A thread came around the edge of the wall, low, along the glass, toward Mo Xing.

Mo Shi screamed his brother's name a full three seconds before it reached him. Mo Xing folded. The air beside him rippled, and the thread went into the fold and came out of the other side two hundred meters away, lashing harmlessly at empty glass.

Another thread, toward Bai Lu. Lingyue was already there. A flash of white-gold. A violin-string snap. The thread recoiled, smoking.

Another, toward Yan Shuang. Sergeant Sokolova knocked her flat and lay on top of her, again, forehead to forehead, whispering in Northern.

Another, toward Qin Hao. He did not duck. He raised one hand, and fire burst out of his palm, a white-hot flare of Western War Zone flame, and the thread went into it and shriveled like a hair in a candle.

"It burns!" he shouted. "Fire works too!"

"Don't burn the ones that go back to the hollows!" Lingyue was cutting, cutting, needle-point light, eyes half-closed. "Only the loose ones! Only the ones coming at us!"

And the dead walked through it all as if it were a summer breeze.

Third Battalion did not need an order. They had heard Yuanshu say it on Concordia. Hold the wall. Eighty-four old soldiers of the eastern line came forward in their rows, gray bones and rags of greatcoats, and the lashing threads passed through them, through ribs and skulls and empty eye sockets, and found nothing, and did not slow them at all.

They formed up around the living. A ring. Shoulder to shoulder. Facing out.

And Mercer could not see them.

He stood by the fire with his face split open and his threads whipping across the plateau, and the soldiers of Third Battalion walked straight up to him, close enough to touch, and he did not turn his head. He was looking past them. Through them. At the living. At the staff.

Old Seven reached him first.

The spear went in under Mercer's arm, between two ribs of the very good coat, in a single perfect thrust that a lifetime of practice had made, ankle-knee-hip-spine-shoulder-elbow-wrist. It went through the coat and through whatever was under the coat and came out the other side.

There was a sound like a bell cracking.

Mercer staggered. He looked down, puzzled, at the spear blade sticking out of his side. He couldn't see the spear. He couldn't see the hand on it. He only saw a hole in his coat, and gray crystal dust trickling out of it like sand.

"Stop!" Yuanshu's voice cracked across the plateau. "Seven, stop! Don't break him!"

The old spear captain froze. The spear stayed where it was.

Three hundred and twelve threads.

If he shatters, they all snap.

And three hundred and twelve people, all across the Wastes, lose the last of themselves at once.

Mercer began to laugh.

It came out of all of his mouths at once, a hundred different laughs layered on top of each other, high and low and broken, like a crowd laughing at a joke in a language none of them spoke.

"You see," he said, through all of them. His voice was the shriek and the laugh and a pleasant cultured voice all at once. "You see the problem. You can stab me. You can burn me. You can't break me." The split in his face widened into something like a grin. "And I can stand here and lash at your friends until one of them gets frightened enough." Threads cracked out again, dozens, toward the ring of the dead, through it, toward the living in the middle. "One of them always does."

A thread got past Lingyue.

It came over her shoulder, too fast, and touched Bai Lu on the crown of her head.

The girl went still. Her hands, which had been glowing green over Yan Shuang, went dark. Her head turned. Three degrees. North.

"No!" Lingyue spun and cut. The thread snapped. Bai Lu crumpled, gasping, clutching her head. Lingyue caught her. "She's back, she's back, she's all right..."

"That one was free," said Mercer pleasantly. "The next one won't be."

Yuanshu stood by the white stone with the staff in his hand and understood that they could not win this. Not this way. Not with spears and fire and light. Every thread they cut, he sent another. Every time they hit him, three hundred and twelve people somewhere in the dark held their breath.

He looked at the staff.

At the small white skull at its top with the silver around its eye sockets, glowing softly in the firelight.

"Jiang Lan," he said. "The first door. The way the threads come down."

"Yes."

"Does it go the other way?"

A pause.

"...Xuanming never thought of that," said the dry voice slowly. "He only ever thought about going up. Never about sending anyone down." Another pause, and something in it sharpened, the way a teacher's voice sharpens when a student says something interesting. "I don't know. Nobody's ever asked me."

"I'm asking."

"Then try it and find out, young man. That's how arithmetic works."

Give me four seconds.

A thread is a channel. Sound goes up it. Thought goes up it. Memory goes up it. But the pulse comes down it, every thirty-one hours. So it carries both ways, and the staff holds its bottom end.

Six thousand runs. If you push the minds down while the mouths are holding them, the threads tear: all three hundred and twelve lost. If the mouths let go first, the channel is open at both ends. Ninety-seven percent arrive.

It depends on one thing. Whether the mouths say yes.

Then I ask the mouths, he thought.

He walked toward Mercer.

Through the ring of the dead. Third Battalion parted for him without a word and closed again behind him. The threads lashed past him, and he didn't flinch, and he didn't think. He held the quiet. All the way down. Further than on Concordia. Further than the eleven minutes with the monks. A boy walking across black glass in the firelight toward a thing with a split face, with a white staff in his hand, thinking about nothing at all.

Mercer could see the staff. He couldn't see the boy holding it. Not really. Not his mind. Only a glowing white shape floating toward him across the plateau, held by a quiet so deep it was almost not there.

His threads turned toward it. Uncertain. Feeling.

Su Mingli.

Ask her. Not to break him. To hold him.

"Mingli," said Yuanshu, without raising his voice. "Hold him still. Don't crush. Just hold."

Su Mingli did not answer. She simply looked at Mercer, and the air around him locked.

Not a crushing weight. A cage. Gravity from every direction at once, perfectly balanced, pressing in on him evenly from above and below and every side, so that he could not move a finger, could not turn his head, could not lash a single thread. He stood frozen by the fire with his face split open and his hundred mouths shrieking and every one of his three hundred and twelve threads hanging motionless in the air around him, held, like a spider caught in its own web.

Yuanshu stepped up to him.

He placed the small white skull at the top of the staff against the gray crystal where Mercer's chest should have been. Gently. The way you'd lay your hand on a gravestone.

And he asked.

Not Mercer.

Mercer was not anyone. Seed had been right: a hole in the world in the shape of a man. There was no one in there to ask.

He asked the mouths.

My name is Zhou Yuanshu. I'm not here to take anything from you.

Do you want to go home?

It was like WARDEN and it was nothing like WARDEN.

The mouths didn't answer automatically. They didn't answer at all, at first. They screamed. They had been screaming for so long that they had forgotten there was anything else to do, and they screamed into his question the way you'd scream into a storm.

He waited.

He held the quiet. He let them scream. He stood there with the staff against the cracked crystal and the gray dust trickling out of the spear wound and the whole frozen plateau ringing with the sound, and he didn't push, and he didn't ask again. He just stayed. The way you stay beside someone who has been crying for a long time.

The scream faltered.

One mouth closed.

...home?

Then another.

...he said home.

where's home

I had a camp. By a spring. With my brother. We had a sled.

I was walking south. To the wall. I had a letter for my wife.

I was a surveyor. I was measuring. I was just measuring.

Three hundred and twelve voices. One by one, falling quiet. Telling him where they'd been when a pleasant man in a very good coat had walked past them in the Wastes and smiled.

can we go back?

is my body still there?

is anyone

"Yes," said Yuanshu aloud. "Yes. You're still there. All of you. Your bodies are still breathing. Waiting for you. At the end of your thread." His voice broke. He didn't stop. "Do you want to go back down?"

...yes.

yes

yes yes yes yes

"Jiang Lan," he whispered. "Now."

The staff blazed.

White. Not the white-gold of Lingyue's light, not the white of Old Seven's flame. A clean, soft, gray-white glow like a full moon coming out from behind a cloud, pouring down the length of the silver-joined bones and out of the small skull and into the crystal.

And down the threads.

He felt it go. Three hundred and twelve threads lighting up, one after another, like strings of lanterns being lit in a long dark street. Silver turning moon-white. Lighting up out of Mercer's chest and away across the plateau in every direction, into the dark, south, east, west, along the ground, over ridges, down ravines, for kilometers, a vast glowing web spreading out across the Wastes under the stars.

And down each thread, very small, very fast, like a spark running along a fuse, something went home.

Mercer's mouths were closing.

Not shutting. Emptying. One by one, the small human mouths in the cloudy crystal went slack, and then still, and then simply faded, the way a reflection fades when someone walks away from a window. The shriek thinned. Thinned. Became a few voices. Became one.

The last mouth was in the center of his split face, behind the very good teeth. It was his. The one that had smiled in the photograph. The one that had said civilized.

It looked at Yuanshu.

"The gardener sees you now," it said. Pleasantly. Without fear. Without anything. "Not just your noise. You. That thing in your hand. That door." The very white teeth gleamed one last time. "It has always come to the field when the grain is ripe. You've just shown it the gate is open."

Then the crystal went dark.

All of it, at once, from the inside out, like a lamp switched off. The gray clouded through to black. The cracks spread. And with a sound like a sheet of ice breaking on a frozen lake, Julian Mercer came apart, and fell, and was a heap of gray dust on the black glass in a very good coat, and the wind came across the plateau and began, slowly, to blow him away.

Nobody moved for a long time.

The fire crackled. The staff in Yuanshu's hand had dimmed to its soft, candle-through-paper glow. Out across the dark plateau, the glowing web of threads was fading, line by line, the way the lights of a city go out one by one late at night.

Three hundred and twelve.

I felt every one arrive.

Yuanshu. They went home.

Somewhere out there, in the dark, in camps and ravines and survey tents, three hundred and twelve people just blinked.

Mercer counted. I did not think a thing like that would count.

He sat down on the frozen glass beside the heap of dust, quite suddenly, because his legs had stopped holding him.

Lingyue was there. She sat down beside him without a word. Then Old Seven, standing over both of them, spear grounded, flame low.

And on the far side of the fire, on his folding camp stool, Gu Hanshan had taken his face out of his hands, and was looking at the heap of gray dust in the very good coat.

He was weeping.

"Yuanshu," he said. His voice was barely a whisper. "Please. I need you to ask me something."
