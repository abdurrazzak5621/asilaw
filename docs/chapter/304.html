Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 304The Two O'Clock

czverse9 min read

His name was Kang Yousheng, and he had run the ferry at the old steps for fifty-two years.

Lingyue had not known that. She had seen him. Everyone in the hall had seen him, because he came to the two o'clock every night, and the two o'clock was small enough that you saw everyone. A thin old man in a padded jacket with a flask of something that was not tea, who sat in the third tier on the aisle with his hand flat on the stone of the step in front of him, the way the holders did when they could not reach the platform, and who said eh to Wen Jia every time she asked him, and once, on the fourth night, I'm eighty-one, girl, what else am I going to do with two hours.

He died at twenty to three, on the thirteenth night, with his hand on the step.

Nobody noticed at first. That was the thing she kept coming back to, afterward. He had not made a sound. He had not fallen. He had been sitting in the third tier on the aisle with his hand on the stone and his eyes closed, the way half the two o'clock sat, and the woman next to him had thought he was asleep. It was Wen Jia who knew.

...Lingyue.

...the ferryman.

...he's stopped being warm.

Bai Lu said it was his heart.

She said it standing in the aisle at a quarter past three, with her bag open and her sleeves pushed up and her face set in the way it set when she had already done everything there was to do and it had not been enough. She said it to Lingyue, quietly, and then she said it again, louder, to the nine hundred people of the two o'clock who were all standing up in the tiers and not knowing what to do with their hands.

"His heart," said Bai Lu. "It was old. It was tired. It would have stopped this week or next, whatever he was doing. I've had him on my list for a year." She looked round the hall. "Holding didn't help. I'm not going to tell you it didn't hurt, either. I don't know. Nobody's done this before."

Nobody said anything to that. There was nothing to say.

Lingyue went down the steps of the platform with her daughter asleep in the sling and stood in the aisle beside the third tier and looked at him. A thin old man in a padded jacket, with his eyes closed and his hand still flat on the stone of the step, and the flask beside him, uncapped.

Wen Jia, she thought. She did not have Seed. She did not need it; the girl in the stone could hear anyone in the hall who wanted to be heard. Is he there?

...yes. By the stone. Close. He's very cold. Everybody is, at first.

...I asked him. I didn't know if I was allowed. I'm not the boy. I don't have a door. But I can ask.

...he said he'd wait.

...he said: I want the one who asks to ask me. Not that I don't like you, girl.

Lingyue stood in the aisle for a moment.

Then she knelt down in the third tier and put her free hand on the stone of the step beside his, flat, the way he had been holding it, and stayed there until the shift was over.

The two o'clock the next night was six hundred.

She felt it before Gao Min said it. She was on the platform with her hand on her husband's chest, and at two o'clock the floor under him thinned. Not gone. Not close to gone. But six hundred people in the tiers of a hall that held fifteen hundred, sitting far apart, not looking at the third tier on the aisle where someone had left a flask and a paper boat of dumplings and a white chrysanthemum.

She could feel what it cost him. Under her hand. The body on the mattress under the blue jacket drawing on the floor the way it had drawn on it for two weeks, harder every day, and the floor bearing it, and bearing it, thinner.

"Novaya Zarya's on," said Gao Min, behind her, low. She had the stopwatch in one hand and a pencil in the other. "It's morning there. Orlov's put another four hundred in. Kesse's sending what it can. It's enough." She paused. "For tonight."

"And tomorrow?"

Gao Min did not answer.

There was a man on the steps of the Awakening Hall in the morning.

Lingyue saw him when she came out at seven for the shift change. Fifty, perhaps. In a work coat. With a sheet of card on a stick, the way people carried them on the news from Port Halvard, and on it, in thick black marker that was not Xiaoyu's and was not true, only what he thought:

HOW MANY MORE?

He was not shouting. He was standing on the third step with the card on its stick, and the line for the seven o'clock was going round him on both sides, and nobody was saying anything to him, the way the old quarter did not.

She went down the steps.

She did not plan to. Su Mingli, at the door, said Lingyue, in the voice that meant don't, and she went anyway, with her daughter against her chest and her grandfather's sword on her back, and stopped on the step above him.

He looked up at her. He knew who she was. Everybody did.

"He was my uncle," said the man. "Kang Yousheng. The ferry."

She had not known that either.

"Fifty-two years," said the man. "He took me across the river every morning to school. Every morning. In the Black Tide. When the bridges were down." His hand was not steady on the stick. "He was eighty-one. He should have been in bed. And he came here every night and sat on a step for two hours to keep your husband warm, and now he's dead." He looked at her. "How many more? That's all. I want to know how many more. Is he worth it? Is one boy worth that?"

The line had stopped moving. On both sides of the steps.

Lingyue looked down at him for a long time.

She thought of all the things her husband would have said. He would have said something quiet and true that made the man's face change. He would have asked him something. He always asked.

She was not her husband.

"No," she said.

The man stared at her.

"Nobody's worth anybody," said Shen Lingyue. "I'm not going to stand on these steps and tell you my husband's worth your uncle. He isn't. Nobody is." Her voice did not shake. "Your uncle said yes. Every night. The girl in the stone asked him first, every night, and he said yes, and once he said: I'm eighty-one, what else am I going to do with two hours." She stopped. "I'm not going to tell you that makes it all right. I don't know if it does."

The man did not say anything.

"He's by the stone," said Lingyue. "Your uncle. Wen Jia asked him. He said he'd wait. He wants the one who asks to ask him properly. When he comes home." She looked at the card on the stick. "So that's what I'm doing. I'm keeping my husband warm so he can come home and ask your uncle what he wants. That's all I've got."

She turned to go back up the steps.

"You can stand there," she said, over her shoulder. "With that. It's a fair question. He'd say it's a fair question. I'm going to keep counting."

He was still on the steps at noon.

At two in the morning, he came in.

She saw him from the platform. He came through the doors of the Awakening Hall without the card and walked up the aisle to the third tier, to the seat on the aisle where a flask and a paper boat and a white chrysanthemum were, and stood there a moment. Then he went up the three steps of the platform, past her, past her husband on the mattress, past the dead nun with the lamp, and put his hand on the stone.

[He's going a long way. You'll be warm on purpose for two hours. It might make you tired. Do you want to?]

The man looked at the letters for a while.

"Is my uncle there?" he said.

[Yes. Right here. He's cold. He says: eh.]

The man made a sound that was not a word. Then he said, "Yes," and went down the steps, and sat in the third tier on the aisle, next to the flask, and put his hand flat on the stone of the step, and closed his eyes.

The two o'clock that night was eleven hundred.

Gao Min said a noodle shop on Ferry Lane had closed early and come in, all of them, the owner and the cooks and the owner's mother. She said a boy in the Academy uniform had come with his whole class and said his great-grandfather used to take him across the river on the ferry when he was small, and he had not known until yesterday that the old man had been coming here. She said she had stopped trying to work out why people came. She just wrote the numbers down.

On the nineteenth night, the mark came from Anthos.

Not from him. From the other way. Down through the Lens, out of the root, along the net, through Ichiro, who had a way of ringing that Xiaoyu could read like handwriting now. She was on the bottom tier with the marker, asleep, and she woke all at once with her hand on the floor, and sat up, and her face changed, and she read it out in the dark hall, slowly.

FROM HALVARDE. AT ANTHOS. THROUGH THE FOG-RINGER.

THE SIXTEEN HOUSES HAVE STOPPED.

THEY WENT TO THE EDGE OF THE FEDERATION. THEY FOUND THE STONES THERE HAD READ THE MARK. ALL OF THEM. THERE IS NOWHERE IT WASN'T READ.

THEY ARE ASKING WHERE IT CAME FROM.

Xiaoyu stopped.

"Go on," said Lingyue.

Her sister-in-law did not look at her. She was looking at her own hand, flat on the floor of the Awakening Hall, the hand that had written THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE in square black capitals on a wall in a bank on East Market Road, and sent it, through a girl in a stone and a fog-ringer in a lens, to every Paragon in the Lamplit Worlds at once.

THE FOG-RINGER CAN SEE THEM THROUGH THEIR FACES. THEY KNOW IT WAS WRITTEN. THEY KNOW WHAT A SCRIBE IS. THE OLD HOUSES REMEMBER THE WORD.

THEY KNOW IT WAS A GIRL. ON AN UNLISTED ORCHARD.

FOUR OF THE SIXTEEN WANT TO COME AND ASK HER TO WRITE SOMETHING ELSE.

TWELVE DO NOT.

THEY ARE ARGUING.

THE FOG-RINGER SAYS: TELL THE GIRL WITH THE SWORD. HE SAYS: HE'S SORRY. HE'S WATCHING. HE'LL RING THE MOMENT THEY DECIDE.

The Awakening Hall was silent. Eleven hundred people of the two o'clock, in the tiers, with their hands on the stone, and their eyes open now.

Lingyue looked at her husband's face, thin, asleep, a very long way away. She looked at her daughter. She looked at the girl on the bottom tier with the marker in her hand, eighteen, Scribe, not marked, whose writing was true.

"Ask them," said Zhou Xiaoyu. Very steady. Her mother's voice. "If they come. Ask them what they want me to write." She capped the marker. "And then I'll tell them no."

Lingyue put her hand flat on her husband's chest.

"Gao Min," she said. "I need a second rota."
