Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 169Watch It Back

czverse10 min read

It asked them all at once.

He felt it go out. The lens under the hall had been focused on a single spot on the polished floor, on him, like a camera on a face. Now it opened. The focus fell back, the way Seed had said it would, from the spot to the hall to the clearing to the whole mountain, and the attention that had rested on him went down the mountainside the way morning light goes down a valley, over the gates, over the cedars, over the old terraces where three thousand two hundred and nine people in white sat in the dark of the moon with their faces turned up.

And every one of them heard it.

Hello.

I am the one who keeps. You have been waiting for me. Thank you for waiting.

What do you want?

Hara Setsuko answered first.

He did not see it. He felt it, through the Field, through the Lattice where his dead stood in their rows at the bottom of the path, through Ren on the step with her hand flat on the wood who felt everything that happened in a mind on this mountain and gasped. On the first terrace, under the cedar where she had sat for six days with a blanket over her head, an old woman of eighty-one lifted her face to the dark sky and said, out loud, in a clear voice, to no one:

"I want to be with him."

And the attention took it.

Not her. The want. It lifted out of her the way a coin lifts out of a pocket, small and bright and very sharp, pointing somewhere Yuanshu could not see, and went up. Up the mountain. Up through the hall. Up the lens, into the dark. And Hara Setsuko sat on the wet stone under the cedar with her face still turned up and her mouth still open, and nothing in her face at all.

Her head turned.

Not three degrees. Straight up. And stayed there.

...hollow.

She is hollow, Yuanshu. Like the bench at the Night Market. Like Garrett Hale in the crater.

The body is alive. The mind is there. But the thing that made her get up in the morning is gone up the lens.

She does not mind. That is what it said. Without the want, she is very light.

She does not mind.

"Lingyue," he said.

She was already moving. She went out through the paper door of the hall in her bare feet, past Ren on the step, past the Abbot standing with his face turned up and his hands at his sides, past Old Seven, and down the clearing toward the gates at a run.

"Tell them," said Yuanshu, behind her, through the staff. "All of them. What it does. Out loud. In person. With the reason. They have to know what they're saying yes to."

"I know," said Lingyue. "Article Ten. I read it four times too."

Another. Second terrace. A man from the Atlantic Federation. He wanted his wife.

Another. Another.

Eleven.

Twenty-six.

He could not stop it. He knew that, standing on the spot in front of the empty platform with the staff in his hands. He could not reach into three thousand minds and close their mouths. He could not move them; there was nobody behind them, no window, no road, and their wants were theirs, and the thing above the sky had asked them, kindly, the way he would have asked.

But he could close the eye.

"Ren," he said. "Now."

On the step of the hall, Shirasagi Ren lifted her hand off the wood, and put it down again, and spoke to the mountain.

Not to the living. To the dead. Down the path. Under the gates. To three hundred and ninety old monks and nuns who had sat down in a hall twelve hundred years ago so that the world would see the trouble coming, and crumbled, and been swept up very carefully and buried under gates, and listened for twelve hundred years without being able to see, and had been asked, three nights ago, for the first time, what they wanted.

Now, said Ren. Watch it back.

And they did.

He felt it happen through the Field like a long breath drawn in down the whole length of the mountain. Three hundred and ninety small, still, ancient embers under the black gates, who had only ever been able to listen, turned their attention up. Not at the sky. At the lens. At the thing under the hall that had been ground out of their brothers' watching and turned around.

They knew it. Of course they knew it. It was made of their brothers. Twelve hundred years of seven minds looking up at the sky so as not to miss a sound. They knew the shape of that looking the way you know the shape of your brother's handwriting on an envelope.

They reached into the lens and took hold of it.

Not to break it. To take it back.

The lens is changing.

They are pulling the watching out of it. Their brothers' watching. Grain by grain. The way the Abbot swept it in. Backward.

It is losing its shape.

Mo Shi says six minutes. He says the bell is still swinging but it is getting heavier.

The attention felt it.

He felt it feel it. A pause in the warm patient enormous thing looking down the mountain. A turn. Not toward him. Toward the gates. Toward three hundred and ninety old dead monks who were, for the first time in twelve hundred years, looking up. Straight up the lens. Straight at the thing at the top of it.

And the thing at the top of it, which had looked at a great many things in a long, long time, and had not been looked at by anything in longer than that, went very still.

...oh.

Who is that?

Nobody answered it.

The monks under the gates did not answer. They had not been asked a question by it, and they did not offer. They only watched. Three hundred and ninety minds who had sat in the dark so that the world would see the trouble coming, seeing it. Looking at it. Steadily. Without wanting anything from it at all.

...you are looking at me.

Nobody has looked at me in a very long time.

The lens shrank.

Fifty-eight.

Seventy-one.

Yuanshu, they are still answering. The lens is losing its shape but it is still open. Every one who answers before it closes, it keeps.

On the terraces, Lingyue was running.

He could feel her through the Field, a small bright point going down the mountainside terrace by terrace in the dark with her two swords on her back, not lit, not drawn. Stopping in front of people. Crouching down so that her face was level with theirs. Telling them. Out loud. In person. Blunt, the way she told everyone everything. It keeps what you want and you go hollow. Like her. Look at her. Look at her face. That's what kept means. Nobody told you. I'm telling you.

Some of them looked at Hara Setsuko under her cedar, with her face turned straight up and nothing in it, and closed their mouths.

Some of them answered anyway.

"I know," a young man said to her on the fourth terrace, gently, with tears running down his face. "I know. I still want it." And lifted his face, and said what he wanted, and went still.

Lingyue did not stop him. She stayed crouched in front of him until he was hollow, so that he would not be alone when it happened. Then she got up and went to the next one.

At four minutes, the Abbot walked past him into the hall.

Yuanshu had not heard him come. Kurose Tōya came in through the paper door in his bare feet, in his gray robe, with his straw hat in his hand, and walked across the polished boards to the empty platform, and knelt down on the floor in front of the cushion at the end, the one whose robe had faded almost to white, where the oldest Sleeper had sat.

He did not look at Yuanshu. He looked up.

"I'm sorry," he said, to the dark under the roof. Not to Yuanshu. To the lens. To the thing at the top of it. "I made it out of them. I should have asked them first." He bowed his head. "I didn't know you could ask the dead, until the boy came. And then I didn't want to know."

...you swept the path.

"I swept the path."

Every morning. I watched you.

What do you want?

The Abbot was quiet for a moment, kneeling on the polished floor in front of seven empty cushions in the dark of the moon.

"I want to be kept," he said. "I've wanted it since the Sleepers went quiet. I've never wanted anything so much." He smiled, the small sad kind smile. "I know what it means now. The girl with the swords told me. I watched Setsuko." He lifted his face. "I still want it."

Yuanshu stood three meters away with the staff in his hands.

He isn't in anyone's window, he thought. There's nobody behind him. His yes is his.

Yes.

I am sorry. It is.

He did not move.

Kurose Tōya, who had tended the Sleepers for forty years and swept the gates every morning and given rice cakes to a small girl on the temple steps, lifted his face toward the roof of the hall, and the attention took what he wanted, small and bright and very sharp, and it went up.

He knelt there on the floor in front of the platform with his face turned straight up and his hat still in his hand, and nothing in his face at all.

On the step outside, Ren made a sound Yuanshu would not forget.

At six minutes, the lens closed.

Not with a sound. With a kind of settling. The three hundred and ninety monks under the gates drew the last of their brothers' watching back out of it, grain by grain, and the thing that had been ground into an eye stopped being an eye, and was only a seed again: a gray stone the size of a man, eleven meters down in the rock under the hall, warm, empty, humming one low note with nothing to hum through.

The attention lifted.

He felt it go. Up. Out. Away. Not angry. Not anything. The way a person in a window turns away when the curtain is drawn.

But at the very last, before it was gone, it spoke once more. Not to him. To the gates.

...thank you for looking.

I will remember that too.

Old Seven broke the seed at dawn.

Not alone. With three hundred and ninety old monks under the gates holding the last ringing of it down, the way the Sleepers had once taught Yuanshu to hold his own mind still: there, did you hear that? That was you, shouting. With Lingyue's light falling off two sword points through the floor of the hall, through eleven meters of rock, onto a seed with nothing left to look through. With Mo Shi calling the seam.

The seed rang when it broke. It had to. But there was nothing left to hear it. No other seeds. No lens. And three hundred and ninety old minds on the mountainside folded the ring down into silence the way you fold a letter you will not send.

The floor of the hall cracked when the rock beneath it settled. The platform tilted. Hu Dabao, who had been standing at the back of the hall all night with his shield because nobody had told him not to, put the shield over Lingyue and Yuanshu and the kneeling Abbot as the roof beam came down, and held it.

The beam broke the shield. The shield broke his arm in three places. He did not let go of it.

"I'm fine," he said afterward, white-faced, sitting in the wreck of the hall with Bai Lu's green light running up his arm. "It's the left one. I mostly use the right."

One thousand one hundred and four.

That is how many answered before the lens closed.

They are hollow. All of them. Sitting on the terraces with their faces turned up. The Abbot. Hara Setsuko. The man from the Atlantic Federation who wanted his wife. The young man who said he knew.

They are alive. They will eat if someone feeds them. They will sleep if someone lays them down. Garrett Hale came most of the way back, with Harlan Voss sitting beside him for a year, saying his name.

One thousand one hundred and four people will need someone to sit beside them and say their names.

And two thousand one hundred and five did not answer.

Lingyue reached most of them. I counted. She told one thousand six hundred and twelve people to their faces what kept means, in six minutes, on a mountain, in the dark.

I did not count her. Again.

I am going to stop saying that, because I am going to start counting her.
