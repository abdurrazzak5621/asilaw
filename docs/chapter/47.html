Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 47The General's Offer

czverse10 min read

The military cemetery at the Western Pass covered a whole mountainside, and at dawn it was white with frost.

Yuanshu came up the road in the back of the dark green jeep with Qin Hao driving and Old Seven folded into the seat beside him, and when they came around the last bend he saw it all at once, the way you see the sea for the first time. The mountain rose in front of them, steep and bare, the color of iron. And from the bottom of it to the top, in long straight rows that followed the curve of the slope like lines of writing on a page, stood small gray stones.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one exactly the same size. Each one exactly the same distance from the next. Row above row above row, climbing the mountain until they disappeared into the low gray cloud on the summit. The frost on them caught the first light coming over the eastern ridge and turned them, for a moment, gold.

Qin Hao stopped the jeep at the gate.

"Forty-one thousand, three hundred and six," he said, not looking at the cemetery. "Every soldier who has died holding the Western Pass since the Black Tide." He took the keys out of the ignition. "My father is in row two hundred and twelve."

The general was waiting at the top of the first terrace, alone.

General Qin Zhenyue of the Western War Zone was eighty-one years old, and he looked like the mountain. Tall, square, gray, with a face like a cliff that had been weathered by sixty winters on the wall. He wore a plain dark green greatcoat with no medals on it. His left sleeve was pinned up empty at the shoulder. He stood with his one hand resting on top of a gray stone, the way a man rests his hand on the head of an old dog.

He watched them come up the steps without moving.

When they reached him, he looked first at Old Seven. For a long time. At the broken left forearm. At the spear. At the gray flame.

"Qi Zhenshan," he said. His voice was deep and rough, like gravel in a bucket. "Spear captain. Third Battalion. Black Tide, the eastern line." He nodded slowly. "I was a lieutenant in the Seventh. We were two kilometers north of you on the third day. I heard what you did with that spear." He paused. "I didn't think I'd ever get to say thank you."

Old Seven stood very straight. Then he bowed his skull, just slightly. Two old soldiers, one living, one dead, thirty-one years and two kilometers apart.

Then the general turned to Yuanshu.

"You're shorter than on television," he said.

"Everyone says that."

"Walk with me."

They walked along the terrace between the rows of stones, the general setting a slow, steady pace with his boots crunching on the frost, Yuanshu beside him, Old Seven and Qin Hao a few steps behind.

"Six weeks ago," said the general, "I did something I have never done in sixty years in uniform. I asked my soldiers what they wanted." He didn't look at Yuanshu. "Not an order. A question. I put your Ledger on every barracks screen in the Western War Zone. I said: This is not policy. This is not command. Nobody will see your answer but you and that boy. Choose."

"And they chose."

"Forty thousand of them chose YES." The general stopped walking. He looked out over the frost-gold rows. "Living men and women. Nineteen years old. Thirty. Fifty. They wrote things in the box. You've seen what they wrote?"

"Some of it."

"If I die on the wall, let me keep standing on it." The general's voice didn't change. "Don't send me home in a box. Send me back up. One boy from my own battalion wrote, I'd rather be useful than buried. He's nineteen." He was silent for a moment. "I read every single one. Six weeks. Every night."

He turned to face Yuanshu fully, and his eyes under the gray brows were hard and clear as winter water.

"And then I came up here and looked at these," he said. "And I thought: forty thousand of my living soldiers want to keep standing after they die. What about the forty-one thousand who already did?"

The wind came down off the summit, cold and thin, and moved the frost on the stones.

"That's the proposal," said General Qin Zhenyue. "Ask them. All of them. Every soldier on this mountain. The ones who say yes, raise them. Not now. When it's needed. When the lens opens and the north comes down on us." He laid his one hand flat on the nearest stone. "A legion of the dead on the Western Pass. Forty thousand soldiers who don't eat, don't sleep, don't feel cold, and remember every battle they ever fought on this ground. You command it. I pay for it. The Western War Zone backs you on Concordia with everything it has." He looked at Yuanshu. "That's my offer."

He is not lying.

He is not trying to own you.

He is an old man with one arm who has buried forty-one thousand people on one mountain, and he is very, very frightened of what is coming.

And he is offering you an army.

Yuanshu looked out over the rows of stones for a long time.

"I'll ask them," he said. "If you want me to. And if they say yes, I'll raise them, when it's time." He turned to the general. "But there's a rule."

The general's eyebrows went up a millimeter.

"The dead may defend," said Yuanshu. "Never conquer."

The general looked at him without expression.

"Go on."

"They stand on walls. They guard cities. They carry people out of fires. They hold a line so the living can get behind it." Yuanshu kept his voice level. "They never cross a border to attack. They're never used against living people, except to stop living people from killing someone. They're never sent north to burn something because a general decided it would be efficient." He paused. "Not by you. Not by me. Not by anyone."

"And if the best defense," said the general quietly, "is to march north and burn the thing before it comes?"

"Then the living do it. And the dead hold the wall behind them, so the living have somewhere to come home to." Yuanshu looked at the rows. "They already gave everything once. They said yes to standing. They didn't say yes to being a weapon. If I start using them as one, then I'm just a harvester with better manners."

The wind moved over the mountain.

General Qin Zhenyue looked at him for a very long time with his winter-water eyes. Behind them, Qin Hao had gone completely still.

Then the old man did something Yuanshu did not expect.

He laughed. A short, hard, rough sound, like a rock falling down a slope.

"Good," said the general. "Good. If you'd said yes to my army without a rule, I'd have had Hao drive you home and burned the Ledger." He took his hand off the stone. "I've spent sixty years watching men decide that the dead are a resource. Bones for cores. Names for speeches. Graves for votes." He looked out at his mountain. "Write your rule down. I'll put my name on it. I'll stand up on that island and read it out myself."

"There's something else," said the general.

He turned and walked, without asking whether they would follow, down the terrace and along the slope, between the rows, a long way, until they came to a row that was marked at its end with a small carved post. 212.

He stopped at the ninth stone.

It was the same as all the others. The same size, the same gray, the same frost. The same plain carving.

COLONEL QIN ZHAOLIN. WESTERN PASS. HE HELD.

Qin Hao had stopped two steps behind. He stood with his cap in his hand and his face very white.

"My son," said the general. "Hao's father. Eight years ago. A Rift opened inside the Pass. Inside. Where nothing should have been able to open. He held the gate for four hours while the garrison got out." The general's voice didn't change at all. "We never understood how a Rift opened there. The Institute said it was a freak. A weather event." He looked down at the stone. "I'm not asking you to raise him. I'm asking you to ask him. Whatever he wants." He swallowed. "And if he says no, then he says no."

Yuanshu knelt on the frost in front of the ninth stone in row two hundred and twelve.

He put his hand flat on the cold rock.

And he did what the old monk had taught him. He went quiet first. All the way down. No questions racing ahead. No hope. No fear. Just an open hand on a stone, waiting.

Then he asked.

Colonel Qin. Your father is here. And your son. Do you want anything from me?

The answer came up through the stone like warmth through a cup.

ONLY TO SPEAK.

And he has something he very much wants to say.

The voice came out of the air above the grave, rough and tired and amused, a man's voice from eight years ago, and when Qin Hao heard it he made a sound like he'd been punched.

"Dad. You've lost weight. You're not eating."

General Qin Zhenyue closed his eyes.

"And Hao. Look at you. In uniform. You look ridiculous. You look exactly like me." A pause. "Stop crying, both of you, I haven't got long and I need to tell you something important."

The two of them, the old man and the young man, stood side by side over the stone with their faces wet and did not move.

"The Rift," said Colonel Qin Zhaolin. "Eight years ago. It wasn't a freak. Before it opened, for nine days, there was rain in the Pass. Silver. Very fine. Out of a clear sky. Nobody else saw it. I saw it because I was on the gate at night and I couldn't sleep." His voice tightened. "Some of my soldiers stopped. Just stopped. Turned their heads north. I thought they were sick. I wrote a report. Sent it to the Institute."

He was quiet for a moment.

"A very kind man came out from Lingzhou to look at them. Gray cardigan. Brought tea for everyone. He said it was altitude sickness. He took my report home with him. Then the Rift opened."

The wind came down off the mountain.

"That's all," said the colonel. "That's what I had to say. I've been waiting eight years for someone to ask." A pause, and something softer in it. "Dad. Hao. Hold the wall. I'll be here."

Then he was quiet.

They stood there for a long time.

General Qin Zhenyue had put his one hand on his grandson's shoulder. Neither of them spoke.

Silver rain in the Western Pass. Eight years ago.

One year before your mother went down to the basement.

A report sent to the Institute. Collected by a kind man with tea.

That is one.

Someone who said yes to telling us. Someone who was there.

The general let go of Qin Hao's shoulder. He turned and looked up the mountain, at the forty-one thousand stones climbing into the cloud.

"Ask them," he said. His voice was hoarse. "All of them. Now."

So Yuanshu stood up, and walked to the very center of the cemetery, where the main path crossed the hundredth row, and knelt there on the frost with both hands flat on the frozen ground.

He went quiet.

And he asked forty-one thousand soldiers, all at once, the same question he'd asked the colonel.

For a moment, nothing.

Then, at the bottom of the mountain, on the first stone of the first row, a small gray flame lit. Like a match struck in the dark.

Then another. And another. Then ten. Then a hundred. Climbing the mountain row by row, stone by stone, faster and faster, like a fire going up a hillside in a dry wind, until the whole slope from the bottom to the summit was covered in tiny gray flames, tens of thousands of them, flickering on the frost in the dawn light.

YES: 31,882.

ONLY TO SPEAK: 1,209.

QUIET: 8,215.

The Quiet ones have not lit. Look. There are dark stones all over the mountain, between the flames. That is them, saying no. I will write every one down.

And then, row by row, from the bottom of the mountain to the top, thirty-one thousand gray flames rose up off their stones. Not bodies. Not yet. Just the flames, hanging a meter above each grave in the cold air, in perfect straight lines.

And every one of them turned to face the general.

And dipped. Once. All together.

A salute.

General Qin Zhenyue of the Western War Zone, eighty-one years old, one-armed, who had buried every one of them, stood in the middle of his mountain and raised his one hand to his brow, and held it there, shaking, until the sun came over the ridge and the flames went out one by one, back down into the stones, to wait.
