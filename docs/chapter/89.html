Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 89The Clipboard

czverse10 min read

Zhou Xiaoyu had a clipboard, and she was not afraid to use it.

She sat at a folding table in the middle of the old banking hall of the Ossuary, under the board with its five columns of numbers, with a stack of forms in front of her and three black pens lined up in a row and a mug of tea going cold at her elbow. Beside her, in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees, sat her mother. Beside her mother, on a plain wooden chair, with a candle, sat Sister Ning.

Three witnesses. Su Mingli had insisted. Nobody goes out into the dark because someone said something brave on a harbor wall.

And one by one, all through the long gray afternoon, people came across the marble floor and stood in front of the table.

And Xiaoyu asked them.

"Name."

"Mo Xing."

"Mo Shi."

"One at a time," said Xiaoyu, without looking up. "You're two people. You get two forms."

The twins looked at each other. Then Mo Xing stepped forward alone, and Mo Shi stepped back, which was the first time anyone in the Ossuary had ever seen them more than an arm's length apart.

Xiaoyu looked up from the clipboard.

"Here's the question," she said. "Exactly as written. The Council wrote it. I'm not allowed to change it." She read it aloud. "An expedition will leave Blue Star through the keeper's door to meet the Hunger in the Star Sea before it arrives. No one knows what is out there. No one knows if anyone will return. Do you want to go?" She put the pen down. "And you can say no. Nobody will think anything. You can say no right up until the door. Even at the door. Even after."

"Yes," said Mo Xing.

"Why?"

He blinked. "That's not on the form."

"I'm asking anyway," said Xiaoyu. "My brother asks everyone why. I've decided it's genetic."

Mo Xing was quiet for a moment. He looked back at his brother, standing two meters away, with his hourglass pin glinting on his collar.

"Because he's going," he said. "And I can fold space. And if he sees something coming three seconds before it comes, somebody has to be there to move him out of the way." He shrugged, very slightly. "We've always been like that. Since we were born. He sees it. I move it."

Xiaoyu wrote it down.

Then Mo Shi stepped forward, alone, and when she read him the question, he said, "Yes," and when she asked why, he looked at his brother and said, "I saw him say yes about four seconds before he did. I wasn't going to let him go by himself."

Bai Lu came with her aunt.

Instructor Bai stood behind her with both hands on her niece's shoulders, iron-gray hair cropped close, burn scar pale on her neck, saying nothing at all. When Xiaoyu read the question, Bai Lu said yes very quietly to the table, and when Xiaoyu asked why, she said:

"Because somebody has to heal them. Even out there. Even the dead." She looked down at her hands. "I couldn't fix Old Seven's arm. I tried for two days. I can't heal bone that's been dead for sixty years." She swallowed. "But I can stop it hurting. I can do that. I want to be there to do that."

Behind her, Instructor Bai's hands tightened on her shoulders. Her face did not change. But she bent down, after Bai Lu had signed, and picked up the pen, and wrote her own name under her niece's, in the witness box, very carefully, in ink.

Twice. Because the first time, her hand was shaking too much.

Qin Hao came in full Western War Zone uniform, cap under his arm.

When Xiaoyu read the question, he said, "Yes," before she had finished reading it, and when she asked why, he reached into his breast pocket and took out something small and set it on the table. A tarnished brass insignia. A colonel's star.

"My father held the Western Pass for four hours so the garrison could get out," he said. "I'd like to hold something."

General Qin Zhenyue had not come to the Ossuary. But there was a note pinned to Qin Hao's form, in a rough old hand, signed and witnessed. It said only: I asked him. He said yes. I said yes too. — Q.Z.

Sergeant Vera Sokolova came alone, and said yes, and when Xiaoyu asked why, she said, "Because I carried the staff once, and I would like to do it again," and then, after a long pause, "and because I do not have anyone at home who would need me to stay," and Xiaoyu looked at her and wrote it all down and then, without saying anything, slid her own mug of cold tea across the table, and Sokolova looked at it, and drank it.

Yan Shuang came with Ama.

The Wastes girl with the braid stood beside her, holding her one hand, looking around the old banking hall with enormous eyes. She had not stopped looking at things since she woke up on the ice. She had not stopped asking questions. Yan Shuang had answered every one.

Xiaoyu read the question.

Yan Shuang did not answer. She stood in front of the table with her weathered face perfectly still and Ama's hand in hers, and looked at the form.

"I want to go," she said at last. "I want it so much I can taste it. I've been useful once. I'd like to be useful again." She looked down at the girl beside her. "But."

Ama looked up at her.

"Auntie Yan," said the girl, "you can go. I'll be fine. I'm sixteen. I can look after myself."

"You ate a candle last week," said Yan Shuang. "You thought it was cheese."

"It looked like cheese."

Yan Shuang looked back at the form.

"No," she said. "Write no." She tightened her hand around Ama's. "I drove her to the edge of the world and got out and didn't look. Eleven years. I'm not going to the edge of the world again and leaving her behind." She looked at Xiaoyu. "Write: She's got someone to stay for. First time in her life."

Xiaoyu wrote it down. Exactly.

The dead came at dusk.

Not all of them. Nineteen thousand would not fit in the banking hall. They sent Sergeant Huo. One arm. Gray greatcoat in rags. Standing in front of the folding table with his flame steady.

Yuanshu translated, standing beside him.

"The Third Battalion," he said. "All seventy-two. They say yes. They say they've walked with their captain this far and they'd like to keep walking." He listened. "They say they know it can see them out there. They say they've been kindling before. On the eastern wall. Thirty-one years ago. They say it didn't stop them then either."

Xiaoyu wrote it down.

"And the others? The nineteen thousand?"

Yuanshu listened.

"No," he said. "They say no. Not out there." He paused. "They say Article Seven. The dead may defend. Never conquer." He listened again. "They say going out into the Sea to meet the Hunger, even if it's to protect us, even if it's to keep it away from here, it's still going out. It's still crossing a border. They don't want to be that." He looked at the board. "They want to stand on the wall. Here. At home. In case it gets past us." He swallowed. "They say somebody has to hold the line so the living have somewhere to come back to."

Xiaoyu looked at him over her clipboard.

Then she wrote it down. Every word.

Su Mingli came last.

She walked across the marble floor in her long gray coat with her hands at her sides and stopped in front of the folding table. She did not look at Yuanshu. She looked at the clipboard.

Xiaoyu read her the question.

Su Mingli was silent for a long time.

"No," she said.

The banking hall was very quiet.

"There are five of us that thing counted," said Su Mingli. "Significant. Finite." Her voice was perfectly flat. "If one of us goes out into the dark and doesn't come back, there are four. If the Hunger gets past whatever's out there, if it comes here anyway, four is not enough. Five might not be either. But four is less." She looked up from the clipboard at last. At Yuanshu. "And somebody has to stand in front of the Stele. If it comes. Somebody has to stand on the wall and tell eight billion people not to think anything at all." Her face did not move. "I said I would. Seven years ago. To your mother. Under the old telescope."

In her wheelchair, Zhou Wanqing closed her eyes.

"So no," said Su Mingli. "I'm not going." She turned, and looked at Yuanshu, and something moved at the corner of her mouth that was not a smile. "You don't need me to carry you anymore. You haven't for a long time. I just kept doing it because I didn't know how to stop."

She walked out of the banking hall without waiting for anyone to say anything.

Yuanshu watched her go. He did not follow her.

He thought about a woman standing in front of him at the Stele, on the first day, when no one else in the world would. About a coat buttoned to the throat. About the air bending.

You don't need me to carry you anymore.

He found that his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them.

When everyone else had gone, and the lamp was lit over the board, and the old banking hall was dark and quiet, Xiaoyu looked down at the last form on her clipboard.

It was blank.

She looked up at her brother.

"Name," she said.

"Xiaoyu..."

"Name," said his sister. "I asked everyone. I'm asking you. Mama's a witness. Sister Ning's a witness. It's on the form."

He looked at her. At her scowl, which was not holding very well. At their mother beside her, very still in the wheelchair, watching him with quick dark eyes.

"Zhou Yuanshu," he said.

Xiaoyu read him the question. Exactly as written. Not one word changed. Her voice cracked once, on return, and she kept going anyway.

Do you want to go?

He thought about it.

Honestly. Standing there, in front of the folding table, in the old banking hall, under the board with its five columns, the way he had asked a million people to think about it. Not the brave answer. Not the right answer. The true one.

"No," he said.

Xiaoyu stared at him.

"I don't want to," said Yuanshu. "I want to stay here. I want to eat breakfast. I want to go to Sunday dinner at the Academy and get asked stupid questions. I want to sit in the hospital and listen to Mama snore, because she does, Xiaoyu, she snores too, I heard her." His voice was not steady. He let it not be. "I don't want to go out into the dark and meet something that burns people. I've never wanted anything less in my life."

He took a breath.

"But I'm going," he said. "Because it put me at the top of the list. And if I'm out there, it comes to me. And if it comes to me, it doesn't come to you."

Xiaoyu looked at him, pen still.

Then she looked down at the form. And she did not write yes. She did not write no.

She wrote exactly what he had said. All of it. Every word. I don't want to. I'm going anyway. In careful, neat, slightly shaky handwriting, filling the whole box and running over into the margin.

Then she turned the clipboard around and held out the pen.

He signed it.

Their mother leaned over in the wheelchair and signed it too, in the witness box, in a small quick perfectly neat hand.

Sister Ning lit her candle, and signed it last.

"Ask me," said Wanqing quietly, afterward, when Xiaoyu had gone to fetch more tea and the banking hall was dark except for the lamp. "Ask me what I want."

He looked at her.

"What do you want, Mama?"

His mother was quiet for a long time, sitting in her wheelchair under the board, looking up at the fifth column. CAME HOME.

"I want to go with you," she said. "I want it more than I've ever wanted anything. I've been out there. In the field. I know more about what's past that door than anyone alive." She looked down at her thin hands on the blanket. "And I can't stand up for more than ten minutes. And I fall asleep in the afternoons. And Xiaoyu snores in the chair next to my bed every night because she's afraid if she goes home, I'll be gone again when she wakes up."

She looked up at him.

"So I'm staying," said Zhou Wanqing. "Someone has to make sure she eats breakfast." Her mouth moved, very slightly. "And someone has to build something while you're gone. Something for when it comes. If it comes." She looked at the board. "A world that asks is very bright, Yuanshu. The Hunger said so. I think it might be possible to make it a little less bright. Just for a while. Just enough." She smiled, very faintly. "I've been thinking about it in the afternoons. Instead of sleeping."

"What is it?"

"Come back," said his mother, "and I'll show you."
