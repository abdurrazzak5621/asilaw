Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 244Stakes

czverse13 min read

By the third morning, Gao Min had a chart on the wall of the banking hall, and nobody liked looking at it.

Eleven lines. One for each stone. She drew them by hand on squared paper, taped together, the way she drew everything, and pinned it beside the long white wall where Xiaoyu could see it from the stepladder, and every morning at nine, when the Bureau offices in ten other cities telephoned her with their numbers, she added a point to each line in pencil and joined it to the last.

Port Halvard: four centimeters the first night. Seven the second. Eleven the third.

Novaya Zarya: three. Five. Eight.

Kesse, Port Abiri, Yamato, the others. All of them going up. Not together, not at the same rate, but all of them, every night, the way the Talent line on her old graph had gone up, a hair higher every year, except that this was not years.

Lingzhou: two. Three. Five.

"It's accelerating," said Gao Min. She did not say it to anyone in particular. She said it to the chart, with a pencil in her hand, in her flat careful voice. "Not linearly. The second derivative's positive. Every night it's pulling harder than the night before." She drew the point. "At this rate, Port Halvard comes out of the ground in nineteen days."

"And Lingzhou?"

"Thirty-one." She put the pencil behind her ear. "It's going to be here in fifty-seven."

Nobody needed her to say what that meant. The stones would be out of the ground and up the threads long before the thing pulling them arrived. With eleven people from the bottom of a well sitting inside them.

Roman Sterling went home that morning.

He did not fly. He said it was not an emergency until it was, and when it was, he would fly, and Concordia could fine him. He took the train to the coast with his canvas bag over his shoulder and his gray raincoat buttoned to the throat, and before he went, he came down the vault steps where Yuanshu was sitting with the staff and stood in front of him.

"If you use the twelfth again," he said, "call me. I'll say it on the radio."

"I will."

"If you don't use it," said the King of Force, "call me anyway. I'd like to hear what you did instead." He looked across the banking hall at the chart on the wall, at the line marked PORT HALVARD going up faster than the others. "That one's mine. Callum Ward was from my city. His mother lives four streets from the hall. I'm going to go and stand next to it."

He went up the steps. At the bronze door, Old Seven stood aside for him without being asked.

They argued about cutting the threads for two days.

Qin Hao proposed it. He said it at the long table on the first afternoon, with his notebook open and his healed pink hands flat on either side of it, in the voice he used when he had been thinking about something for a long time and had decided he was right.

"My fire burns what binds," he said. "That's what it does. Fat Tan's ropes. The pressing on the branch. The threads at Kirovsk." He looked at the diagram on Kwan's laptop: eleven fine pale lines going up off the stone into the dark. "It's pulling them up by the threads. So burn the threads. Eleven of them. I'll do Lingzhou myself. I'll teach Orlov's people. Su Mingli can fold me to the rest." He shut the notebook. "No thread, no pull. The stones stay in the ground."

Lingyue, on the stairs, said: "Yes."

He looked at her. She did not look back. She was looking at the chart.

"It's the thing that works," she said. "Somebody has to say it. It's always me. Fine."

I have run it.

It works. In most of the runs. The threads burn. The stones stop rising. The pulling stops.

And then the one who picks stops pulling, and stands in the dark at the end of eleven cut threads, and cannot see anything, because it only sees bright, and the stones were the only bright it had.

And then it keeps walking.

It knows the way now. It has walked most of it. It does not need the thread to find Blue Star. It needs the thread to see Blue Star.

Without it, it comes here blind.

"So it's blind," said Qin Hao. "Good."

I do not know if that is good.

Something very large, with many hands, that cannot see, walking into a world it came to look at.

It will put its hands down to feel what is there.

It was Wen Jia who settled it.

Not in the banking hall. In the Awakening Hall, at one in the morning on the second night, after the Collector had come and gone, warm and patient, and said to Xiaoyu, when she asked it what it wanted, I looked further. There is a shape in the empty place. I do not know what it is yet. Ask me again tomorrow. After Xiaoyu had written it on the back of Gao Min's hand. After the hall had gone quiet again.

Yuanshu asked her. Through the staff, standing at the foot of the platform steps, with the warm stone in front of him and the bright blue jacket over its shoulder and the floor round its base cracked, now, in a line you could fit a fingernail into.

If we burn the threads. What happens to you?

...we stop being able to hear each other.

It was a while before she said anything else.

...the threads aren't only what it pulls on. They're how we talk. All eleven. They go up and they join, somewhere in the dark, and that's how I know the old man at Port Abiri is holding, and the girl in Yamato, and the one who counts the no's. That's how we went in, the night you put the captain's hand on me. Sideways. Along them.

...if you burn them, we're eleven people in eleven rocks who can't hear each other any more.

...I was in a sack for thirty years where I couldn't hear anyone. I can do it. I'm just telling you.

The hall was very quiet.

...and the other thing.

...it's pulling because it can't see. If you cut the thread, it still can't see. So it'll come down here to look. Instead of up the thread.

...I'd rather it pulled on me than walked into the hall.

In the morning, Qin Hao closed his notebook.

"Fine," he said. "Not the threads." He did not look happy about it. "Then what?"

Nobody answered.

They had been at the long table since six. Kwan with three diagrams. Gao Min with the chart. Su Mingli, who had come in at seven with her arms folded and said she could hold one stone down with gravity for about four hours before she needed a day to recover, and there were eleven, and fifty-seven days, and she would like someone to do the arithmetic for her because she was too tired. Old Seven at the bronze door. Lingyue on the stairs with the flame in her hand, the size of a peach now, which she kept looking at as though it belonged to someone else.

And Xiaoyu at the end of the table with Shi Lei, who had come at eight with his steel spear, the one the Defense Corps had issued him on Awakening Day, plain and new, which he had not stopped carrying since, and who had sat down without being asked and had not said anything for three hours.

He said something now.

"It opens what you say."

They all looked at him. He went red, the way he did, and held on to his spear.

"The stone," said Shi Lei. "It used to read you and open what it found. Now it asks, and it opens what you say. That's what happened to me. I said what I wanted and it opened that." He looked at Xiaoyu. "It opened Scribe in her because she said Scribe. It's not on the tree. Seed said. It made it up because she said it." He swallowed. "So what if somebody says they want to hold it down?"

The long table was silent.

...say that again.

"What if somebody puts their hand on it," said Shi Lei, slowly, "and it asks what do you want to become, and they say: someone who holds you down. So you stay." He looked at the chart on the wall. "And it opens that. In them. And they hold it."

I have never run that.

I am running it now.

Seed went quiet for a long time. He felt it, in the place above his eyes: the hum of it, a city at night, every light on.

The yeses inside cannot hold the stones down, because they are in them. You cannot push down a chair you are sitting in.

But someone standing outside, with their hand on it, who has asked to be opened toward holding.

The stone opens a little of what you say. That is what the old knot said: OPEN IT THAT WAY, A LITTLE. Wen Jia took the grading out. She did not take the opening out.

A little holding. In one person. With their hand on a stone.

It is not much. It is like one dead soldier with one thread of pale fire, against a branch the size of a mountain range.

But there were fifty thousand of them.

"How many people," said Gao Min, "to hold one stone?"

I do not know. Nobody has ever been opened toward holding. I do not know how much holding is in one person.

But I know the pull grows every night. So whatever the number is tonight, it will be bigger tomorrow.

Su Mingli unfolded her arms.

"Then test it," said the King of Gravity. "Tonight. Before anyone puts it on the television." She looked at the boy with the steel spear. "You first. It was your idea."

They went at midnight, before the Collector came.

Shi Lei went up the three steps of the platform with his steel spear in one hand and stopped in front of the warm black stone. The crack in the floor round its base was wider than it had been in the morning. Gao Min had a laser level set up on a tripod at the foot of the steps, pointed at a pencil mark she had made on the face of the stone at six, and a notebook open on her knee.

"Hello," said Shi Lei, to the stone. "It's me again. Shi Lei. I'm going to touch you."

He put his hand on it.

[What do you want to become?]

The letters formed in the air above the platform, under Xiaoyu's three lines, which were still hanging there. He looked up at them. Everyone in the hall looked up at them.

"Someone who holds you down," said Shi Lei. "So you stay. So it can't take you."

Nothing happened.

Then, slowly, in the hand that was not the Stele's:

[That is not a becoming. That is a doing. Do you want to be it, or do you want to do it?]

Shi Lei stared at the letters. He went red.

...sorry, said Wen Jia, through the staff, to the hall, a little embarrassed. ...it's the stone. It's very particular about the grammar. I'm still working on it.

At the foot of the steps, Gao Min made a sound that, in anybody else, would have been a laugh.

Shi Lei looked down at his hand on the stone. At the steel spear in his other hand. At the front row, where his father was not sitting tonight, because his father was on the night shift at the coal yard, because somebody had to be.

"Be it," he said. "I want to be someone who holds things down. So they stay. So nobody takes them." He held on to the stone. "That's what I wanted for the coal yard anyway. I just didn't have the word."

[Chosen.]

He felt it from the foot of the steps. Everyone did.

Not light. Weight. As if, for one moment, the air in the Awakening Hall had become a little heavier, all of it, all at once, and then settled. The boy on the platform with his hand flat on the black stone went very still, and set his feet, the way you set your feet in spear forms at five in the morning before the first thrust, and leaned.

Not on the stone. Into it. Down.

...there.

I can see it. Very small. A little holding, opened in him. Going down his arm into the stone. Down the stone into the floor.

Down.

At the foot of the steps, Gao Min was looking at the laser.

She did not say anything for a while. Then she wrote a number in her notebook, in pencil, and looked at it, and checked the laser again, and wrote it a second time.

"Point eight of a millimeter," said the acting director of the Awakening Bureau. "Down." She looked up at the boy on the platform. "It went down."

By three in the morning, there were forty-one.

Nobody called them. Xiaoyu sent one message, at a quarter past twelve, to her class, the ones who had awakened with her and the ones who had said I don't know and the ones who had said not this year: Shi Lei held the stone down. It asks you what you want. If you want to hold it, come. And they came. On bicycles, on foot, in pajamas, with their parents in coats behind them. Forty-one of them, through the side door with Gao Min's keys, up the three steps one at a time.

Some of them had been opened already, on Awakening Day, toward something else. Healer. Flier. Something that makes things grow. The stone asked them anyway. They said also this. And it opened that too, a little.

Some of them had said I don't know. They knew now.

And one of them, a thin girl with glasses who had walked up the steps on Awakening Day and down again without touching it and said not this year, went up and put her hand on the stone and said, when it asked: I still don't want to be anything. But I want you to stay. And the stone was quiet for a long moment, and then wrote, in the hand that was not its own:

[That is allowed.]

And she held it.

At ten to one, the Collector came, warm and patient, and rested on the hall, and on forty-one children with their hands on a black stone, and on Xiaoyu at the foot of the steps. And Xiaoyu asked it what it wanted, and it was quiet for a long while, looking at the children.

...I am still looking.

...but I would like to watch this. If that is allowed.

"It's allowed," said Xiaoyu. "Watching's free."

At three o'clock, Gao Min checked the laser.

"Lingzhou," she said. "Net change since midnight. Zero." She wrote it down. Then she looked at it, and crossed it out, and wrote it again, bigger. "It stopped."

At 5:40 in the morning, the Number Nine bus left the depot on East Ring Road.

It did not have a number in the window. Wen Hao had taken the number out. In its place, on a sheet of cardboard, in thick black marker, in square capitals that were not Xiaoyu's but were trying to be, he had written:

TO THE HALL. FREE.

It stopped at every stop. It was full by the second.

At 5:52, outside the Awakening Hall, the doors opened, front and back, with a hiss. Wen Hao counted to ten out loud. And then he did not close the doors. He sat with his steady hands on the wheel while forty people got off his bus and walked up the steps of the hall in the gray light, a bus driver's worth of people, a cleaner and two nurses and a man from the fish market and a schoolteacher and a grandmother with a stick, to put their hands on a black stone with a bright blue jacket on its shoulder and tell it they wanted it to stay.

On the radio at Kwan's belt, from Port Halvard, a heavy accented voice, out of breath, as if its owner had been walking fast:

"This is Sterling. I'm at my stone. There's a queue round the block. There's a woman at the front. She says she's Callum Ward's mother." A pause. "She says she wants to hold it down so the thing that took her son can't take anything else. She asked me if she was allowed." Another pause. "I'm a Force King. I've been deciding things for people for fifty years. I told her to ask the stone." A pause. "I'm putting that on the record."
