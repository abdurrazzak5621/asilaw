Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 66The White Stone

czverse8 min read

They went down to the fire in the dark, because there was nowhere else to go.

Su Mingli led. She walked across two kilometers of frozen glass in a straight line with her coat open and her hands empty and her face like a closed door, and the air around her bent very slightly with every step, the stars above her wavering like reflections in water. Behind her came the living, in a tight group. Behind them, in rows, the eighty-four soldiers of Third Battalion who were still with them, and Old Seven, and Iron Wall Zhao, and a drift of ash with an iron arrowhead for a heart.

The fire grew. The white stone grew. The two figures beside it did not move.

At fifty meters, Su Mingli stopped.

"That's close enough," she said.

"Of course," said Julian Mercer warmly. "Whatever you're comfortable with."

He was exactly as Fat Tan had described him and exactly as he had looked in the photograph on the steel table in the vault. Tall. Forties. A very good coat, dark gray wool, unmarked by forty days in the Wastes. Very good shoes, which had no frost on them at all. A pleasant, open, handsome face, the face of a man you would trust to arrange your hotel.

And his teeth. White. Even. Perfect. Catching the firelight every time he smiled, which was always.

"Mister Zhou," he said. "What a pleasure. I've wanted to meet you properly for a very long time. Ever since the cemetery, really. The first night." He tilted his head. "You were so loud."

I cannot see him.

Yuanshu, I can see him with your eyes. I cannot see him with anything else.

No law signature. No heartbeat. No warmth. Nothing.

It is like looking at a hole cut in the world in the shape of a man.

Beside him, on a folding camp stool by the fire, sat Gu Hanshan.

He looked older. Much older than on Concordia, forty days ago. His face had gone thin and gray. His cardigan was filthy. There was frost in his eyebrows. He held a cup of tea in both hands, steaming, and did not drink it, and when he looked up at Yuanshu across the fire, his eyes were very tired and very kind and very, very frightened.

"Hello, Yuanshu," he said. "You got my flask."

"I got it."

"I'm glad." Gu looked down into his tea. "I didn't know if you'd stop."

"Let me guess," said Su Mingli. "You can't open it."

Mercer's smile didn't change. But something at the edge of it tightened, very slightly.

"No," he admitted pleasantly. "I can't." He turned and looked at the white stone, three meters behind him, pale in the firelight. "Forty days. I've tried everything. Digging. Breaking it. Burning it. I've had the ground under it dug out to two meters, and there's nothing there. Just rock." He spread his hands. "The grave is there. I know it's there. I simply can't see it."

Of course he cannot.

The grave is Death Law. The staff is bones. Everything in that ground is dead.

He cannot see the dead. The thing that made him cannot see the dead.

He has been standing on top of it for forty days, blind.

"So you need me," said Yuanshu.

"So I need you." Mercer nodded happily, as if a student had gotten a hard question right. "You can see it. You can talk to it. And from what I hear, it'll talk back, if you ask nicely." He gestured at the white stone, an usher showing someone to their seat. "Please. Be my guest."

"And then you take it."

"And then we discuss it." The smile widened. "Like civilized people."

Su Mingli took one step forward.

The air around her collapsed. That was the only word for it. The firelight bent inward toward her like light pulled into a drain. The frost on the glass around her feet cracked in a ring with a sound like a gunshot. Gu's cup of tea lifted out of his hands, hung in the air for a second, and spilled sideways, the tea falling not down but toward her in a curving brown arc that spattered on the glass.

Mercer didn't move.

"I wouldn't," he said gently.

He unbuttoned the top of his very good coat. Just the top button. And opened the collar a little, like a man who was too warm.

Under the collar, where his throat should have been, there was crystal.

Gray. Cloudy. Cracked. And in it, very small, a mouth. Human. Open.

And out of the mouth, thin as a hair, a silver thread ran down his chest and out from under his coat and away across the glass, south, along the ground, glittering in the firelight. And beside it, another. And another. Dozens. Hundreds. Running out from under the hem of his coat in every direction like the roots of a tree, across the plateau, into the dark.

The scavengers.

The thread from the scavengers did not go up. It went to him.

They all go to him.

Yuanshu, there are three hundred and twelve threads. Three hundred and twelve people. All across the Wastes. Every camp, every scavenger, every survey team, every lost soldier he has walked past in eleven years.

They are all connected to him.

"I collect," said Mercer, still smiling, buttoning his collar again. "On my travels. A little at a time. It's what I'm for." He looked at Su Mingli. "If you break me, Your Majesty, every one of those threads breaks with me. And I don't think the little embers at the bottom will survive that." He tilted his head. "Three hundred and twelve people. Most of them still alive. Out there in the dark. Breathing. Waiting for someone to come and sit beside them with a candle." His teeth gleamed. "Your choice, of course."

The pressure held for one long second.

Then it let go. The firelight straightened. The ring of cracked frost stopped spreading. Su Mingli stood very still on the glass with her fists closed at her sides and her face perfectly blank, and Yuanshu had never in his life seen anyone so angry.

"There," said Mercer kindly. "Civilized."

Gu Hanshan was staring at the brown splash of tea on the glass where his cup had spilled. He did not look up.

"You said you'd stop," he whispered. "You said if I brought you here, you'd stop collecting. You said..."

"I say a lot of things, Hanshan." Mercer didn't look at him. "So do you."

Yuanshu walked past him.

Not toward him. Past. Around the fire, around Mercer, around Gu on his stool, toward the white stone. He didn't look at any of them. He held the quiet, very carefully, the way you'd carry a full bowl across a room, and he heard Lingyue start to say his name behind him and stop.

Yuanshu.

I know.

If you open it, he will take it.

He can't see it.

He cannot see it in the ground. If you pick it up, it will be in your hands. Living hands.

He will see it then.

I know.

Then why...

Because Lu Xuanming said ask it. Not take it. Ask.

He stopped in front of the stone.

And I don't know what it'll say.

It was older than he'd thought.

Up close, in the firelight, the white survey marker was not smooth. It was weathered and pitted, its corners rounded by a hundred years of wind, and on its northern face, very faint, almost worn away, were letters. A grid number. A date. A surveyor's initials. Ordinary. Meaningless.

And below them, newer, scratched into the stone with something sharp, in a small quick perfectly neat hand:

Ask.

That was all. One word.

Yuanshu knelt on the frozen ground in front of the stone, the way he had knelt in a cemetery on the first night, and on a frozen pass, and in a crater, and in front of seven dead monks on a mountain. He took off his gloves. He laid both bare hands flat on the ground at the foot of the stone, on the cold rock that Mercer had dug out to two meters and found nothing in.

And he went quiet. All the way down.

Behind him, the fire crackled. Nobody moved. Mercer's smile had gone very still.

My name is Zhou Yuanshu, he thought, into the ground. I'm like the man who's buried here. He left me a note. He said, ask.

So I'm asking.

Is anyone there?

For a long time, nothing.

Then, under his hands, the rock grew warm.

Not hot. Warm. Like a stone that has been lying in the sun all day. The warmth spread outward from his palms in a slow ring across the frozen ground, and the frost melted in a circle around him, and steamed, and the steam rose white in the firelight around his kneeling shape like breath.

There.

Under the rock. Three meters down. Mercer dug to two.

Not one presence. Two.

One is very faint. Almost gone. Quiet. A man. Old. He chose quiet a long time ago, and he is keeping it.

And one is not faint at all.

She is wide awake.

And she has been waiting for someone to ask her that question for thirty-one years.

A voice came up out of the ground.

Not into his head. Out loud. Into the air. Clear and dry and a little impatient, a woman's voice, the voice of someone who had spent her whole life in front of a blackboard telling children to sit down and pay attention.

"Finally," said the voice. "He said someone would come. He said they'd ask. He was always too optimistic." A pause. "You took your time, young man."

Behind him, at the fire, Julian Mercer stood up very slowly from where he had been leaning, and stared at the white stone, and for the first time since they had come down the ridge, he was not smiling at all.

He had heard it.

He could not see her.

But he had heard.
