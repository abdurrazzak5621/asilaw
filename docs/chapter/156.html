Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 156Uncounted

czverse9 min read

"You counted me," said Ifeoma Adeyemi. "Didn't you, child."

She had wheeled herself in under the dead ceiling fan without anyone pushing her, which Yuanshu had not known she could do, and she sat now at the end of the table with the hand-drawn chart of her coast spread in front of her, weighted at the corners with coffee cups, and looked at the three marks Seed had drawn on it in pencil. Port Abiri. Kesse. Sandu.

Yes. You hold the pipe. At Port Abiri, then at Kesse. Without the pipe, the drowned cannot hear the orders, and the Field cannot reach the bottom with anything but itself.

"You counted me as a pipe."

...yes.

"I am the King of the Tide of the Southern Compact," said the old woman, without raising her voice. "I have held this sea for a hundred and ninety years. I have fought in eleven wars. I lifted a spire out of that bay and drowned it in the deep with my bare hands, a year ago, when I was two hundred and thirty years old." She put one thin finger on the pencil mark off Sandu. "And you have counted me as a pipe."

Nobody in the room said anything.

I am sorry.

I counted what you showed me. I did not count what you did not.

"That's all right, child. That's what counting is." She did not take her finger off the chart. "Now count this."

She told them.

It took her ten minutes, in her dry, level voice, with her finger on the chart and her eyes on the pencil mark off Sandu. Yuanshu listened to all of it without interrupting, and somewhere about the fourth minute he understood what she was going to say at the end, and his hands went cold on the table.

The trunks rode the morning tide. She had told them that. The deep cold water that came up every dawn at the foot of the shelf, because the land breeze pushed the warm water at the top away from the shore. The trunks grew inside it, the way a seed grows in a crack in a wall. They did not have to climb. The sea lifted them.

"So I will stop the sea lifting them," said Ifeoma Adeyemi.

...that is the whole upwelling. At Sandu. Ten kilometers of coast.

"Yes."

You would have to hold it down. Every morning. The cold water at the bottom wants to come up. The warm water at the top is being pushed away. You would have to hold the whole sea in place against the wind.

"Not every morning. One." She moved her finger. "Your numbers say that the Sandu trunk surfaces four hours after the trunk at Kesse. Late afternoon. That's because it's furthest down the coast and its morning tide comes last. If I sit on the Sandu tide that morning, it doesn't come. And if it doesn't come, the trunk doesn't rise. It sits at the bottom, waiting for the next morning." She looked up at last. "And by the next morning, you'll have finished at Kesse, and you can come down the coast and kill it on the bottom with your drowned and your soldiers and your girl with the swords."

One day.

"One day."

...running.

Seed ran it.

Yuanshu felt it run, a thousand times and then another thousand, and felt the shape of it settle on the inside of his eyes before Seed said anything, and knew from the shape what it was going to say.

It works.

If Ifeoma holds the Sandu upwelling for one dawn, the Sandu trunk does not surface. Eleven thousand people on the river road get one more day. That is enough to move all of them.

Port Abiri and Kesse without the pipe: fifty-two percent and forty-one percent. Not seventy-one and sixty-four. The drowned will not hear the orders well enough. Many of them will burn.

And Ifeoma.

It stopped.

"Say it," said Ifeoma.

Holding ten kilometers of sea against the wind for three hours is more Tide Law than you used on the spire in the bay. More than the boat. More than anything I have ever measured from you.

You are two hundred and thirty-one years old.

Probability that you are alive at the end of the morning: seventeen percent.

The old King nodded once, the way someone nods at a price they had already guessed.

"Seventeen," she said. "That's more than I thought. You're a kind machine."

I am not being kind. I am counting.

I would like very much to be wrong.

"No," said Lingyue.

She had been standing at the window with her arms folded, both swords on her back, looking out at the dark bay. She did not turn around.

"You don't get to say no," said Ifeoma, mildly. "You're nineteen. I was holding this sea before your grandfather's grandfather was born."

"I'm not saying no to you. I'm saying no to the numbers." Lingyue turned around. "Fifty-two percent and forty-one percent. Without the pipe. Because the drowned can't hear the orders and the pale fire can't reach the bottom." She looked at Yuanshu. "Who's carrying the pale fire?"

"You are."

"How?"

"The same way as at the Western Pass. Inside the Field, your Light turns pale. The Field reaches the bottom. So you stand on the harbor wall, and you..."

"...and I shine it down three kilometers of seawater," said Lingyue. "And Light goes about two hundred meters in seawater before it's gone. Seed told me that this afternoon. I asked." She folded her arms tighter. "So it doesn't reach. So I'm not there. So I'm counted as a girl standing on a wall with two swords doing nothing. Again."

...I did count you that way.

"Then count me differently."

I do not know how.

"Neither do I yet," said Shen Lingyue. "Give me until dawn."

He asked Ifeoma afterward.

Not in front of the others. He went out after her when she wheeled herself back down the harbor wall in the dark, and walked beside her chair, and when she stopped at the end of the wall, where she had held a pipe of still water three kilometers into the sea two nights ago, he crouched down beside her so that their faces were level.

"Ifeoma," he said. "I'm going to ask you. You don't have to answer."

"I know how you ask, child."

"What do you want?"

The oldest King on Blue Star looked out at the sea for a while. Past the end of the wall. Past the mouth of the bay. Past the point, where the water turned dark, where a man named Emeka had gone over the side of a white fishing boat in a squall a hundred and forty years ago.

"I want to hear seven Architects say sorry to Akosua Mensah on that hill," she said. "In five months. I told them I'd be alive. I want to be alive." Her hands were folded on the blanket. "I want my household to stop fussing. I want to go home to my palace and sit in my chair by the window and watch the fishing boats go out in the morning. I want to see what you do when you're a Lord, because I think it's going to be interesting." She was quiet. "And I want eleven thousand people on the river road at Sandu to get to the hills. That most of all."

"You can't have all of them."

"No. I can't." She did not look away from the sea. "Ask me the rest, child. Properly. The way you do."

He did.

"Will you go to Sandu, and hold the tide, knowing what Seed said?"

"Yes," said Ifeoma Adeyemi. "Yes."

He did not say anything. He could not, for a moment.

"Good," said the old King. "Now you've asked me, and I've answered, and it's mine. Not yours. You don't get to carry this one, child. I've watched you carry things for most of a year. You carry them very badly." She reached out with one thin hand and straightened his collar, the way a grandmother does before a photograph. "This one's mine."

She left for Sandu at two in the morning, by sea.

Not in the white boat. She would not take it. She left it tied up at the end of the harbor wall, rocking gently on the black water, with its blue stripe and no engine, and went south in one of her household's launches, with two of her people at the tiller and her wheelchair lashed down in the stern.

Before she went, she asked to be taken up the hill.

Yuanshu carried her. She weighed almost nothing. He carried her up the path between the white stones in the dark, with Old Seven walking behind him and the lights of the empty town below, to the place near the top where a woman in a black dress was sitting on the grass beside two stones, awake, with a blanket around her shoulders and her basket beside her.

Akosua Mensah looked up.

"Your Majesty," she said. She did not stand. She was too tired, Yuanshu thought, or too stubborn, or both.

"Don't call me that," said Ifeoma. "Nobody's called me that since the war before last." He set her down on the grass beside the widow, carefully, and she arranged her blanket over her knees and looked at the two stones. KOJO MENSAH. KWAME MENSAH. 19. "I came to tell you something."

"I'm not getting on a bus."

"I know. I wouldn't either." The old King looked at the sea. "I told seven Architects to come back in a year and say it again. To you. And to me." She was quiet. "I may not be here for it."

Akosua Mensah turned and looked at her.

"I'm going down the coast in an hour," said Ifeoma Adeyemi. "To sit on the sea for a morning. The boy's machine says I won't come back. It's a kind machine, so it may be wrong. But in case it isn't." She put one thin hand flat on the grass between them. "When they come back, you'll have to hear it for both of us. Make them say it twice as long. Two hours. I'll know if they cheat."

The widow did not say anything for a long moment.

Then she reached over, slowly, and put her cracked fisherman's hand on top of the old King's.

"Two hours," said Akosua Mensah. "I'll count."

"Good," said Ifeoma. "Somebody should. The machine's busy."

At four in the morning, three hours before dawn, Yuanshu stood on the harbor wall at Port Abiri with the staff in his hands and the Field folded over the bay, and the Spear Corps in their rows behind him, and the twins on the wall, and Bai Lu and Hu Dabao at the top of the beach where a hundred and fifty-one people in white were still sitting on the sand, waiting to be moved.

Lingyue was not there.

She had gone off along the beach at midnight, alone, with both swords, and nobody had seen her since. He had not gone after her. She had asked him not to.

Sixty-one hours from the first model. Three hours left.

Ifeoma's launch is at Sandu. She is sitting at the end of the river jetty with her eyes shut. She has not started yet. She is waiting for dawn.

Two thousand eight hundred and forty-six of the drowned are standing on the bottom of the sea around the Port Abiri trunk, banked, in the dark, in the cold, waiting for orders they will not be able to hear very well.

Captain Efua Asante has asked if she may command them. Emeka has asked if he may stand at the front.

Yuanshu. I have run it again. It is still fifty-two percent.

"I know," said Yuanshu.

Out on the bay, very faintly, in the starlight, the water off the mouth of the harbor had begun to steam.
