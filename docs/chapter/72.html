Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 72The Granary

czverse8 min read

They went down into the murk at dawn, and the world went gray.

Not dark. Gray. The haze closed over them twenty steps below the ridge, and the sky disappeared, and the ice disappeared, and there was only a dim, even, shadowless light coming from everywhere and nowhere, and the threads.

They were everywhere now. Close. Drifting past at head height, at knee height, brushing faces and sleeves like cobwebs in an old attic. Silver. Fine. Moving. All flowing the same way, slowly, north, the way water in a slow river all flows downstream. Billions of them, so many and so close that the air itself seemed to shimmer.

None of them touched the living for long. They slid past. Over. Around. Looking for something else.

"Don't touch them," Su Mingli had said on the ridge. "Don't cut them. Don't burn them. Every one of those goes to someone. Somewhere. A hollow in a ward. A boy in a teahouse." She had looked at Lingyue, at Qin Hao. "They're not attacking. They're just going home. Let them."

So they walked through them. Single file. Eyes half closed. Quiet.

And around them, invisible in the murk, the dead went too.

Not all of them. Su Mingli had asked for a thousand. A thousand had said yes. They walked in a loose wide ring around the nine living, a hundred meters out in every direction, gray shapes in the gray haze that you could only see when one came close. The rest waited on the ridge, at Grid Nine, around the frozen blockhouse, in their great patient circle.

Old Seven walked directly in front of Yuanshu. Third Battalion walked around them in a tight square, seventy-two strong, Sergeant Huo at the corner, the way they had walked through the river of the dead.

The stalk is eleven kilometers north.

The Monarchs are circling it. Three of them. I can hear them.

They are not hunting. They are walking in a ring around the base. Slowly. Like guard dogs on chains.

We have time.

The ground under the haze was strange.

Not ice. Not rock. Something smooth and pale and faintly warm, like the inside of a shell, like the ground in the field above the sky. In places it rose in low ridges. In places it fell away in shallow bowls. And everywhere, standing on it, in the gray, there were shapes.

Beasts.

Rift beasts. Dozens of them, then hundreds. Things with too many legs. Things with horns. A lizard the size of a house. A pack of wolf-things with gray fur gone white with frost. A bird with a wingspan wider than a truck, standing on the pale ground with its wings half folded.

They were alive. Breathing. Very slowly. All at the same rhythm.

And every single one of them was standing perfectly still, with its head turned north.

Hollow.

All of them. Harvested. The bodies left standing.

This is where the beasts went. The ones that sat in rows facing north, before the Black Tide. Lu Xuanming wrote about them.

They came here. And they were cut.

Bai Lu walked past a wolf-thing with its head turned north and its eyes open and empty and frost on its eyelashes, and put out one hand, very gently, and touched its shoulder as she passed. Not healing. Just touching. The way you'd touch a stranger at a funeral.

It did not notice.

At the fourth kilometer, Yan Shuang stopped.

She stood in the gray with her one hand in her coat pocket and did not move. The threads drifted past her face.

"Here," she said, very quietly. "This is where."

Yuanshu came up beside her.

In front of them, the pale ground fell away into a wide shallow bowl, perhaps a kilometer across. The haze was thinner here. You could see across it.

It was full of people.

Standing. In rows. Hundreds of rows. Thousands of people. Men and women, old and young, in the clothes they had been taken in. Wastes scavengers in furs. Soldiers in white Northern Union uniforms and gray Huaxian ones and dark green. Survey teams in orange. Farmers. A woman in a nurse's uniform. A man in a business suit, with a briefcase still in his hand. Children.

They stood in perfect rows, shoulder to shoulder, filling the bowl from edge to edge, all facing north. All breathing. Slowly. At the same rhythm. Frost on their shoulders and in their hair. Eyes open. Empty.

And from the crown of every head, a thread rose, silver and fine, up into the gray, north, toward the stalk.

A granary.

This is where the trucks brought them. To the edge of the ridge. And the Monarchs came and carried them down here.

And they stand here. Kept. The bodies alive. The minds above.

Waiting.

I am counting. Eleven thousand four hundred.

Yan Shuang walked down into the bowl.

Nobody stopped her. She walked down the gentle slope of pale ground between the first two rows of standing people, slowly, looking at the faces. Her boots made no sound. The threads drifted past.

She stopped in the fourth row in front of a girl.

Perhaps sixteen. A Wastes girl, in a patched fur coat, with a knitted hat pulled down over her ears and a braid hanging out from under it. Frost in the braid. Her face was round and young and empty, turned north.

Yan Shuang stood in front of her for a long time.

"Her name's Ama," she said, without turning around. Her voice was perfectly flat. "Scavenger family. I picked her up at a camp on the Black Ridge, three years ago. She sat in the back of my truck for four days. Right behind the cab. I talked to her the whole way. Told her about my sister. About my dog. About the time I got lost in a whiteout for three days and ate my boot." She reached out with her one hand and brushed the frost off the girl's braid. Gently. "I brought her to the ridge. I got out. I didn't look." She was silent. "That was the rule."

The girl did not move.

Yan Shuang stood there in front of her with her hand on the frosted braid, and her weathered face did not change at all, and her shoulders did not shake, and she did not make a sound.

But the threads drifting past her bent. Very slightly. Away from her. The way grass bends away from wind.

She is not loud.

She is not thinking anything.

She is just standing there, holding that girl's braid, being sorry.

It is very quiet. It is the quietest thing I have ever heard.

Su Mingli came down into the bowl.

She stood at the end of the row and looked out over eleven thousand four hundred people standing in the gray. Her face was stone.

"We take them," she said.

Nobody answered.

"We take them out. All of them. Up the ridge. To Grid Nine. Away from here." She turned to Yuanshu. "The threads stay attached. You saw it in Lingzhou. The fourteen moved across a city and the threads went with them. These will too." Her voice was low and very hard. "And when you go up there, if you can send them back down, the way you sent the three hundred and twelve, they'll be somewhere warm when they wake up. Not in a bowl at the bottom of the world with frost in their hair."

"The Monarchs," said Lingyue quietly.

"Can't see the dead." Su Mingli looked out into the gray, where a thousand gray shapes stood waiting in a ring. "And these people can't think anything for them to hear." She looked back at Yuanshu. "Ask them. The dead. Ask if they'll carry."

He asked.

He stood at the edge of the bowl with the white staff planted in the pale ground, and went quiet, and reached out through the small skull to the thousand who had come down into the haze with them. And past them, up the slope, eleven kilometers back, to the eighteen thousand waiting at the ridge.

Eleven thousand people. Alive. Hollow. Standing in a bowl at the bottom of the world.

Will you carry them home?

He didn't need Seed to count.

The answer came up through the staff all at once, from nineteen thousand four hundred and two of the dead, like a single word spoken by a crowd.

Yes.

It took them all day.

The dead came down out of the haze from the ridge in a long gray stream, eighteen thousand of them, walking through the beasts and the threads without a sound. They came down into the bowl and moved among the rows. And they lifted.

Two of the dead to each living person. One at the shoulders, one at the knees. Gently. Carefully. The way you'd lift someone asleep. And they turned, and carried them out of the bowl, up the slope, south, through the gray, toward the ridge. In a long, slow, silent procession, eleven kilometers long, the dead carrying the living, threads trailing silver from each hollow head behind them like the tails of kites.

Sergeant Huo and Third Battalion carried the children.

Yan Shuang carried Ama herself. With one hand, and her shoulder, and her teeth set, the girl's frosted braid hanging down her back. Sokolova walked beside her, one hand under the girl's knees, and said nothing.

The Monarchs have not noticed.

They are still circling the stalk. Seven kilometers north.

They cannot see the dead. They cannot hear the hollows.

To them, the granary is just slowly... emptying. Like water draining out of a bowl. With nothing there to drain it.

At dusk, the last row was lifted.

Yuanshu stood at the edge of the empty bowl in the gray light and watched the last two dead soldiers carry the last hollow person up the slope and away into the haze. A man in a business suit. They had left his briefcase. One of the dead had come back for it, and picked it up, and carried it after him.

The bowl was empty. Just pale ground, and frost, and eleven thousand four hundred sets of footprints in it where people had stood for years.

Seven kilometers north.

The Monarchs have stopped.

He went still.

All three of them. They have stopped circling.

They are turning.

Their threads are going out. Sweeping. South. Toward the granary.

Yuanshu, they have noticed it is empty.

Far off in the gray, to the north, a sound began. Not a wail. A shriek. Three shrieks. High and sharp and tearing, like metal ripping, like the sound Mercer had made on the plateau when his face split open.

The ground under their feet trembled.

"Up," said Su Mingli. Very quietly. Very fast. "Everyone. Up the ridge. Now. Quiet. Don't run. Don't think. Walk."

They walked.

Behind them, in the gray, something enormous was coming. Three somethings. The ground shook with each step.

And in front of them, eleven kilometers away, at the top of the ridge, the eighteen thousand dead were laying eleven thousand four hundred living people down on the ice around the frozen blockhouse of Grid Nine, gently, one by one, in the dark, in rows. And turning around. And forming a wall.
