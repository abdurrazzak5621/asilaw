Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 33The Broker Who Kept Receipts

czverse8 min read

Fat Tan did not hurry.

He finished writing the line he was on. He blotted it with a scrap of paper. He closed his ledger, put his pen in the crease to keep his place, and picked up his cup of tea, and only then did he heave himself up off his folding chair and waddle over to the cart.

He was even bigger standing up. A round, soft, heavy man in his sixties with a padded vest straining over his stomach and small, quick, clever eyes in a face like a dumpling. He looked at the scanner screen. He looked at the blue tarp. He sipped his tea.

"Huh," he said.

"Core reading," said the man in orange. He was still looking at Yuanshu. "Inside the ribs. I've never seen a number like that on a dead one."

"Neither have I." Tan turned his small eyes on Yuanshu. They took in the knitted cap, the cheap mask, the stained jacket, the hands on the cart handle. They stayed on the hands. "You want to sell him, son?"

"Depends what you're offering."

Tan smiled. It made his face into a sort of cheerful moon. "Two hundred thousand. Right now. Cash. More than this whole row is worth together." He sipped his tea again. "Because I think I know what that number is, and I think if I'm right, there's only one person in the world who makes them."

The man in orange had gone very still.

Tan reached out with one plump finger and lifted the edge of the tarpaulin, just a little. Just enough to show the skeleton's left forearm, lying along its side. And the old, crooked lump halfway down the bone, where it had once snapped and grown back wrong.

"I've weighed eleven thousand soldiers at this table," he said mildly. "I know a break when I see one. And four hundred million people watched that arm come up in a salute in a cemetery, in the mist, on the morning news."

He set his teacup down on the edge of the cart.

"Hello, Mister Zhou," said Fat Tan.

Everything happened at once.

The man in orange dropped the scanner and went for the pistol at his hip.

The blue tarp exploded upward.

Old Seven came off the cart in a single motion, as if he had been lying on a spring. The gray flame in his chest blazed up from nothing to white. His spear came out from under him where it had been hidden along his spine, and the butt of it swung around in a short flat arc and hit the man in orange across the wrist with a sound like a snapped branch. The pistol went spinning away under the brass scales.

Somebody screamed.

The noise of the market changed in a heartbeat, from the cheerful roar of buying and selling to the jagged roar of a crowd that has seen a dead man stand up. Lanterns swung. People ran. Somewhere a stall went over with a crash of bones.

Down the row, the two guards from the entrance were coming at a run, rifles up.

"Down!" shouted Qin Hao.

He didn't throw fire. He threw light. A flare of pure white flame from his open hand, not hot, just blinding, a sheet of it that went off in the faces of the running guards like a camera flash the size of a house. They staggered, blind, rifles swinging wild.

One of them fired anyway. The shot went high into the lanterns. A string of red paper globes burst into flame and fell, trailing sparks, onto a stall of beast bones.

They are going to start shooting at noise.

There are four hundred people in this hollow.

And ten hollows still on that bench who cannot run.

Yuanshu stood still in the middle of it.

He could feel the dead all around him. Thousands. In heaps on the stalls, in bundles with tags, in crates on trucks, under his feet in the black glass where forty years of the Wastes had buried them. He could have raised all of them. It would have been the easiest thing in the world. One thought. A thousand skeletons standing up in a market lit by burning lanterns, and every guard and broker and buyer on their knees.

Nobody had asked them.

Nobody had ever asked them anything.

No, he thought.

I know.

I was not going to suggest it.

I have a different idea. It requires someone who is already awake.

A kilometer away, on the dark top of the north gate wall, something shifted.

The first arrow put out the lantern above the guard on the left.

The second went through the barrel of his rifle, just in front of the trigger guard, and split it like a stick of bamboo.

The third took the rifle out of the hands of the guard on the right, and pinned it by its strap to a wooden post.

They came in out of the dark in a line, one after the other, each a thin gray streak with no sound at all, a kilometer of night crossed in a heartbeat. Lanterns went dark. Guns flew out of hands. A man raising a pistol from behind a stall found his sleeve pinned to the stall's wooden upright, and stood there pulling at it with a look of total bewilderment.

Not one arrow touched a person.

Thirty-one shots in nineteen seconds.

She would like it noted that the market is very poorly lit and she still did not miss.

In the sudden dark and silence, the market emptied.

People went over the broken concrete walls of the bunker in every direction, out onto the black glass, running for the trucks parked in the dark. Engines started. Headlights swung. In under a minute, the hollow under the swaying, half-dark lanterns was almost empty.

Almost.

Fat Tan was still there.

He had not run. He was sitting back in his folding chair at his table under his white lantern, which no arrow had touched, with his ledger clutched to his chest in both arms, like a child with a teddy bear.

He watched Old Seven walk toward him across the trampled ground, spear lowered, the gray flame burning. He watched Qin Hao come up on his other side with fire curling around his fingers. He did not look afraid. He looked tired.

"I'm not a fighter," he said, when they reached him. "I'm a bookkeeper. My father was a bookkeeper. His father sold coal." He tightened his arms around the ledger. "I just write things down."

"Everything?" said Yuanshu.

"Everything." Tan nodded at the book against his chest. "Every kilo. Every price. Every buyer, every seller, every truck. Twenty-two years. My father taught me: write it down, and nobody can ever cheat you." His small eyes moved from Yuanshu to Qin Hao to the skeleton and back. "I always thought, one day someone's going to come for this book, and I'd better be ready to decide who I give it to."

"And?"

Fat Tan looked down at the ledger in his arms for a long moment.

"Three months ago," he said quietly, "the van came for the bench like always. And when the hollows got up to walk to it, one of them, a Northern boy, very young, narrow face, he looked at me." Tan's voice changed. "Just for a second. Looked right at me. Like he was still in there somewhere, at the bottom, waving." He swallowed. "I haven't slept right since."

He held out the book.

"I'd like protection," said Fat Tan. "From whoever's buying them. Because when they find out you've got this, they're going to want me very badly. And I'd like," he added, "for somebody to find that boy."

They read it by lantern light on Tan's folding table while the fire on the bone stall burned itself out and Captain Lin Wen's Defense Corps came across the glass from the gate in a long line of torches.

The ledger was perfect.

Columns ruled in pencil. Dates. Weights. Prices. Seller names, some real, some obviously not. Buyer names. Twenty-two years of the dead, bought and sold, in a neat, careful, small hand, every line blotted.

The cores were in it. Heishui Coal and Power, twice, two years ago. Six other mining companies. A chemical plant. A man who bought one a year, every year, and paid in gold, and wrote his name as Mister Moon. Northern Gate Remains Recovery was in it, on every other page, as the seller.

And the hollows were in it.

Not many. One every few weeks. Sometimes three. Always the same buyer. Never a name. Only a company, and a delivery address, and a single initial, written by the buyer himself at the bottom of every receipt in a different, slanting hand.

Halcyon Medical Supply.

Deliver to: loading dock B.

— G.

Qin Hao read the delivery address twice. Then he put his finger on it and turned the book around so Yuanshu could see.

The address of loading dock B was 1 Riverside North, Lingzhou.

The Rift Research Institute.

G.

Two hundred keys to the roof.

One man who signs for the hollows.

I would like to know who in that building has a name that starts with G.

Yuanshu closed the ledger.

Across the hollow, Captain Lin's soldiers had reached the bench at the end of row four. They were kneeling in front of the ten people still sitting on it, wrapping blankets around their shoulders, talking to them gently, getting no answer. Private Liang stood beside Qin Hao, breathing slowly, his eyes on nothing, his hand in Qin Hao's because Qin Hao had put it there.

"There's one person at the Institute," said Yuanshu quietly, "that Lu has mentioned to me every time we've talked about the cores. The one who's been studying them for two years." He looked north, out over the dark glass, toward the city and the river and a gray building with a melted box on its roof. "The one Lu calls the smartest man he's ever worked with."

He stood up.

"Deputy Director Gu Hanshan."
