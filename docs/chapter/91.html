Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 91The Raft

czverse8 min read

They did not fall.

That was the first thing. He stepped off the edge of the field into the dark, and braced for falling, the way you brace at the top of a staircase you can't see. And his foot came down on something.

Pale. Smooth. Faintly warm. Like the inside of a shell.

He looked down.

He was standing on a disc of the field. A piece of it. Perhaps fifty meters across, perfectly round, perfectly flat, the same smooth pale warm ground he had walked on above the sky, floating in the black. Its edge was sharp as a coin's. Beyond it, in every direction, up and down and all around, there was nothing but the dark and the far small lights.

Behind him, one by one, the others stepped down onto it. Lingyue, with her hand on her sword. Qin Hao. The twins, gripping each other. Bai Lu. Sokolova. Then the dead, in rows. The Third Battalion. Iron Wall Zhao. Hundred-Arrow Lin. Old Seven, last, with both his arms, one a little grayer than the other.

And the Elder, standing at the very center of the disc, tall and pale, with her long hands pressed together and her silver eyes half closed.

Above them, where the far door had been, a single gold thread ran up into the dark. Thin. Shining. Stretching back the way they had come, toward a small white ring and a patched field and a vast lonely keeper.

"The keeper made it," said the Elder softly. Seed translated. "A little piece of the field. To carry you. It has never made anything to carry anyone before."

"It made it very carefully."

Sokolova knelt down and laid her bare palm flat on the pale ground. Then she looked up.

"It is warm," she said. "There is air." She breathed in, slowly. "It smells of nothing."

The field holds its own air. Its own warmth. Its own light.

As long as we stay on it, we are inside the keeper. A little.

As long as the thread holds.

"What happens if we step off the edge?" said Mo Xing.

The Elder turned her silver eyes on him.

"Do not step off the edge."

The Elder called it a raft. Lingyue called it the plate. The Mo twins called it the coaster, for reasons nobody could get them to explain. Old Seven did not call it anything. He walked to the edge on the first hour, and grounded his spear at the very rim, and stood there facing out into the dark, and did not move.

Within a day, the seventy-two of the Third Battalion stood with him. Around the whole rim of the raft. In a ring. Shoulder to shoulder. Facing outward.

The way the dead had always stood around the living.

There was no day. There was no night.

That was the hardest part, at first. Harder than the dark or the cold or the silence. The light on the raft came from the pale ground itself, soft and even and shadowless, and it never changed. The stars did not move. The far lights did not move. Nothing rose and nothing set.

Bai Lu kept a clock. A small wind-up travel alarm, brass, with a cracked face, that had been her grandmother's. She wound it every twelve hours and set it on the pale ground in the center of the raft, and when it said night, she made everyone lie down and close their eyes.

"You need to sleep," she said, very firmly, to Yuanshu, on what the clock said was the third night. "Even out here. Especially out here."

"I can't."

"Then lie down and pretend. Your body won't know the difference."

He lay down on the warm pale ground and looked up.

And up was the Star Sea.

He had seen it from the edge of the field. It was different, lying on your back in the middle of it.

It went on forever.

Not like the sky over Lingzhou, which was a ceiling, a dome, something you could imagine an edge to. This had no edge. No up. No down. Just dark, in every direction, so deep that his eyes kept trying to find a bottom to it and failing, and the far lights scattered through it like sparks drifting up from a fire that had burned out a very long time ago.

Some of them were stars. Some of them were not.

That one. The faint green. That is a field.

A world, with minds on it. The Elder showed me.

And that one. Blue. Very small. A field.

That one, gold. Another.

They are so far apart, Yuanshu. Each one alone in the dark. I do not think any of them know the others are there.

He lay on his back on the raft and looked at them. Small warm lights. Alone in the dark. Not knowing.

He thought about a teahouse in the old quarter of Lingzhou. Fourteen candles in a dim room. Strangers sitting beside strangers in silence.

He thought it was the same thing. Only bigger.

The Elder steered.

Not with a wheel. Not with her hands. She stood at the center of the raft with her eyes half closed and her long fingers pressed together, and listened, and the raft moved.

Through currents.

He did not understand at first. Then, on the fifth day, Seed showed him. Lent him its eyes, the way it had lent them to Lingyue at the white stone.

And he saw that the Sea was not empty.

It was full of threads.

Old. Faint. Gray and silver and very fine. Running through the dark in long streams, like the threads that had run across the Wastes toward the stalk. Millions of them. Crossing and recrossing the black between the far lights, in great slow curving rivers, some thick and bright, some so faint they were barely there.

The paths keepers have walked.

Every keeper. Every Hunger. For as long as there have been keepers and Hungers.

They leave threads behind them, the way a ship leaves a wake.

The Elder is riding them. Like a boat on a river. She knows which ones go where.

And some of the rivers, he saw, were not silver.

They were gray. Thick. Dull. Cracked, like the crystal of the cores. Running through the dark in long jagged lines from one far light to another.

And at the end of each gray river, where a light had been, there was no light. Only a gray husk, turning slowly in the dark.

Hunger wakes.

Yuanshu. Do not look at those ones for very long.

On the seventh day, they passed one.

The Elder did not steer toward it. But the current they were riding curved, the way a river curves around a rock, and brought them close. Closer than anything in the Sea had come to them. Close enough to see.

A world. Small. Perhaps the size of Blue Star's moon.

Gray.

Not gray like rock. Gray like ash. Like the faces of the dead in Port Abiri. The whole world, from pole to pole, the same flat pale dead gray, with no clouds and no seas and no light anywhere on it. Its surface was cracked. Deep cracks, kilometers wide, running across it in every direction like the cracks on an old dry bowl. And sticking up out of the cracks, here and there, at the edges, jagged gray spires. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Dead. Crumbling. Like the spire in the bay at Port Abiri, if there had been a whole world of them, and they had all burned out.

The living stood at the edge of the raft, inside the ring of the dead, and stared at it as it turned slowly past.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"There were people there," said Bai Lu finally. "Weren't there."

"Yes," said the Elder. "A long time ago. Before your sun was lit."

"They had a word for asking. The keeper told me. They asked each other things all the time. It was the first thing they said when they met. 'What do you want?' Like 'hello.'"

"The Hunger did not ask them anything."

The gray world turned in the dark. Silent. Cracked. Its spires standing up out of its broken surface like the masts of sunken ships.

And then Old Seven, at the edge of the raft, lifted his skull.

Tilted it.

Toward the gray world. Toward one place on it. A deep crack near the dark side, running down into blackness.

And held it there.

His gray flame flickered.

He hears something.

Yuanshu. On the husk.

Someone is being very, very quiet.

He stared at the gray world.

"That's not possible," said Qin Hao. "The Elder said. Before our sun was lit. That's... that's billions of years."

"Yes," said the Elder slowly. She had turned her silver eyes toward the dark crack too. "It is not possible."

"And yet."

The current was carrying them past. Slowly. The gray world turning away. In a few hours it would be behind them, and then far behind them, and then a small gray speck in the dark, and then nothing.

Old Seven did not move his skull. He stood at the edge of the raft with his spear in his three-fingered hand, and his gray flame burning very bright, and listened.

Yuanshu walked to the edge and stood beside him.

"How long until the Hunger reaches Blue Star?" he said, without looking away from the husk.

One hundred and seventy-eight days.

"How long to reach it from here? If we keep going?"

The Elder thinks about forty. If the currents are kind.

"So we have time."

We have some time.

Not much.

He looked at the dark crack on the gray world. At Old Seven's skull, tilted toward it. At the gray flame, burning.

A soldier who can hear a child being quiet under a fire, and does not go, is not a soldier.

"Elder," he said. "Can you stop the raft?"
