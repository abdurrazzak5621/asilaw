Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 69The Kindest Man, Again

czverse9 min read

He told it sitting on his camp stool by the dying fire, with his hands around an empty cup, and nobody interrupted him once.

"I was nine," said Gu Hanshan. "Twenty years before the Black Tide. Nobody remembers it now. It was only one village."

The fire popped. The wind moved over the plateau. Su Mingli stood on the other side of the flames with her arms folded and her face in shadow. The others sat or stood in a loose ring around the fire: the living close, the dead behind them. Old Seven at Yuanshu's shoulder. The staff across Yuanshu's knees, glowing softly.

"A village in the north. Not far from the wall. Rice and pigs. My father mended nets." Gu turned the empty cup in his hands. "There was a ring in the sky for nine days. And then there was rain. Silver. Very fine. My mother said it was a blessing." He smiled, very faintly. "She stood in the yard with her face turned up and her arms open. I stood next to her. I was holding her hand."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Everyone in the village turned north. All at once. Three degrees. And then, a few days later, they went hollow. All of them. My father. My mother. My sister. The neighbors. The pigs, even. Two hundred people." He looked into the fire. "Except me."

"I thought for forty years that I'd been spared," said Gu. "Lucky. Too small to bother with. Something like that." He shook his head slowly. "I hadn't been spared. I'd been kept."

He touched his chest. Just over the heart.

"It took me. Learned me. Pulled everything I was up the thread in one breath, like everyone else. And then it did something it had never done before, or never that I know of. It sent a little bit back down. A little bit of me. Enough to walk. Enough to talk. Enough to grow up." His voice did not change at all. "And it left the thread in. Thin. Very thin. So thin nobody could ever see it. Not even me. For forty years, I didn't know it was there."

Not a law. Not his.

A thread. Since he was nine.

"It wanted an ear," said Gu. "A listener. On the ground. In the middle of the most important city near the field. And the best ear isn't a spy, or a machine, or a monster in a coat." He looked up at Yuanshu with his kind, tired, red-rimmed eyes. "The best ear is someone everyone tells everything to. Because he's kind. Because he listens. Because he brings soup when your back's broken." He smiled, and it was terrible. "Someone in this building has learned how to be loved. Wanqing was right. She just didn't know it wasn't me who'd learned it. It was what was in me."

Nobody spoke.

Then Su Mingli said, very quietly, from the shadows: "Was any of it you?"

Gu looked at her across the fire.

"All of it," he said. "That's the worst part, Mingli. All of it was me. The soup. The lemon tree. Holding Wanqing's coat at her wedding. Reading to the hollows in the evening. I meant every bit of it. I'm a kind man. I've always been a kind man." He held the empty cup very tightly. "And every kind thing I ever did, every secret anyone ever told me because I was kind, every time someone cried on my shoulder in the Institute canteen, it went up. All of it. Up a thread so thin I couldn't feel it. For fifty years."

"The mast," said Yuanshu.

"I built the mast because a voice in a dream told me the Institute needed better weather data." Gu laughed, a broken little sound. "I thought it was my idea. I thought most things were my ideas. The reports from the Western Pass that I took home and lost. The visit to the hollows. Fat Tan's cores, delivered to my loading dock, signed with my initial, in a hand I didn't remember writing." He looked down. "I'd wake up some mornings and there'd be things in my diary I didn't remember doing. I told myself I was overworked. I told myself I was getting old."

"When did you know?"

"When Wanqing told me." Gu closed his eyes. "Seven years ago. She came to my office late at night and shut the door and said, Hanshan, they're not empty. They're connected. And so are you." He opened his eyes. "She'd seen it. In my law signature. She was the only one who ever looked closely enough. She said she was going to find a way to cut it. She said she was going to find a way to cut all of them. And then she went into the basement, and through the cylinder, and I never saw her again."

"And after that?" said Yuanshu.

"After that, I knew." Gu's voice was very quiet. "And knowing didn't help. That's the thing nobody tells you. You'd think knowing would help. It didn't. The thread was still there. The voice in the dreams was still there. And I was still kind, and people still told me things, and it still listened." He looked at the heap of gray dust in the very good coat, blowing slowly away across the glass. "And then Mercer came. Seven autumns ago. In the car. He said he could help. He said if I helped him, he'd make it stop. I drove the car. I took Wanqing to buy her core because she asked me to drive, and Mercer was in the front seat with me, and I told her he was a colleague." He swallowed. "She knew he wasn't. She bought the core anyway. She asked every mind in it. I watched her do it, four nights in the basement, and I've never seen anything so brave."

He put the cup down on the glass beside his stool.

"Everything I've said to you since the ward," he said, "every word, was true. I made sure of that. It's the only thing I could control. I couldn't stop it listening. But I could make sure that everything it heard me say was true." He looked up. "Please don't believe me. I meant that too. I meant it most of all."

The fire had burned down to embers.

Yuanshu sat with the staff across his knees and looked at the man on the camp stool. The kindest man in the Institute. The favorite uncle. The lemon tree on the windowsill, watered on Thursdays.

"What do you want me to ask you?" he said.

Gu Hanshan looked at him with his tired, frightened, kind eyes.

"Whether I want it cut," he said. "The thread. I've wanted it cut for seven years, and there was nobody I could ask to do it, because anyone I told, it would hear. Anyone who tried, it would know." He looked at Lingyue, sitting beside Yuanshu with her sword across her knees. "I saw what your light did on Concordia. On the threads." He looked back at Yuanshu. "But I've had it in me since I was nine. It's not just in my head. It's in everything. I don't know what's under it anymore. I don't know if there's anything. If you cut it, I might not wake up." His voice did not shake. "So I need you to ask me. Properly. With your witness. The way you ask the dead. Because I don't trust myself to decide it alone. I've never been sure which of my decisions were mine."

Sergeant Vera Sokolova put down her notebook, and picked it up again, and opened it to a new page.

Yuanshu stood up. He walked around the embers of the fire, with the staff in his hand and Old Seven behind him, and knelt down on the frozen glass in front of the camp stool, so that his face was level with Gu's.

He went quiet. All the way down.

"Gu Hanshan," he said. "The thread in you has been there since you were nine. It listens to everything you do and everything anyone tells you, and it sends it up. If it's cut, it stops. And you might not wake up. Or you might wake up different. Or you might wake up exactly the same, without it. Nobody knows." He held the man's eyes. "Do you want it cut?"

Gu looked at him for a long time.

Then he said, "Yes."

"Are you sure?"

"No," said Gu Hanshan. "But yes."

Sokolova's pencil scratched.

Lingyue stood up.

She drew her sword. She did not light it. She walked around the embers and stood beside Yuanshu, and looked down at the old man on the camp stool, and for a moment her face was very young and very uncertain.

"I can't see it," she said quietly. "On Concordia I could see the threads. I can't see this one."

I can.

It is so thin. Thinner than anything I have ever seen. Thinner than the hollows'.

It comes out of the top of his head and goes up. Straight up. Toward the north. Toward the lens.

I will show you.

"Seed says it'll show you," said Yuanshu.

He reached up and touched Lingyue's wrist, very lightly, just above the hilt of the sword. And Seed did something it had never done before: it reached out through his fingers, the way it had reached out to the monk on the mountain, and touched someone else's mind directly, and lent her its eyes.

Lingyue gasped.

She was looking up. Above Gu's head. At something nobody else could see. Her eyes followed it up, and up, into the starry dark.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it's so long."

"Can you cut it?"

She set her feet. Not too wide. She raised the sword in both hands, and closed her eyes, and lit the very tip of the blade. A single bead of white-gold, small and hard and bright as a needle of sunlight.

"Hold still," she said to Gu Hanshan, very gently. The way you'd say it to a child at the dentist.

"I've been holding still for fifty years," said Gu. "I can manage another minute."

She cut.

There was no sound at all.

The needle of light passed through the air above Gu's head, a hand's width from his gray hair, and touched something nobody but Lingyue and Seed could see, and for one instant, the whole thread lit up. All of it. From the crown of Gu's head straight up into the night sky, a line of white-gold fire, thin as a hair, running up and up and up into the stars, kilometers long, a hundred kilometers, so long that it disappeared into the north before it ended.

Then it went out.

Gu Hanshan's eyes rolled back.

He slid sideways off the camp stool and onto the frozen glass and lay there without moving, his gray cardigan spread out around him, his reading glasses on their cord fallen across his face.

Bai Lu was there before anyone else. On her knees, both palms flat on his chest, green light flooding out of them, her face white with concentration.

"He's breathing," she said. "He's... his heart's going. It's going. It's going very fast." Her hands glowed brighter. "I don't know. I don't know what's under it. There's so much damage, it's been there so long..."

They waited.

The embers ticked. The wind blew. The heap of gray dust in the very good coat blew away a little further across the plateau.

Gu Hanshan opened his eyes.

He was looking straight up at the sky. At the stars. At the cold clear dark over the Northern Wastes.

He lay there for a long time without speaking. Bai Lu's hands were still on his chest. His breathing slowed. Steadied.

Then his eyes filled with tears, and ran over, and ran down into his gray hair on either side, and he didn't wipe them.

"It's so quiet," he whispered.

Nobody answered.

"I can't hear it," said Gu Hanshan. "For the first time since I was nine. I can't hear anything at all." He turned his head, slowly, on the frozen glass, and looked at Yuanshu. His face was the same face. Kind. Tired. Intelligent. The face of a favorite uncle. But something behind it had changed. Something had gone very still and very small and very, very ordinary. "Is this what it's like? For everyone? All the time?"

"Mostly," said Yuanshu. "When we're lucky."

Gu Hanshan closed his eyes.

"It's wonderful," he said.

Then he turned on his side on the frozen glass, like a child, and pulled his gray cardigan around him, and for the first time in fifty-one years, he slept without dreaming.
