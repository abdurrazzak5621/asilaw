Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 85The Bite

czverse9 min read

The harbor wall exploded.

Not from the front. From underneath. The roots had been there all along, under the water, under the sand, under the old stone blocks of the harbor wall, spreading silently through the night while an old King sat in her wheelchair and watched. And now, all at once, they came up.

Gray crystal burst out of the stones in a dozen places along the wall, thick as a man's arm, jagged, glowing faintly red in their cracks, rising, curling, reaching. Not at the living. Not at the King in her wheelchair, or the girl with the sword, or the boy with the fire in his palms.

At the staff.

"Down!"

Su Mingli's voice. The air bent. A crushing weight came down on the harbor wall like the flat of a giant's hand, and the nearest roots shattered under it in a spray of gray splinters. But behind them, more were already coming. Out of the stones. Out of the sand on the beach. Out of the water.

Yuanshu stumbled back with the staff clutched against his chest. A root came up out of the stones at his feet, fast as a striking snake, and Lingyue was there, a flash of white-gold, and the root fell in two pieces, smoking.

"It's not after us!" she shouted. "It's after the staff!"

"I know!"

Ifeoma Adeyemi rose out of her wheelchair.

Not on her legs. Her legs did not work. The sea lifted her. A column of blue water rose up out of the harbor and wrapped around her and lifted her, blanket and all, two meters into the air, and held her there, an old woman in a green headwrap sitting on a pillar of the sea.

She raised one thin hand.

The bay rose with it.

The whole bay. Two hundred meters of blue water, lifting, swelling, rearing up into a wall of sea as high as the spire, green and white and roaring. It hung there for one impossible second. And then the oldest King on Blue Star brought her hand down, and the sea came down with it, onto the spire, like a mountain falling.

The sound was like the world splitting. Spray went a hundred meters into the air. The harbor wall shook. The painted fishing boats on the beach were flung up the sand like toys.

When the water fell back, the spire was broken. Snapped off halfway up, its jagged top lying in the shallows, its cracked trunk leaning.

And from the broken end, as they watched, new crystal was already pushing out. Gray. Jagged. Red in the cracks. Growing.

Faster.

"It burns them to grow," said Ifeoma, from her pillar of sea, in her thin dry voice. "Every time I break it, it burns more of them to heal." Her black eyes were wet. "I am killing them faster by trying to save them."

"Stop hitting it!" Yuanshu was shouting before he knew it. "Everyone! Stop! Don't break it!"

"It's trying to eat your staff!" Qin Hao, with fire blazing in both hands, a root curling and blackening in the heat in front of him.

"I know! Burn the roots! Only the roots! Don't touch the spire!"

The roots kept coming. Out of the stones. Out of the sand. Out of the water. Lashing toward the white staff. Lingyue cut. Qin Hao burned. Mo Xing folded, again and again, sending lunging roots through ripples in the air into the open sea, his face going whiter with every fold, a thin line of blood running from his nose. Mo Shi shouted warnings three seconds before each one came. Su Mingli crushed. Bai Lu ran from one of them to the next with her hands glowing green, catching them as they staggered.

And the spire grew.

It is getting faster.

Every root they cut, it grows two.

It does not care what it costs. It does not care how many it burns.

It only wants her.

"...young man," said Jiang Lan. Very calm. Very dry. "Give me to someone and run the other way."

"No."

"It wants me, not you. I'm a staff. I'm not afraid of being eaten. I've been dead for sixty-eight years."

"No."

"Then think of something," said the schoolteacher, "because I'd rather not."

Someone took the staff out of his hands.

He turned, startled, grabbing.

Sergeant Vera Sokolova had it. Both hands around the white bones. Her ice-gray eyes on his.

"Marshal Orlov told me to bring you back alive," she said, very fast, very calm. "I could not do anything at Grid Nine. I could not carry anyone. I could only write names." She hefted the staff. "Let me carry something."

And before he could say anything, she turned and ran.

Up the harbor wall. Away from the spire. Up the narrow street into the silent town, between the white houses with blue shutters, past the dead in their doorways, with the white staff held high over her head, glowing, gray-white, like a torch.

The spire screamed.

Every root along the harbor wall turned at once and went after her. Out of the stones. Out of the streets. Gray crystal bursting up through the cobbles of the town in a long jagged line, chasing her, faster and faster, tearing up the road, smashing through a garden wall, splintering a bicycle.

And then, out of the shadows of the houses on either side of the street, a drift of ash with an iron arrowhead for a heart. And a broad old skeleton with a great empty shield of rusted iron.

They had come down too.

Hundred-Arrow Lin drew a bow that wasn't there and fired, and fired, and fired, and every black arrow struck a root as it lunged at Sokolova's back, and shattered it. Iron Wall Zhao dropped into the street behind the running sergeant and set his shield, and the roots hit it, and burst against it, and he did not move.

They were kindling. The spire could see them now. Could reach them.

They stood in the street anyway.

Old Seven had not moved.

Through all of it. The roots. The sea. The shouting. The running. He stood at the edge of the harbor wall, exactly where he had been, with his skull tilted toward the spire, listening.

And now, with every root in Port Abiri chasing a Northern sergeant up the hill, and the spire half turned away, leaning toward the town, its great red eye fixed on the glow of the staff, the old spear captain stepped off the edge of the harbor wall.

Into the bay.

The water closed over his skull. Yuanshu saw his gray flame for a moment, under the surface, like a lamp going down into the sea. Moving. Fast. Along the sandy bottom. Toward the spire.

"Seven!"

He hears someone.

Yuanshu. Inside the spire. Under the burning.

He has heard them since we came down the hill. That is why he came.

Someone in there is not burning.

Someone in there is being very, very quiet.

He came up out of the water at the foot of the spire.

Two meters ten of white bone, streaming seawater, climbing out of the bay onto the jagged gray crystal at its base. His gray flame blazed.

The spire felt him.

Its great red eye swung around. Away from the town. Away from the staff. Down. To the old soldier climbing up its side.

A root lashed out of the crystal, fast as a whip, and wrapped around his left arm. The arm he had broken thirty-one years ago on the eastern end of the wall. The arm that had never healed straight.

The crystal glowed red where it touched him.

And Old Seven's arm burned.

Not with fire. With nothing. The bone simply went gray, and then white, and then fell away into ash, from the shoulder down, the way paper falls away when you hold it to a candle. And the gray flame in his chest flickered, and dimmed, and went down to a single shaking bead.

He did not stop climbing.

One arm. One hand. His spear in it. Up the jagged gray side of the spire, crystal splintering under his feet, roots lashing at him, the red eye glaring down. Toward a place halfway up, where the crystal was cracked, and dark, and very still.

He drove his spear into the crack.

Not to break it. Not to kill it. Gently. Carefully. The way you'd slide a pole into a well to give someone at the bottom something to hold on to.

And held it there.

Yuanshu felt it.

Through the spear. Through Old Seven. Through the thin bright thread that ran between a Necromancer and every one of the dead he had raised. Down the spear shaft, into the crack, into the spire.

Into the fire.

Three thousand and eleven minds. Burning. Broken open. Screaming without sound. A roaring red inferno of everything they had ever been, being turned into heat and light and growth, crystal pushing out, roots spreading, a hunger feeding on itself.

And at the very bottom of it, in the dark, under all the burning, something small. Cold. Still.

Not burning.

Quiet.

A child.

She was sitting with her arms around her knees and her eyes squeezed shut and her face pressed against her knees, perfectly still, the way children sit when they are playing a game where you have to be a statue. The way you hold still when the person who is It is looking for you. Not moving. Not breathing. Not thinking anything at all.

She had been holding still for thirty-one hours.

She was playing a game.

When it came. Yesterday at dawn. In the church. A game where you have to be a statue.

So she was being a statue.

And it could not find her.

She is still being a statue, Yuanshu.

She does not know how to stop.

He walked off the edge of the harbor wall.

Not into the water. Ifeoma Adeyemi saw him go, and her hand moved, and the sea rose up under his feet like a floor of glass, and he walked across the top of the bay on it, toward the spire, toward an old soldier with one arm, clinging to its side with his spear driven into a crack.

He climbed up beside him.

He put his hand on the spear shaft, next to Old Seven's one bony hand. Their fingers touched.

And he did not shout. He did not ask anything of the fire. He went down the spear, into the crack, past the burning, to the bottom, to the small cold quiet shape sitting with her arms around her knees.

And he sat down beside her.

Not touching. Just there. The way you'd sit down beside a child at a funeral who is trying very hard not to cry.

Hello, he thought. Very softly. I'm not It. You don't have to stop being a statue. I just wanted you to know someone's here.

The small shape did not move.

My name's Yuanshu. What's yours?

A long, long silence.

Then, so faint it was barely there at all, from somewhere under the arms and the knees and the squeezed-shut eyes:

...Nkechi.

...am I still winning?

He felt Old Seven's bony fingers tighten on the spear shaft beside his own.

Yes, he thought. You're winning. You've been winning for thirty-one hours. That's the best anyone's ever played.

Nkechi. Do you want to stop playing now?

...yes.

...yes please. I'm so tired. And everyone's being so loud.

He looked up. Through the crack. Through the spire. Through the hot red roaring of three thousand people burning. Up. North. Toward a small white ring hanging over the end of the world, with a door left ajar.

And he asked.

Keeper. I need help. Please come.
