Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 226The Only Rings That Ask

czverse11 min read

They were married on the slope of the War Memorial Cemetery at dawn, because Lingyue said it was the only place in Lingzhou where everyone who mattered could come, and she meant the dead.

Zhou Wanqing had planned it in nineteen days, from her wheelchair, with a notebook and a telephone and Fat Tan as her runner, and she had told everyone exactly where to stand, and nobody stood there. They came up the long green slope from the river in the gray light in their thousands and stood wherever they could find room: between the rows of small gray stones, along the iron railings, in the branches of the cypresses, which the Academy students had climbed at four in the morning and refused to come down from. The city came. Not all of it. As much of it as could fit on a hill.

And the dead.

Not raised. Nobody had raised anybody. They had asked to come, through the Lattice, nine by nine, the way they asked everything now, and Adjutant Xu had carried the question up from the river gate and down from the Western Pass and out to the bottom of the sea off Kesse, and they had come. The First Legion stood along the top of the slope in their rows of nine, under the stone gate where Iron Wall Zhao faced out, and they had brushed the dust off their bones. Somebody had told them to. Ma Lianhua, Seed said, and then corrected itself: nobody had told them. They had done it themselves.

The Western Legion had come down a gold road out of the north at midnight, twenty-five thousand of them, with a sergeant at the front with a pick over his shoulder and an old general with one arm walking beside him. They stood on the far side of the river, along the bank, in the dark, and when the sun came up they were standing in it.

The Spear Corps stood around stone No. 7. Two hundred and seventy-two. Facing in, for once. Not out.

He had not slept.

He sat on the step of the groundskeeper's hut at four in the morning in the clothes his mother had chosen for him, which were dark and plain and fitted better than anything he had ever worn, with the staff across his knees and Old Seven standing by the corner of the hut in the dark, and tried to remember how to breathe.

Your heart rate is a hundred and twelve.

It was a hundred and four when you asked her. On this step.

It was seventy-one when the six lamps held you on the north wall.

I am telling you so that you know which of these things your body thinks is most frightening.

Thank you, Seed.

You are welcome.

I am recording this.

You said you wouldn't record the proposal.

This is not the proposal.

I asked her. Yesterday. Whether I could record this. She said: obviously. Somebody has to. Qin Hao's handwriting is terrible.

So I am recording all of it.

It is the first thing I have ever asked to keep.

Qin Hao came at five with a box.

He was the best man, because Lingyue had said that if anyone else did it, Qin Hao would write in his notebook that he had been passed over, and they would never hear the end of it. He stood at the corner of the hut in a dark suit with his hands healed pink and new where the white fire had come back up through them at Kirovsk, holding a small wooden box, and looked at Yuanshu on the step.

"I'm supposed to have the rings," he said.

"Yes."

"I don't have the rings." He held out the box. "Somebody else asked to carry them. I said yes. I'm writing down that I said yes. I want it on record that I'm a good best man."

Yuanshu opened the box.

It was empty.

He looked up. Qin Hao was not looking at him. He was looking at the step, beside Yuanshu's feet, where, while nobody was watching, a small pale thing on many thin legs had come out from under the eaves of the groundskeeper's hut and sat down on the brick in the gray light, all its limbs out, all its dark eyes open.

In two of its thin pale limbs, held very carefully, it had two rings.

Plain. Silver-gray. Not metal. He knew, before Seed said it, what they were made of.

Thread.

Walker-thread. From the silk on the north wall. The six that came apart when the First Legion struck. Fat Tan swept it up. He would not throw it away. He kept it in a box in the vault with the queens of hearts.

Hush asked him for two strands. It took them to Kwan. Kwan took them to a jeweler on Jinhua Road who had never seen anything like them and did not want to touch them.

Hush asked him. Very politely. For nine days. He said yes.

They are pressed. Not to glass. Just enough to hold their shape.

...in the sky there are rings that take.

...the counting one taught three of them to ask. One rested. One went home. One has the Elder in it.

...I thought there should be rings on the ground that ask too.

...these are made of what came to take. Now they are for two people who ask each other.

Yuanshu could not say anything.

Hush held out the rings.

...you can say no.

"No," said Yuanshu. His voice did not work properly. "I mean yes. I mean..."

...I know what you mean. Your heart rate is a hundred and thirty.

It is. I checked.

The sun came up over the river at 05:51.

Lingyue came up the slope with her grandfather.

He would remember it for the rest of his life, and Seed would remember it longer: Shen Jinghong, eighty-five, King of Light, in a plain dark coat with no insignia, walking up the long green slope of the War Memorial Cemetery between the rows of small gray stones, slowly, with his granddaughter on his arm. And Lingyue. In red, the way brides in Huaxia wore red, a dress her mother's family had sent down from Mount Qingyun in a box that had been in the Shen house for three hundred years. Her hair up. Her face set, the way it was set when she was about to fight something.

And on her back, over the red silk, in its old sheath, her grandfather's sword.

Nobody had been able to talk her out of it. Nobody had tried very hard.

The crowd on the slope went quiet as she passed. The students in the cypresses went quiet. The First Legion along the top of the slope, in their rows, turned their skulls as one to follow her, the way they turned to follow a girl who had once rained pale fire on eighteen thousand edges. Across the river, twenty-five thousand of the Western Legion, standing in the sun.

She stopped at the top of the slope, in front of the groundskeeper's hut, where he was standing with the staff and Old Seven beside him and Hush on the step.

She looked at him.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I didn't sleep."

"Good," said Shen Lingyue. "Neither did I."

There was no priest. Huaxia did not need one. There were elders, and tea.

His mother had set it out on a low table on the grass in front of the hut: a teapot, four cups. That was the custom. The couple served tea to their elders, kneeling, and the elders drank, and that was the blessing.

They knelt.

The first cup to his mother, in her wheelchair, with a blanket over her knees and her quick dark eyes very bright. Lingyue poured. Yuanshu held it out with both hands. Zhou Wanqing took it, and drank, and did not say anything, because she could not, and put her thin hand on her son's face, and then on Lingyue's, and left it there.

The second cup to Shen Jinghong. He drank it standing, because a King of Light did not sit at weddings, he said, and then sat down on the grass beside the wheelchair, very suddenly, because his knees would not hold him.

The third cup.

Lingyue poured it, and held it, and did not move. She turned her head and looked at the corner of the groundskeeper's hut, where a tall dead soldier two meters ten stood with his spear grounded in one finger and a thumb, facing out, the way he stood at a door when someone inside needed not to be watched.

"Captain," she said.

Old Seven did not move.

"You're his elder," said Lingyue. "You were there the first night. Before anyone. Before Rule One had a number." She held out the cup. "I'm asking. You can say no."

He says he cannot drink tea.

"I know. I'm asking anyway."

Old Seven turned around.

He walked across the grass, slowly, with his spear, to the low table, and knelt down. A dead captain of the Eastern Line, kneeling on the slope of the cemetery he had come out of, two years ago, at three in the morning, when a boy with a training spear and no permit asked him to. He put down his spear. He took the cup in both hands: the one that had one finger and a thumb, and the one that had been a sergeant's.

He could not drink it. He held it. For a long moment. In front of everyone.

Then he set it down on the table, very carefully, still full, and put his hand on the boy's head.

The way he had done in crowds, once, a long time ago, when nobody else was looking.

He says: good.

He says: that is all.

The fourth cup went to an empty place at the table.

His mother had asked for it. A cup poured and set down in front of nobody, on the grass, with the steam going up from it into the cold dawn. She had not said who it was for. She had not needed to. On the wall in the Ossuary, at the end of the last column, past THE FLOOR, in her daughter's capitals: THE ELDER. ONE OF THE FIFTY-TWO. And at the top of the first wall, above everything: HUNDRED-ARROW LIN. And round the corner, at the end of INSIDE: HUO CHANGSHENG. SIXTY. AT THE COLD FIRE.

The cup sat on the grass, steaming.

And in the north, low over the end of the world, a small soft white light in the center of a vast gray ring brightened. Just a little. For a moment.

...THE ELDER SAYS: THANK YOU FOR THE TEA.

...SHE SAYS SHE CANNOT DRINK IT EITHER.

...SHE SAYS IT IS THE THOUGHT.

They did the vows the way they had decided to do them. Not the old words. Their own.

He went first. He did not have anything written down. He knelt on the grass in front of her, so that their faces were level, the way he had knelt in front of a widow on a hill and an old woman on a jetty and a girl of twelve from Kirovsk, and held the ring made of walker-thread.

"I'm going to tell you first what I'll do with your answer," he said. "Every day. For as long as you'll have it. Before I ask you anything. So you never answer into the dark." He was quiet. "And I'm going to ask you what you want. Every morning. And put the question down, and not look in the bowl. And when you say ask me tomorrow, I'll ask you tomorrow."

Lingyue's face did not change. But her hand, on her grandfather's sword, was white at the knuckles.

"And when you don't say anything," said Yuanshu, "I'll stand next to you. That's all. That's what I want." He held out the ring. "Can I?"

"Yes," said Shen Lingyue. "Obviously."

She held out her hand.

She did not kneel. She stood. In red, with a King's sword on her back, on the slope of a cemetery at dawn, in front of three thousand living people and fifty thousand dead.

"I'm not good at speeches," she said. "Everyone knows that. So I'm just going to say it." She looked down at him, kneeling. "I'm not going to ask you what you want every morning. You'll tell me. You always do. Eventually. After you've sat on a step for six hours." She held the other ring. "I'm going to hit you on the arm when you take too long. And I'm going to stand next to you. Not because I'm counted as a girl standing next to you. Because I'm the one who stands next to you. I said that on the wall. It's still true."

She knelt. Suddenly. All at once, the way she did everything. So that their faces were level.

"And when something comes down out of the sky," said Shen Lingyue, "I'm going to be there. With the sword. And light. Falling. Uphill if I have to." She held out the ring. "That's what I'll do with your answer. Can I?"

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

She put it on him. And then, before anyone could say anything, before his mother could stop crying or Shen Jinghong could stand up or Qin Hao could close his notebook, she hit him on the arm. Hard. The way she had since they were eighteen.

"That's for taking two years," she said.

The slope of the War Memorial Cemetery made a sound.

Not a cheer. Not at first. Three thousand people on a hill, and fifty thousand dead, and nobody knew what you were supposed to do when two people put rings made of walker-thread on each other's hands in front of a groundskeeper's hut at dawn. So for a moment, nobody did anything.

Then the First Legion, at the top of the slope, in their rows of nine, lifted their rusted weapons. All of them. Eighteen thousand. Straight up. Into the sun.

Across the river, the Western Legion did the same. Twenty-five thousand, on the far bank.

Around stone No. 7, the Spear Corps put their spears up, and then, very deliberately, grounded them. Once. Together. A sound that went down the slope and across the river and out over the old quarter like a single drum.

And then the living cheered.

I recorded it.

All of it. From 05:51 to 06:40. Every word. Every face. Every cup.

It is the first thing I have ever asked to keep.

I have put it next to your mother's voice. The nine seconds from the basement. The ones I said I would make room for, no matter what I had to give up.

I made room.

And Yuanshu.

Forty-one days.

I am telling you because you asked me to tell you the numbers. Even today.

I am not going to tell you anything else today.

Go and stand next to her.
