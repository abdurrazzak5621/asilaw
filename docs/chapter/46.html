Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 46Sunday Dinner

czverse9 min read

The Academy dining hall went silent when the skeleton walked in.

It was a long, high, noisy room with rows of wooden tables and steamed-up windows and the smell of rice and frying garlic, and three hundred students in dark training uniforms had been shouting at each other across it in the way that three hundred eighteen-year-olds always shout at each other across a dining hall on a Sunday night.

Then the double doors at the end swung open, and Old Seven ducked under the lintel, two meters and ten centimeters of white bone with a gray flame burning in its chest, and straightened up, and looked around the room with the empty holes of its eyes.

Three hundred pairs of chopsticks stopped in midair.

Somewhere near the back, a bowl of soup hit the floor.

Old Seven considered the room for a moment. Then he raised his right hand in a crisp, perfect, thirty-year-old military salute, held it for one second, and lowered it.

Behind him, Yuanshu came in, in a clean white shirt with no coal dust on it, and said, "Sorry. He wanted to come."

Shen Lingyue was standing on a bench at a table near the windows, waving one arm over her head, with an expression that said she had known this would happen and was enjoying it enormously.

He walked down the long aisle between the tables with Old Seven behind him. Every head turned to follow them, slowly, like sunflowers. A boy at the end of one table quietly moved his tray along the bench to make room for a skeleton to pass, and then seemed to realize what he had done, and went pale.

At Lingyue's table there were eight people, and one empty chair, and one very large, very sturdy chair clearly borrowed from the staff room, with a hand-lettered sign on the back that said FOR MR. SEVEN.

Old Seven looked at the sign for a long moment.

Then he sat in it. Carefully. The chair creaked.

He laid his spear across his knees and folded his finger-bones on top of it, exactly the way an old soldier sits at a wedding.

"Everyone," said Shen Lingyue, sitting down. "This is Zhou Yuanshu. He's boring and he thinks too loudly. Yuanshu. Everyone."

Everyone turned out to be a lot of people.

There was Instructor Bai, at the head of the table: a lean woman of about fifty with cropped iron-gray hair and a burn scar down one side of her neck, who taught Rift Tactics and looked at Yuanshu the way a carpenter looks at a new piece of wood, checking the grain.

There was Principal Yue Changfeng, who had not been invited, and had come anyway, and was sitting at the end of the bench in a tracksuit with his saber propped against the wall behind him, eating dumplings off three different plates and complaining about his back.

And there were the students. Lingyue's class. Five of them, crowded along the far bench, all staring at him.

"Ask your stupid questions," said Lingyue. "He promised."

There was a short pause.

Then all five of them started talking at once.

"Do skeletons get cold?"

"Can you raise my goldfish? He died in the second year. His name was General."

"Is it true you made a fireman sing?"

"Does Seed have a favorite color?"

"What's it like? Talking to the dead? Is it scary? Is it sad? Do they ever tell you where they buried money?"

Yuanshu looked at Lingyue for help. She was eating a pork rib with great concentration and did not look up.

Answer them.

No. Yes. No. Your goldfish, if he was a good goldfish. Green, I think. It was the color of the light through the leaves outside your mother's window, in the oldest memory I have.

Sometimes. Usually. Twice. Both times it was already spent.

He answered them. All of them. He told them about the goldfish problem, which was that fish don't really do yes, and about Sergeant Wu's joke, which made one of the girls laugh so hard she choked on her tea, and about the old woman at the Ossuary who had come in to register and asked whether she could come back as a dog, which was not a thing he could do, but which he had very much wanted to.

The five of them listened with their elbows on the table and their food going cold.

So did the next table. And the one after that. By the time he had finished explaining why the dead are so polite, half the dining hall had turned their chairs around.

Two of the five were twins.

They were small and thin and identical, with the same sharp narrow faces and the same messy black hair, and they were sitting so close together on the bench that their elbows touched. One of them wore a silver pin shaped like a compass on his collar. The other wore one shaped like an hourglass.

"Mo Xing," said the compass one. "Space Law."

"Mo Shi," said the hourglass one. "Time Law. And you're about to drop that."

Yuanshu's elbow knocked the soy sauce bottle off the edge of the table.

It did not hit the floor. A small fold appeared in the air beside the table, a ripple, like heat over a road, and the bottle fell into it and came out of a second ripple directly above Mo Xing's plate and landed upright beside his rice.

Mo Xing poured some soy sauce on his rice and slid the bottle back across the table without looking up.

"He can see about three seconds ahead," Mo Xing explained. "On a good day. I can fold about a meter of space. On a good day." He shrugged. "Together we're very good at catching things."

"And very annoying," said Lingyue.

"You're about to say that," said Mo Shi.

"I did say that."

"Two seconds ago. I was being polite."

The fifth student was a girl with round glasses and a long plait over one shoulder, who had not asked a single question and had been watching his left hand the whole time.

"You've burned your finger," she said quietly.

He looked down. There was a small red blister on the side of his forefinger, from the teapot in the Ossuary vault that morning. He hadn't noticed.

"May I?" she said.

He held out his hand. She touched the blister with one fingertip, very lightly, and a faint warm green light went into the skin, like sunlight through a leaf, and the blister was gone.

"Bai Lu," she said. "Healing Law." She hesitated. "Only small things. So far."

"Bai?" He looked up the table.

"No relation," said Instructor Bai, without looking up from her fish.

"Some relation," said Bai Lu.

"She's my niece," said Instructor Bai to the fish. "We don't talk about it at school."

It was after the dumplings and before the oranges that it happened.

Principal Yue put down his chopsticks. He leaned back against the wall, and folded his hands on his stomach, and said, very casually, to nobody in particular:

"The four of them came to my office on Thursday."

The table went quiet.

"Lingyue. The Mo boys. Bai Lu." Principal Yue looked at the ceiling. "With forms. Signed. Requesting leave from the Academy for an expedition into the Northern Wastes, at a date to be confirmed, under the command of King Su Mingli." He paused. "Bai Lu's form had been signed by her aunt. In ink. Twice. Because the first time, her aunt's hand was shaking too much."

Instructor Bai did not look up from her fish. Her ears had gone faintly red.

Yuanshu put down his own chopsticks.

"No," he said.

"Too late," said Mo Shi.

"You didn't let me finish."

"You were going to say it's too dangerous, and we're only eighteen, and you can't protect us." Mo Shi tapped his hourglass pin. "I saw it about four seconds ago. You were going to say it really well. There was a pause in the middle for effect."

"He's eighteen too," said Mo Xing, to the table at large.

"He's Elite," said Bai Lu quietly.

"So am I," said Lingyue. "In case anyone forgot." She looked at Yuanshu across the table, and her voice was perfectly level. "Su Mingli said a real expedition. People who can fold space and see the next three seconds and heal you when you do something stupid. You're going to do something stupid. Probably more than once. You need a healer." She picked up her chopsticks again. "Principal Yue approved the forms."

"For the paperwork," said Principal Yue to the ceiling. "Purely for the paperwork. I approve everything. My back can't take arguing."

I have looked at their law signatures.

Space. Time. Healing. Light.

They are very young. They are also very rare.

And they have all already decided. I can hear it.

He looked at the four of them. At Mo Xing and Mo Shi with their elbows touching. At Bai Lu, looking at her plate. At Lingyue, eating a pork rib as if nothing whatever had been said.

"I'll ask Su Mingli," he said at last.

"She already said yes," said Lingyue. "On Friday."

The oranges came. Somebody's phone came out. And one of the girls at the next table, the one who'd choked on her tea, leaned over the back of her chair and asked the last question of the night.

"What did you choose?" she said. "In the Ledger?"

The table went quiet again.

Yuanshu opened his mouth. Then closed it.

You have never registered.

One million and thirty-one thousand people. Not you.

"I haven't," he said slowly. "I... haven't."

Lingyue looked at him for a long second. Then she reached across the table, took his phone out of his shirt pocket, opened the Ossuary page, and pushed it back into his hand.

"Now," she said.

The whole table was watching. The next table too. Old Seven had turned his skull very slightly toward him.

He looked at the four choices on the small bright screen.

YES. ASK MY FAMILY. ONLY TO SPEAK. QUIET.

He thought about it for a long time. Long enough that Principal Yue stopped eating. Then he pressed ASK MY FAMILY, and a box came up asking Who decides?, and he typed Zhou Xiaoyu. She'll know.

He turned the screen around so they could see it.

Bai Lu smiled at her plate. One of the Mo twins nodded. Instructor Bai looked at him with a carpenter's look again, and this time it seemed she'd found the grain good.

"What did you choose?" he said to Lingyue.

"Draw," she said. "Remember? Nobody has to tell anybody anything." She ate a segment of orange. "Ask me after the north."

They were stacking the plates when the double doors at the end of the hall swung open again.

Qin Hao stood there.

He was in full Western War Zone uniform, dark green, with the cap under his arm and his boots polished like black glass, and he had clearly driven straight here from somewhere far away, because there was dust on his shoulders and his face was gray with tiredness. He looked down the long hall, past three hundred turned chairs, and found Yuanshu, and came straight down the aisle without looking left or right.

He stopped at the table. He looked at Old Seven in his chair with the sign on the back. He looked at the empty orange peels.

Then he saluted. Not Yuanshu. Old Seven.

Old Seven returned it.

"My grandfather," said Qin Hao, "would like to see you." His voice was hoarse. "Tomorrow. At dawn. At the military cemetery at the Western Pass." He swallowed. "He says to tell you he's been reading the Ledger. Every name. For six weeks. And he has a proposal."

"What kind of proposal?"

Qin Hao looked at him with his tired, serious, dust-gray face.

"He says," said Qin Hao, "that forty thousand of his soldiers have said yes."
