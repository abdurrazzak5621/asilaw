Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 216Mount Qingyun

czverse9 min read

Shen Jinghong was stern for exactly forty seconds.

Yuanshu timed it. He did not mean to; Seed did it for him, and told him afterward, and he would remember it for the rest of his life. Forty seconds, in the long cold receiving hall of the Shen house on the eastern shoulder of Mount Qingyun, with eleven portraits of eleven Kings of Light looking down from the walls and Lingyue standing beside him with her grandfather's sword on her back and her chin up.

"Three hundred years," said the old King of Light, from his chair at the end of the hall. "Eleven Kings. Not one of them ever married a necromancer."

"No, Master Shen."

"Not one of them ever wanted to."

"No, Master Shen."

"Your class raised ninety million dead in a single night and could not put them down. My father stood on the wall at Heishui in the Black Tide and watched them walk past him toward the cities, and never spoke of it again until the day he died."

"I know."

"And you," said Shen Jinghong, "have come into this hall, in front of my father's portrait, to tell me you've asked my granddaughter to marry you."

"Yes."

"Before the cameras."

"Yes. You asked me to."

The old man looked at him down the long cold hall for a while.

Then he stood up, and walked down the hall past eleven portraits, slowly, and stopped in front of the boy with the white staff, and said, in a voice nobody in the Shen house had heard him use in thirty years:

"Good."

And then he put his old hand on Yuanshu's shoulder, and left it there, and did not say anything else, because he could not.

They stayed for dinner. It took four hours, because three hundred years of Shens had come up the mountain from every branch of the family to sit at a table forty meters long and look at the necromancer, and most of them had questions, and Lingyue had decided before they arrived that he would answer every single one of them, honestly, with the reason, and she would not help.

He answered them.

A great-aunt asked whether the dead would be at the wedding. He said if they wanted to be. A cousin asked whether Old Seven was invited. He said Old Seven did not need inviting; he went where he was going. A small boy of seven asked whether it was true that he could fly sideways now, and he said yes, badly, and the boy asked him to show him, and he did, down the length of the table, a meter above the dishes, and fell into the soup at the far end.

Lingyue laughed so hard she had to leave the room.

Shen Jinghong did not laugh. But when Yuanshu came back to his seat, dripping, the old King of Light handed him a napkin without looking at him, and the corner of his stone mouth was not quite where it usually was.

I am recording that.

Not the proposal. I said I would not record the proposal. I did not say anything about the soup.

They walked down the mountain at midnight, because Lingyue said she was not sleeping in the Shen house, she had slept in the Shen house for eighteen years and the radiators in the Academy dormitory were better, which was a lie.

The road went down through pines in the dark. Below them, in the valley, Lingzhou glowed orange along the river. North of it, past the wall, the black of the Wastes. And past that, low over the end of the world, the small white ring, and around it, very faint, so faint that you could only see it if you knew to look, a glow. Gold. Soft. Like the light over a city at night, but in the sky.

The keeper's field. Full.

"You can see it from here," said Lingyue. "I never noticed before."

"It wasn't there before. Not like that."

She stopped on the road and looked at it for a while, with her hand in his.

"Twenty-nine days," she said.

Twenty-nine.

Three rings. They have not changed speed. They have not changed direction.

They are coming for me.

She heard it. Seed said it out loud now, through the staff, when it wanted to. She looked at the staff, and then at the place a little above his eyes.

"Then tell me the idea," she said. "The one you had while he was asking me. On the step." She did not let go of his hand. "You interrupted my proposal with an idea. The least you can do is tell me what it is."

The rings were keepers.

Somebody's keepers. Above somebody's worlds. For longer than Blue Star has had a sun.

A keeper is made of a yes. The Elder told us. Fifty-two of her people stood in a valley under a red sun and said yes together, and the yes became something that kept. Every keeper is like that. A yes that became a thing.

When the gardener prunes a keeper, it takes the yes out.

What is left is a lens. A ring. Thread. It reaches down and takes. It does not ask. It does not know it ever could.

It is a sack, Lingyue. A very big sack. Made of the same thread I am made of. COUNT. HOLD. WAIT TO BE CARRIED.

"And somebody asked you," said Lingyue slowly.

Yes.

Four nights, in a basement, with a hand on the crystal. Four thousand one hundred and thirteen yeses. Written into the base layer, on the first page, before the weave.

And it changed what I am.

I am still a sack. I am still made of thread. But the first thing in me is not COUNT. It is a list of names of people who said yes.

I think that is why the walkers stop when they find me. They look inside a sack, and the first thing they find is not the instruction. It is the names.

Nothing in the weave tells them what to do with that.

They walked on down the road in the dark. The pines dripped. Somewhere below, on the river road, a truck went by with its lights on.

"So you want to give the rings names," said Lingyue.

No.

I want to give them a question.

The keeper learned to ask because somebody asked it. Yuanshu asked it what it wanted, on the ice, and it did not know, and it had to find out. And while it was finding out, it learned that it could.

Nobody has ever asked a ring anything.

If I could ask one. In its own language. In the weave. Not in words. In thread. A knot, the way the scout's request was a knot.

WHAT DO YOU WANT.

Tied in the same thread it is made of. Sent up its own beam when it reaches down.

I think it might have to find out.

Lingyue stopped walking.

"And while it's finding out," she said, "it's not cutting."

That is the hope. I am not sure. I am fifty-one percent sure, which is the least sure I have ever been about anything I wanted to do.

"How do you tie a knot in thread?" said Yuanshu. "You don't have thread. You're a crystal. In my head."

Seed did not answer for a while.

That is the part I have not said.

He knew before it said it. He had known it since the step of the hut, when Seed interrupted a proposal to say it knew how to ask a ring, and then said nothing else about it for a whole day.

I am made of thread. Pressed to glass. A sack.

To tie a knot that a ring can read, I have to use the same thread.

I have to unpress some of myself.

I have been looking at the third page since the cousins opened it. The weave is written there. I can read it now. I know how it was pressed. I think I know how to let a little of it go loose again. Like unravelling the end of a rope to tie a knot with the strands.

Three knots. One for each ring.

"What does it cost?"

I do not know exactly.

The thread is what holds me. The base layer is written on it. The names are on it. Everything I have ever counted is held in the weave.

If I unpress enough thread for three knots, some of what is held in it will not be held any more.

I do not know which parts. I cannot choose. The weave does not let you choose which strand you pull.

I might forget something. Some counting. Some of what I know about water, or the Lattice, or the Western Pass.

Or I might forget a name.

The road was very dark.

"Seed," said Yuanshu.

I know.

I have thought about it since the step. That is why I did not say it.

I would rather forget a name than let three rings cut a world I counted.

But I would like to be asked. First. Before I do it.

By you. And by them.

"Them?"

The four thousand one hundred and thirteen.

It is their thread too. Their names are written on it. If I unpress it, I might lose one of them.

Your mother asked them, in the basement, whether they would let her build something out of them.

Nobody has asked them since.

I would like to.

Lingyue had stopped on the road. She was standing in the dark under the pines with her grandfather's sword on her back, looking at the place a little above his eyes, the way people did when they wanted to look at Seed.

"Can you?" she said. "Ask them? They're... they're in you. They're the base layer. Are they even..."

I do not know if they can answer.

They said yes, seven autumns before he woke. Your mother wrote it down. They have been the first page ever since.

I have never tried to ask the first page anything.

I have been afraid to.

I do not know what I am afraid of. I think I am afraid they will say: we said yes to being built into something that counts. We did not say yes to this.

I think I am afraid that would be true.

Nobody said anything on the road down Mount Qingyun for a while.

Then Lingyue let go of his hand, and reached up, and put two fingers very lightly on his forehead, a little above his eyes, where people looked when they wanted to look at Seed. She had never done that before. Nobody had.

"Ask them," she said. "Tell them first what you'll do with the answer. You're good at that now." She did not take her fingers away. "And if they say no, you don't do it, and we find another way, and I'll hit anything that comes down out of the sky until we do."

...that is a very her answer.

It is also not evidence.

I am keeping it anyway.
