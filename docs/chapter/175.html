Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 175The Night Bell

czverse8 min read

Ren played it to the monks at dusk, kneeling on the step under the six hundred and twelfth gate with a satellite phone held against the worn stone and her other hand flat beside it.

Yuanshu listened from Lingzhou, two thousand kilometers away, on the vault steps of the Ossuary with the staff across his knees and the phone on speaker beside him. Kwan was there. Xiaoyu, who had refused to go upstairs. Fat Tan. Old Seven by the bronze door. On the line, behind Ren's quiet breathing, he could hear the cedars dripping, and the single falling bird-note, again and again, and very faintly, somewhere far down the mountain, the Quiet saying names on the terraces.

I am going to play it to them through the phone. Through Ren. She will carry it down.

It is eleven minutes long. It repeats every four hundred and twelve seconds. I have made it louder and taken out what I think is noise. I may have taken out things that are not noise. I will tell them that, first.

It told them. Through the phone. Through Ren. In the old Yamato it had learned a year and a half ago from seven Sleepers in a hall, translating as fast as it came. This is a sound from very far away. Past the edge of what the keeper can see. I have cleaned it. I may have cleaned it wrong. I am going to play it to you, and I would like to know if you have heard it before. You do not have to answer.

Then it played it.

Yuanshu had heard it a hundred times by then. Seed had played it to him in the night, and in the morning, and while he ate, until he could have hummed it. It did not sound like anything. A long low tone. A silence. A cluster of shorter tones, rising and falling, like someone saying a word with too many syllables very slowly. Another silence, much longer. And then the long low tone again.

Over and over. Every four hundred and twelve seconds.

On the step in Yamato, Ren held the phone against the stone and did not move.

For a while, nothing.

Then she drew in a sharp breath, all at once, the way you breathe in when someone you thought was asleep says your name.

"They're getting up," she said. Very quietly. "All of them. Under the path. All three hundred and seventy-two. Up and down the mountain." Her voice was not steady. "Mister Zhou. They're standing up in the dark under the gates."

"That is our bell."

The voice came up through Ren, through the stone, out of the satellite phone into the old banking hall in Lingzhou. Not one voice. Many. An old dry chorus, the way the drowned had answered together off Kesse, the way the ferry's wedding party had answered in one slow chord up the pipe. Seed translated it as fast as it came, its white letters running ahead of itself.

"The night bell. Of the village by the sea. Below the terraces. Where the rice was."

"It rang at dusk. One stroke. A long wait. Then the name of the village, on the small bells, so the fishermen out on the water would know which shore was theirs in the dark. Then a longer wait. Then one stroke again."

"Over and over. Until the last boat was in."

"It rang the evening the ring came. We heard it from the hall. It was ringing when the threads came down. It rang all through the cutting, because the bell-ringer was quiet, and the thread did not find him, and he did not know what else to do."

"He rang it until morning."

Nobody in the banking hall said anything.

Xiaoyu had both hands over her mouth.

"The long tone is the great bell. The silence is the wait. The short tones are the small bells, saying the name."

"The name is Shiomi. The village was called Shiomi."

"It means: the place where you can see the tide."

"It's gone," said Ren. "Shiomi. There's no Shiomi. There hasn't been for a thousand years. The village below the terraces is called something else. The old name's only on the bark scrolls." Her voice was very careful. "Mister Zhou. The monks say it's the bell. Our bell. From the night of the cutting. Twelve hundred years ago."

"Then who's ringing it?" said Kwan.

Nobody answered her.

...I have a guess.

I am going to say it as a guess. I would like everyone to remember that it is a guess.

The cutting took three in ten of the people of these islands. Their minds went up the threads. Into the field. Into the keeper's columns.

The keeper said the walkers came sometimes, once in a hundred thousand years, and took columns. The oldest. The brightest. It thought they were weather.

Some of the columns from the cutting before ours. Twelve hundred years ago. Were taken.

Somebody in one of those columns remembered the bell.

"And they're ringing it," said Yuanshu slowly. "From the deep water."

From past the end of the arm. Yes. I think so.

I do not know how. I do not know if they are minds, or people, or something that grew out of minds. I do not know if they are free, or kept, or something in between. I do not know if they know we can hear.

But someone out there remembers a bell from a village that has not existed for a thousand years, and has been ringing it, very patiently, over and over, for so long that it does not know how to stop.

So that the fishermen out on the water would know which shore was theirs in the dark.

On the step under the six hundred and twelfth gate, the monks were still talking.

Seed had stopped translating everything. It gave him pieces. An old voice asking whether the bell-ringer's family had been taken; Ren did not know. A nun saying that her sister had been in Shiomi that evening, carrying rice down to the boats. A great many voices at once, overlapping, the way a crowd talks when it has heard something it cannot hold. And then, under all of it, one voice alone, very old, the one under the six hundred and twelfth gate, who had liked Ren's crumbs.

"Granddaughter."

"I'm here," said Ren.

"Twelve hundred years ago, we sat down in the hall so that the world would remember to be afraid. So that the next time the ring came, someone would know."

"We thought we were the only ones who remembered."

"We were not."

"Somebody else has been keeping watch too. All this time. Out there. Ringing our bell so that we would know which shore was theirs."

Ren did not say anything. On the line, Yuanshu heard her breathing change.

"Is anyone going to answer them?"

Concordia met the next day.

Not in the crater. On screens. Five delegations and Chair Adaeze Okafor, and Ren on a sixth screen from the step of the broken hall in Yamato with her hand still flat on the wood, because she would not leave the monks while they were awake. Yuanshu in the Ossuary with Kwan and the recording. Seed played it to the Council. Kwan explained the arm. Ren translated the monks.

When it was done, the Council was quiet for a long time.

"Mister Zhou," said Okafor finally. "You told me in Yamato that the thing above the sky learned to ask by watching us. That it follows the words and not the reason." She folded her hands on the black glass. "If we answer this bell, who else hears?"

"I don't know."

"Mister Secretary?"

Elias Brandt took his glasses off. "The Atlantic Federation would like to answer," he said. "Immediately. And loudly. Twelve hundred years. If those are human beings out there, they've been calling for twelve hundred years. We owe them a reply." He put the glasses back on. "The Atlantic Federation would also like it noted that it has very recently learned what shouting into the sky can bring down on a world."

"Marshal?"

Orlov was silent for a moment on his screen, in his greatcoat, in the cold light of the Northern Union.

"The last time something shouted from this world," he said, "it was a dead scout with a seed for a heart. It said: field emptied, keeper turned. It called a thing to come and look. The thing came, and looked, and took eleven hundred people's wants up a lens." He folded his gloved hands. "I would like very much to answer a bell. I would like more to know who is listening before I do."

"Mister Zhou."

He looked at the screen. At the five delegations, and the Chair, and Ren on her step in the dark.

"The Western Pass taught me something," he said. "A voice that shouts through every window on Blue Star isn't one person's choice. It's everyone's. If we answer, eight billion people are answering. Most of them don't know there's a bell." He was quiet. "And Article Eleven says that whoever asks has to say first what they'll do with the answer. If we ring back, we're asking them something. Are you there? Who are you? And we don't know yet what we'd do with what they said."

"So we don't answer," said Brandt.

"We don't shout," said Yuanshu. "Not yet. We find out who's listening. We find out what we'd do. And then we ask everyone on Blue Star whether they want to ring a bell into the deep water, and we tell them what might hear it." He looked at Ren. "And in the meantime, someone listens. Every night. And writes it down. So that whoever's ringing it isn't ringing into nothing, even if they can't hear us yet."

Okafor looked at him for a while across the black glass.

"Who listens?"

On the sixth screen, Shirasagi Ren lifted her head.

"The monks," she said. "They've asked. All three hundred and seventy-two. They say they've been keeping watch for twelve hundred years and they know how to listen to a bell in the dark." She was quiet for a moment. "They say it's their village. They say it's their turn."

That night, in the Ossuary, Xiaoyu climbed the stepladder and started a new column.

At the very end of the wall, past INSIDE, past the bare plaster, at the corner where the wall met the next wall. She wrote a heading in her careful capitals, and stood back, and looked at it, and did not write anything under it yet.

SHIOMI.

Then, smaller:

Someone is ringing. Someone is listening.

Four hundred and twelve seconds.

I am going to listen too. Every night. With the monks.

I would like to be able to tell whoever is ringing it that the bell-ringer's village has a new name now, and a road, and a school.

And that there is a small loud girl in Lingzhou writing it on a wall.
