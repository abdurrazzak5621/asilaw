Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 83The Elder

czverse10 min read

The keeper asked first.

That was the strangest part. Afterward, it was the part everyone remembered.

It happened at three in the morning, in the old banking hall of the Ossuary, under the one lamp that stayed lit over the board. Yuanshu was sitting at a desk in the corner with the white staff across his knees, writing names. Jiang Lan was dictating. Ninety million of them, one at a time, in her dry brisk voice, into a ledger that the Ossuary had started the week they came home and would not finish, Fat Tan estimated, for about four hundred years.

"Zhou Fenglan. Seamstress. Raised in the eastern hills. Said nothing. Went quiet in the second year." A pause. "Next. Wu Datong. Blacksmith. Raised in..."

Yuanshu.

He looked up.

There was a thread in the hall.

One. Gold. Fine as a hair. It came down from the high arched ceiling, through the stone, through the roof, from somewhere very far to the north, and hung in the still air above the marble floor in front of the board. Swaying very slightly. Glowing.

It did not come any lower. It did not touch anyone. It did not go toward him, or the staff, or the sleeping volunteer on the bench by the bronze door.

It just hung there.

And then, very faintly, it rang. One clear note, like a fingertip drawn around the rim of a glass.

It is asking.

Yuanshu. It is asking if it may come in.

He stood up.

Iron Wall Zhao had already risen from his stool by the bronze door, his great empty shield turning slowly toward the thread. Old Seven was at Yuanshu's shoulder. Hundred-Arrow Lin had appeared in the shadows of the high ceiling, a drift of ash with an iron arrowhead for a heart.

"Who is it?" said Yuanshu. Out loud. To the thread.

The note rang again. Softer. And Seed, very carefully, translated.

One of the fifty-two. From the white column.

She would like to speak with you. And with the one who came home.

She will not come in unless you say yes.

He looked at the thread.

"Wake my mother," he said to nobody in particular. Then, to the thread: "Yes. Please. Come in."

It took forty minutes for Wanqing to come. Xiaoyu brought her, in a wheelchair, in a coat over her pajamas, scowling so hard at the thread that it seemed to sway away from her a little. Lu came behind them, in a coat over a suit he had obviously slept in, with Doctor Kwan, who had a camera and three different instruments and looked as if Christmas had come early. Sister Ning came last, slowly, with her candle.

Su Mingli did not come through the door. She was simply there, standing at the edge of the lamplight with her arms folded, as if she had been there all along.

They stood in a loose ring around the gold thread on the marble floor.

"All right," said Yuanshu, to the thread.

And the thread came down.

It touched the marble, very gently, the way a raindrop touches the surface of a pond. And light spread out from the point where it touched. Soft. White. Like the white column in the field. Rising. Gathering. Taking a shape.

Tall. Much taller than a person. Slender. With long pale hands and a long pale face and eyes like silver threads, like moonlight on water.

It was not solid. You could see the board through it, faintly. The four columns of numbers, and the new fifth one, CAME HOME. It flickered at the edges, the way a candle flame flickers in a draft.

But it was there.

It looked around the old banking hall. At the high arched ceiling. At the marble floor, worn smooth by a hundred years of feet. At the board, with its numbers. At the steel drawers in the open vault door, full of paper. At the little ring of small warm living people, and the three dead soldiers, and the old woman with her candle.

Then it looked at Wanqing in her wheelchair.

And it bowed. Deeply. Its long pale body folding at the middle, its silver eyes lowering, its long hands pressed together. The way the makers had bowed in the column.

The voice was the glass-rim note. But slower now. Softer. Shaped, Yuanshu understood, very carefully, by something that had spent the last forty-six days learning how to make sounds that small warm creatures could hear without being frightened.

Seed translated. Out loud this time, through the air, in its quiet white voice, so that everyone could hear.

"Thank you for letting me in."

"I am... there is no word in your language. The keeper calls me Elder. It is a word for the one who stood at the front of the ring. In the valley. Under the red sun."

"I was the first to say yes. Six hundred and forty million years ago."

"I was the first to say quiet, in the field, when he asked."

"And then I changed my mind."

It looked at Yuanshu.

"You said: once is not forever. I listened."

"I am one of the fifty-two who stayed."

"Why did you come?" said Wanqing.

Her voice was thin, but very steady. She sat in her wheelchair with her hands folded on the blanket and looked straight up at the tall pale shape, the way she would have looked at a colleague across a lab bench.

The Elder looked down at her for a long moment.

"Because the keeper is afraid to."

"It has never visited anyone. It does not know how. It does not know what to say."

"And because you saw them. In the field. When it was frightened. When it was thinking about them very loudly."

"The four," said Wanqing.

"The four."

The light of the tall pale shape dimmed, slightly. As if a cloud had passed over a moon.

"We call them... there is no word. The keeper calls them Hungers."

It showed them.

Not the way the monk had shown the harvest, overwhelming, a door into a memory. Gently. A picture, hanging in the air above the marble floor of the banking hall, faint and silver, like a reflection in a dark window.

A sea of darkness. Vast. Endless. Scattered with tiny points of light, very far apart.

"The Star Sea. What you would call space. The dark between the fields."

And in the dark, very far away, a shape.

A ring. Gray. Cloudy. Cracked all over, like the crystal of the cores, like the Monarchs. Enormous. Turning slowly in the dark. And around it, drifting, held in its orbit like moons around a world, other shapes. Small. Gray. Dead.

Worlds.

Husks. Cold and gray and empty, like the shell of a cicada left on a tree. Nothing on them. No light. No cities. No minds. Not even the dead.

"A long time ago, longer than the keeper, there was a people. Not like us. Not like you. They did not have a word for asking."

"They built a great empire in the Sea. Many fields. And to make their empire go, they needed power. More power than any sun."

"They found that a mind burns."

"Very bright. Very hot. A thinking mind, broken open, gives more power than a star."

The picture changed. The gray ring opened, the way the lens had opened over Concordia. And something fell out of it toward one of the small warm points of light. And the light went out.

"So they built Hungers. To go out into the Sea and find fields. Not to keep. Not to listen. Not to learn."

"To burn."

"The people who built them are gone. Their empire is gone. They burned themselves, in the end. There was nothing left to feed the Hungers but their makers."

"But the Hungers are still out there. Still going. Field to field."

"Nobody ever told them they could stop."

The hall was utterly silent.

Doctor Kwan had lowered her camera. Lu had taken off his glasses and was holding them in his hand without cleaning them. Xiaoyu's hand had found her mother's on the blanket and was gripping it.

"The cores," said Wanqing, very quietly. "The cores in the market. Full of minds. Broken. Burning."

"Yes."

"That is their craft. Long ago, a little of it fell into the keeper's field, the way a seed blows on the wind. The Monarchs learned it. The man with the good teeth learned it. Someone on your world learned it, and sold it."

"It is a very old, very terrible craft."

"And the dead?" said Yuanshu. "The keeper couldn't see them. The dead have nothing it wants." He held the staff tighter. "Can the Hungers?"

The Elder looked at him for a long time with its silver eyes.

Then at Old Seven. At Iron Wall Zhao. At the drift of ash in the high shadows.

"Yes."

"The keeper wanted minds. Whole. Thinking. The dead are quiet. It could not see them."

"The Hungers want anything that burns."

"Even an ember."

Old Seven did not move. But his gray flame, in the cage of his ribs, went down to a single bead.

Yuanshu felt it in his chest like cold water.

Every advantage they had ever had. The river of the dead. The wall of nineteen thousand on the ridge. The Third Battalion walking up the stalk unseen. All of it. Gone. To a Hunger, the dead were not invisible.

They were kindling.

"It dropped something," said Yuanshu. "The closest one. Two nights ago. Seed saw it open. Toward the south. Toward the sea."

The Elder's light dimmed further.

"Yes."

"A seed. A small piece of itself. To taste the field. To see if it is worth coming."

"The keeper saw it fall. That is why I came tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

"It fell into the southern sea. Near land. Near many small warm lights."

"Yuanshu. It has been two nights."

Lu's phone rang.

It was very loud in the silent banking hall. Everyone jumped. Lu fumbled it out of his coat pocket and looked at the screen and his face changed.

"Concordia," he said. "The Council. Emergency line." He answered it. Listened. His face went gray. "When?" Listened. "How many?" Listened for a long time.

Then he lowered the phone and looked at Su Mingli.

"A fishing town," he said. "On the northern coast of the Southern Compact. Port Abiri. Three thousand people. It went silent yesterday morning. No radio. No phones. A coast guard boat went in last night." He swallowed. "They're all dead, Mingli. All of them. In their houses. In the streets. On the boats."

"Hollow?"

"No." Lu's voice was barely a whisper. "Not hollow. Not harvested. Dead." He looked at the phone in his hand. "And there's something in the bay. The coast guard said it's growing."

The Elder had turned toward the south. Its long pale face was very still.

"It has begun."

"I am sorry. I am so sorry. We could not see it soon enough. The keeper is not used to looking for things that do not want to be found."

Then it turned back. To Yuanshu. To the staff in his hands. To the small skull with its silver reading glasses.

"The keeper asks something."

"It does not know how to ask well yet. It asked me to ask for it."

"It asks: may it help?"

Yuanshu looked up at the tall pale shape for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "Tell it yes."

"It says: then go south. It cannot see the Hunger's seed. It does not know how. It was never made to look for burning."

"But it can see you. And it can see the threads. And it will be watching."

"If you ask, it will come."

The Elder bowed again. To Wanqing. To Sister Ning. To the three dead soldiers. Last, and lowest, to Yuanshu.

Then the tall pale light folded back into the gold thread, and the thread drew up through the high arched ceiling, and was gone.

Su Mingli was already moving. Out of the lamplight. Toward the bronze door.

"The Southern Compact has an airfield on the coast," she said, without turning around. "Two hours from Port Abiri by road. I'm calling the Council. We fly at dawn." She stopped at the door. "We fly. Not walk. I don't care about the old rules."

"Who?" said Lu.

Su Mingli looked back at the hall. At Yuanshu with the staff. At the three dead soldiers. At Wanqing in her wheelchair, with her daughter's hand gripping hers.

"Everyone who can still stand," she said.
