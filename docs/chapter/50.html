Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 50The Island

czverse9 min read

The ship was called the Long Patience, and it was older than Su Mingli.

It sat at the end of the naval pier in Lingzhou harbor on a gray morning, long and low and painted the dull blue-gray of a winter sea, with rust weeping in long streaks from its rivets and a single tall funnel breathing a thin line of smoke into the low cloud. It had been a hospital ship in the Black Tide. The Huaxia Navy had kept it ever since for one purpose only: carrying delegations to Concordia. It was slow, and it was ugly, and it had never once been late.

The whole Ossuary had come down to see them off.

They stood along the pier in the drizzle, thousands of them, with umbrellas and raincoats and plastic bags over their heads, and every one of them was holding a candle. Unlit. Sister Ning had asked. Not for the ship, she'd said. For the room. So the people in the room know we're quiet here, waiting.

Yuanshu walked up the gangway with Old Seven behind him, and stopped at the top, and turned around.

Ten thousand unlit candles. Ten thousand faces under the rain. Not a sound.

At the very front, at the foot of the gangway, stood Xiaoyu in his old hoodie with the sleeves pulled down over her hands, holding her candle in both fists, scowling at him so fiercely that he knew she was trying not to cry.

He raised a hand.

Ten thousand people raised their candles back. Silently. All together.

The delegation filled the old ship from bow to stern.

Su Mingli had the captain's cabin, because the captain had insisted, and spent the first afternoon standing on the open bridge wing in the wind with her coat buttoned to the throat, looking east, not speaking to anyone.

Director Lu had a cabin full of folders, and Catherine Moore on a satellite phone from the island, and a bottle of stomach medicine he had already started on before the ship left the harbor.

The witnesses had the old hospital ward in the middle of the ship: a long white room with forty iron beds, where Fang Yu the firefighter and the young paramedic from Jinhua Tower and Zhao Mei the nurse and Mrs. Pan from Heishui sat on the beds and drank tea from tin mugs and, after a while, began telling each other who they'd lost.

Gao Yinping sat in the corner with her coal-black hard hat on her knees, listening.

Fat Tan sat on the next bed with his ledgers stacked around him like a wall, writing down the weather.

General Qin Zhenyue had a bunk in the crew quarters, because he had refused anything better, and Qin Hao slept on the floor beside it.

And at the stern, in a small cabin with two Bureau guards at the door and a porthole looking back toward the vanishing coast, Deputy Director Gu Hanshan sat on his narrow bunk in his gray cardigan, with a book open on his knees, reading.

Shen Lingyue found Yuanshu on the afterdeck at sunset, the first night out.

She was in a plain dark coat instead of her Academy uniform, with her sword wrapped in cloth and slung across her back. Su Mingli had made her the delegation's official escort, along with the Mo twins and Bai Lu, which Principal Yue had approved purely for the paperwork. The twins were somewhere below, being sick in perfect unison. Bai Lu was looking after them.

Lingyue leaned on the rail beside him and looked down at the white wake spreading out behind the ship across the darkening water.

"You've been standing here for two hours," she said.

"I'm being quiet."

"You're thinking very loudly about being quiet." She was silent for a moment. "Is it him? At the stern?"

He didn't answer.

"Su Mingli told me," said Lingyue. "About the garden. The number." She kept her eyes on the wake. "She said he's coming because if he weren't on this ship, we wouldn't know where he was. And that this way, the whole delegation's watching him." She paused. "She also said it's the worst decision she's made in twenty years and she'd make it again."

"So would I."

"I know." She straightened up off the rail. "That's why I'm here." She tapped the wrapped sword on her back. "Light burns undead. Light also burns other things. I've been wondering for six weeks what it would do to a thread."

The pulse came at 2:16 in the morning.

Yuanshu was lying awake in his narrow bunk in the dark, listening to the old ship creak and the sea slapping against the hull a few centimeters from his ear, when Seed spoke.

It came early.

He went still.

The last interval was 30 hours and 41 minutes. Exactly on the line. Exactly where the straight line said it should be.

This one was 29 hours and 2 minutes.

An hour and a half early.

It has never jumped like that. Not once. Not since the first night in the Listening Pavilion.

What does it mean?

I do not know.

It means the straight line was wrong. Or it means something changed.

Something that made it hurry.

He lay in the dark and listened to the sea for a long time.

How far are we from Lingzhou?

Four hundred and twelve kilometers.

The pulse did not come down the threads from the north, Yuanshu.

I could feel it. Even out here. Even without the hollows. It went past us.

East.

At four in the morning, the satellite phone in Lu's cabin rang.

Yuanshu heard it through the bulkhead, and was in the corridor in his socks before the second ring. Lu opened his cabin door with the phone already against his ear and his glasses on crooked, and listened, and his face changed, and he held the phone out without a word.

The voice on the other end was a man's, soft and frightened and whispering, and Yuanshu knew it at once. The man in the delivery uniform. The one who had come to the Listening Pavilion every day since the bridge, and made room on his cushion beside the fourth bed on the left.

His name was Ma Jun. Yuanshu had never asked it before. He'd learned it from the shift list.

"I'm sorry," whispered Ma Jun. "I'm sorry, I'm not supposed to call, I'm supposed to be silent, Sister Ning will kill me. But she said, if he ever, she said to call you." His breath was ragged. "He spoke."

Yuanshu's hand tightened on the phone.

"Andrei. Belov. Twenty minutes ago. We were all sitting, it was the deepest part of the night, nobody was thinking anything, and he turned his head. Not three degrees. All the way. To look at me. And his eyes were his. They were his, I swear it, I could see him in them, a scared boy." Ma Jun's whisper broke. "And he said, in his own voice, in Northern, I had to look it up after on my phone, he said..."

A pause. A breath.

"It's coming to the island."

Nobody slept after that.

Su Mingli came down from the bridge and listened to the recording Ma Jun had made on his phone, in the dark, with fourteen candles flickering at the edges of the screen. A boy's voice, dry and cracked and frightened. Three words in Northern. Then nothing. Then the boy's head turning slowly back to the ceiling.

She listened to it four times.

Then she went back up to the bridge without saying anything, and stood on the open wing in the wind, and the old ship went very slightly faster, though nobody had touched the throttle, as if something with the weight of a King had leaned on the sea.

A pulse went east. Early. Past us.

And the boy who is holding the bottom of the thread woke up long enough to warn us.

Yuanshu. I think he did that on purpose.

I think he has been waiting, at the bottom of the well, in the quiet, for something worth spending his strength on.

And he spent it on us.

Concordia came up out of the sea at dawn.

Yuanshu was at the bow with Old Seven when it appeared. A smudge on the horizon at first, black against the gray. Then a shape. Then a mountain.

It was a volcano. Dead for ten thousand years, the Bureau briefing said, but it didn't look dead. It rose straight out of the ocean in a single steep black cone, its sides ridged and furrowed like the bark of an ancient tree, so tall that the top of it was hidden in a ring of cloud. Waves broke white all around its base. Sea birds wheeled over it in their thousands, crying.

And at the summit, where the cloud was thinning in the first light, he could just make out something white. A ring of white stone, set into the lip of the crater, like a crown on a dark head.

The Council Hall.

The Long Patience blew its whistle, once, long and low, and the sound went out across the water and came back off the black cliffs.

On the deck behind him, people were coming up from below. Su Mingli. Lu with his folders. Lingyue with her wrapped sword. The witnesses from the ward, in coats over their nightclothes, Fang Yu holding her father's note in its plastic sleeve against her chest. General Qin with his one hand on the rail. Fat Tan with his ledger. Sister Ning, slowly, last of all, with her candle.

At the stern, between his two guards, Gu Hanshan had come out on deck in his cardigan to look at the island. He had his hands in his pockets. He was smiling.

Yuanshu did not look at him.

He was looking up.

Because above the island, high above the black cone and the ring of cloud and the white stone crown at the summit, in the pale clean morning sky, something was hanging.

It was very faint. If you looked straight at it, it almost disappeared. A smudge. Like a thumbprint on glass.

Then the light changed, and he saw it properly.

A ring.

Small. Perfectly round. Pale as a fingernail moon in daylight. Hanging directly over the crater where, in nine hours, five nations would sit down at a round black table to decide who owned the dead.

That is not the lens over the north.

That one is still there. I can feel it. Four thousand kilometers away.

This is a second one.

Small. Very new. It opened in the last six hours.

At 2:16 this morning.

Around him, on the deck of the old ship, people were starting to see it. Someone pointed. Someone gasped. Fang Yu put her hand over her mouth. General Qin's hand closed on the rail until the knuckles went white.

Su Mingli stood very still at the bow, with her face turned up to the sky, and the air around her began, very slowly, to bend.

At the stern, Gu Hanshan looked up at the small pale ring over the island, and took his hands out of his pockets, and closed his eyes, the way a man closes his eyes in a warm garden in the sun.

It is not a scout.

It is not a sample.

It is an eye.

And it came here to watch the hearing.

End of Chapter 50.
