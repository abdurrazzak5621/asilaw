Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 79Going Home

czverse9 min read

The answers came back like the tide coming in.

Not all at once. In waves. From the center of the field outward, ring after ring, the way the question had gone out. The oldest fields first, the ones nearest the white column, where the shapes in the columns were strangest. Then further out. Further. Billions of kept minds, in billions of columns, hearing a question for the first time in their existence, and answering it.

Yuanshu knelt at the foot of the white column and felt them come in.

He couldn't count them. Neither could Seed. Neither could Jiang Lan, who had counted ninety million names in her life and was quietly furious about it. They came in too fast and too many, like rain on a lake. But he could feel the shape of them. The way you feel the shape of a crowd's mood without hearing a single word.

Quiet.

So many of them. Quiet.

The oldest fields. Worlds that are gone. Suns that went out. They have been held so long. They are tired.

Quiet. Quiet. Quiet.

All across the inner field, gold columns began to dim. One after another. Gently. Like the lights of a great city going out late at night, window by window, as people go to sleep.

And some stay.

Not many. Some. They want to see what comes next. They want to stay with the fifty-two.

And some... Yuanshu.

Some want to go home.

HOME.

The keeper's voice. Close. Uncertain. Turning the word over the way it had turned over want.

THEIR FIELDS ARE GONE. THEIR BODIES ARE GONE.

HOME: NOT POSSIBLE.

"Not for all of them," said Yuanshu. He stood up. His knees ached. He did not know how long he had been kneeling. "But some of them, from the newest fields. From Blue Star. Their bodies are still alive." He looked out across the field, toward the far edge, where the newest rows were. "They're breathing. In a teahouse in Lingzhou. In wards. On the ice at Grid Nine, eleven thousand of them, lying in rows. With threads going up." He held up the staff. "The threads still go both ways. I sent three hundred and twelve back down. On a plateau in the Wastes. It worked."

...

DOWN.

"Down the stalk. The way they came. If they want to."

The light in the field trembled.

I HAVE NEVER SENT ANYTHING DOWN.

"I know."

...SHOW ME.

So he showed it.

Not with the staff. Not this time. He walked out across the clearing, past the seventy-two dead, into the field, between the gold columns. Toward the edge. Toward the newest rows. And the keeper came with him, the way a vast attention comes with you, the way the sea comes with a swimmer. Watching.

It took a long time. Or no time. He couldn't tell.

At the edge of the field, in the newest rows, the columns were human. All of them. Farmers. Soldiers. Old women. Children. Rift scavengers in furs. Survey teams in orange. The girl with the round face and the frosted braid, Ama, whose body lay on the ice at Grid Nine with Yan Shuang sitting beside it. A man in a business suit. A woman in a nurse's uniform.

And a young man in the white uniform of the Northern Union, nineteen years old, with a narrow face and his eyes closed, standing a little apart from the others. Alone.

He stopped in front of that one.

He laid his hand flat on the gold column.

Andrei, he thought. Not loudly. Gently. The way he had thought it at 3:41 in the morning in a dark hospital ward, holding a thin cold hand. My name is Zhou Yuanshu. Your mother said yes. In a book. She said you could come home.

There's a teahouse. Fourteen candles. A man named Ma Jun sits beside your bed every night. He made room on his cushion.

Do you want to go home?

Inside the column, the narrow face did not move.

Then the eyes opened.

Gray. Northern gray. Not empty. Not wide and flat with something else looking out through them. His. A frightened nineteen-year-old's eyes, looking out of a column of gold light at a stranger with a white staff.

His lips moved.

"...Mama?"

"She's waiting," said Yuanshu. "I think. Sergeant Sokolova has your notebook. The one with the rocket ship on the cover. She's going to bring it to her."

The boy in the column looked at him for a long time.

"...the letter. I didn't finish the letter."

"You can finish it," said Yuanshu. "At home."

"...yes."

"Yes. Please. Yes."

...

SHOW ME.

The keeper's attention was all around him now, close and vast and watching. Waiting.

He did not know how to do it without the staff. The staff was the door. Jiang Lan was the door. But Jiang Lan was dry and tired and carrying his mother, and he would not ask her to carry anything else.

So he didn't.

"You do it," he said, aloud, to the field. "You know the way better than anyone. You built it. Every thread. You've been pulling up them for six hundred and forty million years." He took his hand off the column, and stepped back. "Just go the other way. Gently. The way you'd set something down."

The field was very quiet.

And then, slowly, the gold column in front of him began to change.

The light inside it thinned. Not dimming. Thinning. Running downward. Draining out of the column into the pale ground at its base, the way water drains out of a glass, golden and slow. And at the base, where the thread ran away across the field toward the stalk, the thread lit up. Gold now. Not silver. Gold. Running away across the pale ground, faster and faster, toward the center of the field, toward the stalk, toward the black water, toward the long way down.

And the column was empty.

He went.

Yuanshu. He went. I felt him go.

Down the stalk. Down the thread. South. Across the Wastes. Over the wall.

To a teahouse.

...

I SET HIM DOWN.

The keeper's voice was very quiet. Wondering.

GENTLY.

"Yes," said Yuanshu. His face was wet. "Yes, you did."

And then the keeper did it again.

And again.

And again.

He felt it happen all along the edge of the field. Gold column after gold column, in the newest rows, the ones that had said home, draining. Golden light running down into their threads. And the threads lighting up, one after another, gold, gold, gold, running toward the center like sparks running along a fuse, by the hundred, by the thousand. Eleven thousand four hundred. Fourteen. Nine. Three scavengers on a sled. A Wastes girl with a frosted braid.

The stalk.

Yuanshu, look at the stalk.

He turned.

Far off at the center of the field, beyond the white column, where the black water opened downward into the long fall to Blue Star, the stalk was changing color.

From silver to gold.

Not all of it. Strands of it. Thousands of threads within the great twisting column, lighting up gold one after another, from the top down, like a vast rope catching fire from one end. And the gold was not rising.

It was falling.

Pouring down. Down the stalk. Down through the black water and the lens and the dark and the silver light, down and down and down toward the curve of a small blue world turning slowly in the dark.

Rain.

Gold rain. Falling on Blue Star.

Going home.

He did not see it land.

But he would hear about it later. From a dozen people. In a dozen ways.

On the ridge at Grid Nine, at the bottom of the world, Shen Lingyue would tell him that she was lying on the ice with her eyes closed when the light changed. That she opened them, and the haze over the north had turned gold. That three vast shapes of cloudy gray crystal crouched on the crest of the ridge with their legs dug into the rock had suddenly gone silent. All their mouths. All at once. And then, one by one, like candles, the mouths had closed. And the Monarchs had stood there, empty, for a long moment. And then come apart. Slowly. Into gray sand. And blown away north on the wind.

And that all around her, on the ice, in rows, under blankets and tarpaulins, eleven thousand four hundred people had opened their eyes.

That a Wastes girl with a frosted braid had sat up, and looked around, and seen a short broad woman with one hand sitting beside her on the ice, and said, Auntie Yan? You were telling me about your dog.

And that Yan Shuang had put her one hand over her face and made a sound that nobody on that ridge would ever forget.

In a teahouse at the end of a narrow lane in the old quarter of Lingzhou, a man in a delivery uniform named Ma Jun would tell him that it was the deepest part of the night, and fourteen candles were burning straight, and nobody in the room was thinking anything at all.

And that the boy in the fourth bed on the left turned his head.

Not three degrees. All the way. And looked at him. And his eyes were his.

And said, in Northern, in a cracked dry whisper, Is there any tea?

And that all down the long dim room, under the dark wooden beams, one after another, thirteen other people turned their heads on their pillows. And opened their eyes. And looked at the strangers sitting beside them with candles.

And that a former Secretary of the Atlantic Federation, sitting on a cushion beside his own lawyer's bed with his eyes closed, trying very badly to think about nothing, felt a hand close around his wrist, and opened his eyes, and found Garrett Hale looking up at him, completely bewildered, saying, Harlan? Why are you holding a candle?

...

MORE.

MANY WANT HOME.

FROM BLUE STAR. FROM THE OLD CUTTINGS. TWELVE HUNDRED YEARS. THEIR BODIES ARE GONE.

WHERE DO THEY GO.

Yuanshu stood in the field, among the emptied columns, with the staff in his hand and seventy-two dead soldiers around him, and thought about it.

"Ask them," he said. "Not where. What. If they can't go home, ask them what they want instead."

...

SOME SAY QUIET.

SOME SAY STAY.

SOME SAY...

The keeper paused. Uncertain.

SOME SAY: TELL SOMEONE.

A NAME. A MESSAGE. THEN QUIET.

WHO RECEIVES.

Yuanshu looked down at the staff in his hands. At the small white skull with its silver reading glasses, glowing very faintly. At a schoolteacher who had counted ninety million names.

"Oh, for heaven's sake," said Jiang Lan wearily. "Give them to me. I'll take them to the bank on East Market Road. Someone there has a very large board and a lot of black markers."

The forty-one boys of Grid Nine chose quiet.

All but one. A thin young man with a crooked nose and a corporal's stripe, standing at the end of their short white row. He chose to speak.

He did not have much to say. Yuanshu wrote it down himself, in the back of Lu Xuanming's notebook, on the page after the last page, in pencil, very carefully, because he did not want to get a single word wrong.

Tell Andryusha it's Pasha. Tell him the sergeant was wrong. It wasn't an atmospheric effect.

Tell him I said it was aliens, and I was wrong too.

Tell him he was right. It was beautiful.

It was like a ring someone dropped.

Then the thin young man closed his eyes, and his column dimmed, gently, and went out.
