Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 127Rift Weather

czverse9 min read

Seed called the time to the minute, and the rifts opened on it.

"Eighteen forty-one," said Yuanshu, on the roof of the old Textile Bureau in the middle of the old quarter, with the staff in one hand and Wei Desheng's map in the other. "Thirty-one hours and fourteen minutes after the last spike. If the beasts are listening to the same clock as the scout, that's when they move."

"And if they're not?" said Captain Lin Wen, beside him, with a radio in each hand.

"Then we've all stood on a roof in the cold for nothing, and I'll buy everyone dinner."

At 18:41, the rift in the old fish market tore open.

It was a grade one. A small one. The kind that opened twice a month somewhere in Lingzhou and closed again before the Defense Corps finished parking. A ragged vertical slit in the air above the empty market stalls, violet at the edges, the size of a doorway. Out of it came what always came out of small rifts: ashbacks, low and gray and doglike, with spines of black glass down their backs. Twenty of them. Thirty.

They did not attack anything.

They came out of the slit and dropped onto the wet cobbles and turned, all together, the way iron filings turn toward a magnet. North. Toward the river. Toward a squat gray building on the far bank. And began, at a steady trot, to walk.

At 18:41 and twenty seconds, the rift by the harbor opened. Then the two under the Exhibition Hall. Then three more that the Defense Corps had not even known were there, in a parking garage and a school gymnasium and the basement of a noodle shop on Jinhua Road. All of them small. All of them pouring out ashbacks and stilt-legged glass herons and things with too many joints that had no names yet.

All of them turning north.

"Eight rifts," said Lin Wen, listening to both radios at once. "Nine. Eleven. All grade one. Maybe six hundred beasts total." She lowered the radios. "They're going to walk straight through the city to the Institute."

"Through the city," said Yuanshu, "past three million people, eating anything that's in the way." He looked down at the map. "Desheng."

He says: Fish Market to the Institute bridge, the short way, is down Salt Street, through the Lantern Arcade, across Tannery Square.

He says: they will not go the short way. Animals never do. They will go the open way. Down the old canal road. It is wider. It has always been wider.

He says: I delivered on the canal road for eleven years. The dogs always went that way too.

"Then that's where we hold them," said Yuanshu. "Seed."

Already moving.

The First Legion came off the north wall at a run.

Not all of it. Seed would not let him bring all of it. Seventeen thousand dead inside the city was a load he could not carry and still think. He brought two thousand, under Captain Ma Lianhua, with eleven of her new sergeants, and the three hundred of the Spear Corps under Old Seven, and they came down the Sector Nine stairs and through the dark streets of the old quarter in four columns, as fast as bone could run, past lit windows and people standing frozen on their balconies with their phones up, filming.

Song Yanyan ran ahead of them in her gray silk with her bouquet of stems, faster than anything on the street, shouting directions that nobody living could hear and every dead soldier behind her could.

Yuanshu went with the Spear Corps, on foot, because the Field went where he went.

He felt it settle over the old quarter as he moved. Five kilometers of slope. Everything that died inside it would run downhill toward the river, toward him, toward standing up again. He felt the cobbles under his boots and the bones of the city under the cobbles: old drains, old foundations, a cholera pit from two hundred years ago that he had not known was there, all of it suddenly leaning toward him in the dark like grass leans toward water.

Field radius: 5.1 kilometers.

It covers the canal road, Tannery Square and the Institute bridge. It does not cover the harbor. The harbor rifts will have to be the Defense Corps.

Load: sixty-eight percent. Do not raise anyone new tonight.

They met the beasts at the canal road.

It was wide, the way Wei Desheng had said: four lanes of old cobbles between a dry stone canal and a row of shuttered warehouses, lit orange by streetlamps. The ashbacks came down it at a trot, three hundred of them in the first wave, spines bristling, eyes fixed on something across the river that none of them could see. They did not look at the dead. Beasts never looked at the dead.

They looked at the shield.

Hu Dabao stood in the middle of the canal road in Defense Corps green with his tower shield set on the cobbles in front of him, and fifty Defense Corps soldiers behind him in a line, and the light from the streetlamps on his face.

"Behind me is home," he said, not loudly, to nobody, the way you say the first line of a prayer.

The ashbacks hit the shield line at a run.

It held. Yuanshu, on the roof of a warehouse forty meters back, with Captain Lin Wen and her radios and Old Seven, watched it hold. Dabao did not move. The first ashback hit his shield and bounced off it, and the second, and the tenth, and the fifty soldiers behind him braced their shoulders against his back and held, and the beasts piled up in front of the line, snarling, climbing over each other, and did not get through.

"Now," said Captain Ma Lianhua, through the staff.

The dead came out of the warehouses.

It was not a charge. Ma Lianhua did not believe in charges. She had held a river gate for six hours with ninety men and a broken radio, and she had learned in those six hours that the side that charges first is usually the side that loses its men first.

She put them on the flanks. Two thousand of the dead in the shuttered warehouses on the left side of the canal road and the dry stone canal on the right, and when the beasts piled up against Dabao's shield, the dead stepped out of the doorways and down out of the canal and took them from the sides. Quietly. Without shouting. With rusted bayonets and bare bone hands and the butts of old rifles, in ranks of nine, each one watching the nine beside it.

They are counting.

Not well. Not the way Grandpa Xu described. But they are trying. Ma Lianhua told every sergeant: watch your nine.

Load: sixty-four percent. It is helping.

An ashback twisted under a soldier's bayonet and got its jaws around the soldier's leg and tore it off at the knee. The soldier fell.

And got up.

Yuanshu felt it happen. The Field did it. Not him: the slope. The bones of the leg lay on the cobbles, and the soldier lay beside them, and the weight of the Field ran downhill through both of them toward the river, and the leg slid back across the stones and fitted itself to the knee. The soldier stood up in the middle of the fight and put her weight on it and went on.

It was the woman with the hairnet. The one who had been afraid her granddaughter would see her.

"Did you see that?" said Lin Wen. Her voice had gone very strange.

"Yes."

"Your dead don't stay down."

"Not inside the Field."

That is what it is for.

Domain of the Dead was always supposed to be this. A place where your army does not die.

It took you a year to grow into it.

Lingyue's students took the second wave.

The glass herons came over the warehouse roofs, stilt-legged and silent, wings of fused black glass glinting under the lamps, forty of them, gliding down toward the shield line from above where Dabao could not lift his shield. They had not come for the dead. They had come because the dead were in the way.

Forty-one students of the Lingzhou Martial Academy were waiting for them on the warehouse roofs, in two lines, with their blades lit.

Inside the Field, every blade burned silver-gray.

Lingyue stood at the end of the front line with her own sword raised. She did not shout. She lifted her blade one hand-width, and forty-one blades came up with it, and when the herons came down over the roofline she brought it down, and the pale fire went out from forty-two edges at once in a flat silver sheet and cut the first rank of herons out of the air.

The pale fire touched the dead on the roof below. It did not burn them. It ran over their bones like water over stone and went on.

"Hold your angle," said Lingyue, without raising her voice. "Hold it. You. The one with the hammer. You're bending it too far left. Stop thinking about the skeleton. It's fine. It likes you. Think about the bird."

The student with the hammer, who was seventeen and very pale, corrected his angle.

The second rank of herons fell.

It was over in forty minutes.

Six hundred beasts on the canal road and in the harbor district, dead or fled back into their rifts as the rifts closed. Eleven Defense Corps wounded, two of them badly, at the harbor, where the Field did not reach. Four students with burns, none serious. No civilians. Dabao's shield dented in the middle in the shape of an ashback's skull.

Of the two thousand dead who had come off the wall, two thousand walked back up the Sector Nine stairs.

Kills under your command count. They always have.

Yuanshu, that was very good. Ma Lianhua is very good. Dabao is very good. Lingyue is extremely good and I am going to tell her you said so, so that you have to say it yourself first.

He was sitting on the edge of the warehouse roof with his feet over the canal road, because his legs had decided to stop holding him. Lingyue came and sat beside him. She had soot on her face and her sword across her knees, still faintly silver.

"You're extremely good," he said.

She looked at him sideways. "Seed told you to say that."

"Seed told me it was going to tell you, so I'd have to say it first."

"Hm." She almost smiled. "It's right."

Kwan called at midnight. He knew from the first word that it was bad.

"The arrays," she said. "Down in the chamber. At 18:41 they spiked, the way they always do on the pulse. Then at 19:20 they spiked again." He could hear her typing. "And kept spiking. All the way to 19:25. Every time a beast died."

He went very still.

"On the canal road?"

"Not just there. The harbor. Everywhere. Every beast that died tonight inside a kilometer of the river." She swallowed. "Something went into the seed when they died. Not much. A thread. Like the ones from Walter Ashby."

Oh.

Oh, no.

Yuanshu, it did not call them so that they would attack the city.

It called them so that they would die near it.

"The wound," said Kwan. "I measured it again an hour ago. It closed six hours faster tonight than the model said it would. Six hours, in forty minutes." Her voice was not steady. "Every beast we kill near the Institute is feeding it."

He looked down at the canal road. At the bodies of the ashbacks on the orange cobbles, and the herons broken on the warehouse roofs, and the dead of the First Legion standing in their ranks of nine, waiting to be told what to do next.

New estimate.

Eleven days. Plus or minus two.

And the next pulse is in thirty-one hours.

They had won. Every one of their dead had walked home. And the thing under the city had eaten well.
