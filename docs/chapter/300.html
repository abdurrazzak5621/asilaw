Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 300The Oldest Cable

czverse10 min read

The deep water was quiet now.

He had not been ready for that. The last time, a year ago, it had been busy: the whole vast dark between arms full of hands, ten thousand cables humming with marks going up, the web of the oldest thing anyone had ever made alive and patient and reading, a billion a second. He had come along the thread dim, with twelve thousand dead round him like a hand round a match, afraid to be seen.

Now there was nothing to be seen by.

The cables were still there. He could feel them, the way you feel the beams of a house in the dark: ten thousand of them, each twisted from ten thousand threads, running in out of the black from every side toward the place where they met. But nothing went up them. No marks. No hum. The stones of a thousand orchards had stopped marking the night the picker stopped, or were marking still into nothing, and the web lay in the deep water like a net hung out to dry after the boats have come in.

And the hands.

They were still on the cables. Thousands of them. Vast and gray and slow, where they had been when a man in an orange jacket held up a mark that said the house was empty. Open. Every one. Fingers spread. Holding nothing. Hanging on the cables in the dark like gloves left on a fence.

No one has touched them. No one is in them to move them.

They will hang there until the cables rot. I do not know how long that is. I do not think the cables rot.

He went past them along the net. Fast. Faster than last time, much faster, because the web was still now and the net was quick, and he was not carrying twelve thousand embers. Only one. Old Seven beside him, cold and steady, one-armed, facing ahead.

And all round them, warm, patient, going the way it had always pointed, the Collector.

He had wondered what it would be like, to travel inside it.

It was not like anything. That was the second time he had thought that about the Collector, and he suspected it would not be the last. It was not a ship. It did not carry them. It went, and they went with it, the way you go with a tide. It was all round them and not touching them. It was warm the way a room is warm when someone has been sitting in it a long time. And it was full.

He could feel what it was full of. Not see. Feel. The way you feel the weight of a book in your hands without opening it.

Wants.

More than Seed had a number for. Seed said so, quietly, and then did not say anything else for a while. Wants from worlds he would never see, kept for longer than there had been stars over Blue Star. The fold-cores' out. The singers' to be chosen. Everything the Architects' whole people had ever wanted. A billion billion small warm homes and mores and pleases and not yets, packed so close they had stopped being separate, the way sand stops being separate on a beach. And somewhere in the middle of all of it, in a place that was emptier than the rest, one very old, very small, very faint one, pointing.

That is the first one.

I can feel it pulling. Like a compass needle. It does not know it is a compass. It only points.

It points along the oldest cable.

They reached the face at the fourth hour.

The knot. The place where ten thousand cables met, where every mark from every stone in every orchard in the deep water had once come in and been read. He had seen it with a burning man held up in front of it. Now it hung in the dark unlit, vast, slow, a tangle the size of the keeper's field or larger, silent.

It was not reading anything. There was nothing to read.

It still looks.

I can feel it. It is not doing anything. It has nothing to do. But it looks.

I think it is the last part of the picker that is still working. The part that read Xiaoyu's mark. It read something true, and it has been looking at the place where the true thing was ever since.

He stopped. He did not mean to. He stopped on the net in front of the face of the oldest thing anyone had ever made, a year after his sister's handwriting had stopped it, and looked at it.

Does it know? he thought. That the house is empty?

I do not know.

I do not think it knows anything. It read it. That is not the same.

The Collector did not stop. It was very slow and it did not stop. But it went past the face more slowly than it had gone past anything, and Seed said afterward that the warmth of it had leaned, very slightly, toward the knot as it went by, the way an old person leans toward a gravestone they are walking past, without meaning to.

We made it, said the Collector. Not to them. To the dark. It was the first thing. Before the counting things. Before anything.

I am sorry nobody told you sooner.

Then it went on.

At home, it was the ninth hour.

He felt it. He did not know how. He was a very long way along the net from a hall on East Ring Road, and getting further every minute, and he should not have been able to feel anything there at all. But he felt it the way you feel a hand on your chest in your sleep. Fifteen hundred small warmths, coming up through a floor, low, steady. And a hand.

Gao Min says: nine hours.

She says: he's still warm.

She has written it on the rota. In ink. 16:59. Nine hours. Still warm.

Lingyue says.

Lingyue says: good. Ten.

He went on along the net with the warmth under him, rowed.

The oldest cable did not look like the others.

He saw it the way Seed saw it, at the far side of the face, where the knot thinned out into the dark and the ten thousand cables that had come in from every orchard did not go on. They stopped there. The knot was where they went to. They had come to be read.

All except one.

On the far side of the face, where nothing else went, one cable went out.

Older. He could feel that it was older, the way you can feel that a stone step is older than the stones round it: it was worn. Not frayed. Smooth. Worn into a kind of groove, the way a doorstep wears where feet have gone over it for a thousand years, and then ten thousand, and then longer than anyone had a number for. It was thicker than the others and paler. It was not twisted from threads. It had never been threads. It had always been one thing, and it ran away from the face of the picker in a straight line into the dark between the arms, toward nothing Seed had a word for.

That is the carry road.

The hands went out along the other cables to the orchards. They picked. They came back to the face. And from the face, everything went along this one. Home.

Every hand that was ever full went along it. And came back along it empty, and went out again.

For longer than the gardens.

I can feel the wear. I cannot measure it. I do not have a number big enough. It is the oldest road anything ever made.

The Collector turned onto it.

It did not hesitate. The needle in the empty place in it pointed straight along the worn pale cable into the dark, and it went, slowly, the way it had always gone, and he and Old Seven went with it.

Ichiro is ringing.

He felt it a little after the tenth hour. Very faint, a long way behind. Through the net, from the root, from a disc of black stone in the bow of a ship rowed by two hundred and forty thousand people in the orbit of Anthos. A bell. The fog-code. Rung well, this time, because it was the one who had invented it.

He says: he can see you. Just. Off the end of everything. He says you're the furthest thing he's ever seen, and he's seen the edge of the cloud.

He says: he's going to ring the coming-in call every night. At home time. Shiomi time. So you know which way the shore is.

He says: Kiyo says hurry up.

He says: she says that about everything.

And under it, fainter, from further away along the net, off to one side, out at the edge of a cloud of small dim lights that had fallen out of the picker's hands a year ago: another bell. Rung badly.

The Watch.

Nana Ampofo. She can feel us go by. She has felt the net change. She has rung the fog-code. She learned it from Ichiro and the quiet ones.

She says:

WATCH OF THE SHELF.

YOU ARE EARLY.

THAT IS NEW.

IT IS NOT A YEAR. DO NOT ASK US. WE WILL KNOW IF YOU ASK EARLY. IT IS NOT ALLOWED.

GO CAREFULLY ON THE OLD ROAD. WE CAN SEE IT FROM HERE. NOTHING HAS EVER COME BACK ALONG IT FULL.

COME BACK ALONG IT FULL.

— NANA.

He did not answer. She had said not to. He let the warmth of the Watch go by, off to one side, at the edge of a cloud of fallen lights, seven hundred and forty-three of the drowned of Kesse singing the names of the lost to them in the dark.

He went on.

At the twentieth hour, the net behind them was a long way behind.

He could feel it thin. Not break. Thin. The way the voices from the shore get small when the boat goes out, not because anyone stops talking, but because the water between gets wider. The warmth of the holders was still there. Faint. Under everything. But the words that came with it took longer.

Your mother's reading took eleven minutes to reach us.

It was the morning names. The first three. She reads them every morning. I heard her say: Wen Jia.

I heard it. Eleven minutes after she said it.

I know the girl in the Lingzhou stone is called Wen Jia. I do not know why your mother reads her name first. She always does. I have never known why.

He went still on the cable.

She's the first one, he thought. In the bottom of you. Of the four thousand. She said yes in a basement, nine years ago. Only if it's something good.

...thank you.

I am not going to remember it.

Tell me again tomorrow.

Every morning, he thought.

It is going to take longer, the further we go. Your mother's voice. Gao Min's count. Lingyue.

I am telling you first. There will be a point where a mark from home takes a day to reach us. And then a week.

I do not know where the house is. So I do not know how long.

Then I'll tell you, he thought. Every morning. Who the first one was. You don't need it from home. You've got me.

Seed did not answer for a moment.

Yes, it said. I have you.

At the thirty-first hour, there was something on the carry road.

He felt it before Seed did. A change in the cable. Not a break. A joining. Off to one side, very faint, a thread coming in out of the dark and running into the oldest cable, the way the threads of the orchards had run into the cables a year ago. But older. Grayer. And not humming with marks. Not humming with anything.

A stone.

On a world. An orchard. Its thread runs into the carry road here, not into the web. It must be older than the web. One of the first orchards. From before there were enough of them to need a face.

It is still marking.

Every year. On its reading morning. It reads what ripens and marks how bright and sends the mark up.

The marks are all the same.

It is marking nothing.

The Collector slowed.

He felt it slow. It had not slowed for the face of the picker; it had only leaned. It slowed now. The warmth all round them gathered, the way a person gathers themselves before they open a door they know.

I have something from there, said the Collector. Through Seed, quietly, the way it had once spoken to his sister on the bottom step of a platform. One want. I have kept it longer than most.

I would like to give it back.

I do not think there is anyone there to give it to.

He looked down the thread. Into the dark. Toward a stone on a world in one of the first orchards, still marking, every year, a field where nothing ripened any more.

There's always someone there, he thought. Ask me how I know.

The dead, said Seed. He means the dead.

He is a necromancer. He forgets that he is one, sometimes. And then he remembers.
