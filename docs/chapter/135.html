Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 135Zero Hour

czverse7 min read

The pulse came at 02:31 on the seventeenth day, and they went down ten minutes later.

Two ways. The steel cage under his mother's laboratory, the way he had gone down twice before. And the Bureau shaft on the other side of the rock, wider and older, that the Institute had used to lower a cylinder of law-glass into the ground thirty-one years ago with a broken scout inside it. Su Mingli had insisted on both. If one gets blocked, you still have a way out.

He went down in the cage. Old Seven. Adjutant Xu, with his cane. Lingyue. Shen Jinghong, who had not spoken since they left the surface. Shirasagi Ren in her white robes. Mo Shi, white-faced, with his eyes already closed. And Lu Zhengtian, in his good suit, with his silver-rimmed glasses and a Bureau keycard on a lanyard round his neck.

"The arrays are mine," Lu had said, in the war room, the night before. "Nobody else knows the control board. I built half of it. I've been running it for thirty-one years." He had not looked at anyone when he said it. "I'll be in the alcove, behind the glass. Where I always stand."

Nobody had argued. There had not been anyone else.

Down the Bureau shaft, in the big freight elevator, went the rest. Su Mingli. Marshal Orlov. Principal Yue Changfeng, with his saber over his shoulder and, for once, no sunflower seeds. Mo Xing and Bai Lu. Sixty of the Spear Corps, all that would fit in the round chamber without getting in each other's way. The other two hundred and forty were standing in the shaft above them, on ladders and maintenance ledges, all the way up to the surface, in a line.

At the top of the Bureau shaft, Hu Dabao stood with his shield across the opening and fifty Defense Corps soldiers behind him, and Hundred-Arrow Lin crouched on the gantry above with her bow across her knees.

Around the Institute, in a ring six hundred meters out, nineteen companies of the First Legion stood in their nines, asking each other, every minute, are you still here.

Field: 5.1 kilometers. Centered on you. It covers everything.

Load: fifty-three percent.

The wound is ninety-one percent closed. Kwan measured it at midnight.

We were right to come today.

The round chamber had changed again.

The arrays were barely glowing now. Pale, flickering, starved, the hum gone up so high it was almost a whine. The cloud inside the cylinder had grown to the top. The faceless one hung in it like a shape in fog, its head tipped back, its smooth front pointed straight up at the ceiling and the rock and the city and the sky. Where the hole in its chest had been, there was gray crystal, branching and cracked, closed over the seed except for a gap the size of a coin.

Through the gap, faintly, the color of a coal.

"Positions," said Su Mingli, from the far side of the chamber. She did not raise her voice.

They took them. Lu went into the observation alcove in the chamber wall, a small steel room behind a pane of law-glass with the control board in it, and put his keycard in the slot, and laid his hands on the switches. Shirasagi Ren stood in the middle of the floor, at the foot of the platform, and closed her eyes, and a pale dome came up around her, thin as a soap bubble, wide enough for six. Inside it: Mo Shi, sitting cross-legged on the stone. Bai Lu. Mo Xing.

The Kings stood outside it. Su Mingli to the north of the cylinder. Orlov to the east, with frost already spreading across the floor around his boots. Principal Yue to the south, with his saber. Shen Jinghong to the west, beside his granddaughter.

Yuanshu stood on the platform, a meter from the glass, with the staff in his hands. Old Seven a step behind him. The sixty of the Spear Corps around the platform in a ring, facing in. Adjutant Xu at the bottom of the steps, leaning on his cane, the knot at the top of the lattice.

When Lu cuts the arrays, the glass will not break at once. Kwan thinks thirty seconds. Perhaps less.

When the glass breaks, it will know it is cornered. I think it will sweep.

One slice at a time. Six hundred and twenty milliseconds of blind between slices. Shi calls the gaps. In the gaps, the living move. Out of the gaps, the living go quiet, or get inside Ren's dome.

The dead do not need the gaps. It cannot read them.

That is the plan.

And if it doesn't sweep?

Then I was wrong. And we find out what it does instead.

"Now," said Yuanshu.

In the alcove, Lu Zhengtian pulled the switches.

The hum stopped.

It was so sudden that Yuanshu's ears rang with the absence of it. Thirty-one years of a sound in the back of everyone's teeth, gone. The pale light of the arrays on the walls went out ring by ring, from the top of the chamber to the floor, and the only light left was the white of the Kings, and Ren's dome, and the faint coal-colored glow through the gap in the faceless one's chest.

The cloud inside the cylinder moved.

It did not drain. It drew in. All of it, toward the center, toward the chest, the way water draws toward a drain. The gray fog thinned and thinned and went into the gap in the crystal and was gone, and the faceless one hung in clear not-quite-liquid with nothing around it at all for the first time in thirty-one years.

Its head came down.

Not fast. Slowly. From straight up, the way a man looks down from a plane he has been watching, until its smooth front was level. Pointed at nothing. At the chamber. At them.

...it is not holding the beam any more.

It let go. It let go of the thing above it.

Yuanshu, it is looking at us.

The law-glass cracked.

It cracked from the inside, from a point in front of the scout's chest, a single white star in the glass that spread outward with a sound like river ice in spring. Shen Jinghong's hand went to his sword. Orlov's frost ran up the steps of the platform. Su Mingli raised one hand.

"Wait," said Yuanshu. "Not yet. Shi."

"Nothing," said Mo Shi, inside the dome, with his eyes shut. "Nothing yet. It's not... it's not sweeping. It's not doing anything. It's..."

The cylinder burst.

Three meters of law-glass came apart all at once, outward, in a ring of glittering knives. The Spear Corps on the platform took it in their ribs and their skulls and did not fall. Orlov caught the pieces that flew toward him in the air, every one, in a wall of ice that came up out of the floor in half a second. Ren's dome rang like a bell.

The not-quite-liquid hit the floor and steamed.

And the faceless one stepped down off its pedestal onto the stone.

It was taller than Yuanshu had thought. Three meters. One arm ending at the elbow, the seams running across its gray body where thirty-one years ago someone had fitted the pieces back together. The crystal in its chest glowing through the gap. Its smooth reflectionless face, holding the white light of four Kings and keeping it.

It turned its head.

Three degrees.

"Now!" said Mo Shi. "Now, it's... no..."

He stopped.

Yuanshu felt it at the same moment. The beam. A slice of attention three degrees wide, swinging out from the smooth face and passing across the chamber. It touched the Spear Corps and found nothing. It touched Orlov's ice and found nothing. It touched the east side of the room, where Marshal Kirill Orlov stood with frost around his boots and two hundred years in his face.

Orlov stopped.

Not frozen. Stopped. The way a woman in a rice terrace had stopped twelve hundred years ago, with the water around her ankles. The way a soldier on Concordia had stopped mid-run in the rain. His face went empty. His head began to turn. Three degrees, toward the faceless one.

"Ren!"

Shirasagi Ren's dome leapt.

It went out from her like a breath, thin and pale, across the floor, over Orlov, and closed around him, and the slice of attention hit the dome and slid off it, and Orlov blinked and staggered and caught himself on his own wall of ice.

"It's not six hundred," said Mo Shi. His voice was high and fast. "The gaps. It's not six hundred milliseconds. It's three. Three hundred. Less. It's sweeping faster than Hale. It's..."

I was wrong.

Hale was being read by something caged and far away, once every thirty-one hours. This is free, and cornered, and a meter away.

The gaps are 290 milliseconds. Not 620.

Yuanshu, the living cannot move in the gaps. They are too short. Nobody alive can do anything in 290 milliseconds.

The faceless one turned its head again.

Three more degrees.

And on the far side of the chamber, behind a pane of law-glass in a small steel room, with his hands on a dead control board and his keycard round his neck, Director Lu Zhengtian looked up, as the slice swung toward the alcove, and met the smooth blank face that had been waiting in this room for thirty-one years, and did not look away.
