Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 108The Key

czverse7 min read

The whole of Blue Star saw the Low Fire arrive.

It came out of the north at dusk, on the thirty-third day, out of the deep dark beyond the stars. At first it was only a faint gold point in the sky above the small white ring of the keeper. Then a coin. Then a disc. Then a ring, vast and slow and turning, filling a quarter of the northern sky, gray stone veined all through with low gold light, and all across its face, where there had once been mouths, small round windows, glowing.

It stopped above the end of the world, beside the keeper's small white ring, and hung there.

And on Blue Star, for about ten minutes, eight billion people panicked.

It was in the news within a minute. On every screen in Lingzhou. On every screen in the world. A THIRD RING. TWINS ACCOMPANIED BY THIRD HUNGER. CONCORDIA COUNCIL IN EMERGENCY SESSION. People ran into the streets in Lingzhou and stood on the corners pointing. Someone started the wall sirens by mistake. In the Northern Union, the state channel showed the gold ring on a loop with no commentary at all, which was somehow worse.

And then, at eleven minutes past seven, on every screen and every radio in Lingzhou, a voice.

Quiet. Sharp. Slightly hoarse. Speaking from a wheelchair on the battlements of Sector Nine, into a microphone, with a girl in a too-big hoodie standing behind her holding the handles.

"It's all right," said Zhou Wanqing. "That one's ours. My son is on it."

He came down the gold thread an hour later.

Not on the raft. The raft was in the cold place, at the heart of the Low Fire, with a dead captain standing at its rim in a doorway, facing out. The keeper made him something new. A thread of its own, from the small white ring to the wall. And he came down it alone, in its light, gold, falling softly out of the dark northern sky like a seed on the wind.

He landed on the battlements of Sector Nine.

The whole wall was there. The whole city. Nineteen thousand of the dead in their line, east and west, out of sight. Three million people on the rooftops and in the streets and on the slopes below the wall. Not shouting this time. Silent. Watching. The way they had watched a dead fireman walk into a burning tower.

He stood on the battlements with the white staff in his hand and Old Seven's absence beside him like a missing tooth, and looked at them.

At his mother, in her wheelchair.

At Su Mingli, standing beside her with her coat buttoned to the throat and her arms folded and her face like stone.

At Sister Ning, with her candle. At Lu Zhengtian with his glasses in his hand. At Fat Tan, enormous, crying openly, not bothering to hide it. At Captain Lin Wen with a drift of ash and dark air standing beside her, and a hand left in it.

At Xiaoyu.

She did not run to him.

She stood behind their mother's wheelchair with both hands on the handles and looked at him. Scowling. Ferociously. So ferociously that he knew she had been practicing it for a hundred and fifteen days.

He walked toward her.

He stopped in front of her.

She did not say anything. She looked at his face. At the white staff. At the brass key on its red string around his neck.

Then she held out her hand. Palm up.

"Key," said Zhou Xiaoyu.

He looked at her for a moment.

Then he lifted the red string over his head, slowly, and put the small brass key in her hand.

She closed her fingers around it.

"You came back," she said. "So you can have it back later. When I've decided." Her scowl was not holding at all. "I didn't answer the door for four months. Nobody came. I practiced."

And then she let go of the wheelchair, and hit him on the arm, not very hard, and then put both her arms around his neck and her face in his shoulder and did not let go for a long time.

His mother did not say anything either.

When Xiaoyu finally let go, Zhou Wanqing reached up from the wheelchair and took his face in both her thin hands, the way she had in an endless field above the sky, and in the cold place of his mother's absence for seven years, and at the north gate when she stepped out of a staff.

And looked at him.

"You're thinner," she said.

"You're not."

"I eat breakfast," said Zhou Wanqing. "Someone makes me." She did not let go of his face. "The babies," she said quietly. "We did it. Every drill this week. Every hospital. Every ward. Sixty-four seconds. Ninety. Five minutes, yesterday." Her thumbs moved on his cheeks. "Mothers holding them. Fathers. The Quiet sitting with the ones who have no one. Four notes. Over and over. In every maternity ward on Blue Star." Her voice was not steady. "The keeper sends the song down the thread to every one. So they all hum the same thing."

"Seed's idea."

"Your idea," said his mother. "Seed told me. It said you were never once loud."

Su Mingli was last.

She stood where she was, at the edge of the group, with her arms folded, and did not come forward.

He went to her.

She looked at him with her stone face.

"Captain Zhao," she said.

"He held the door," said Yuanshu. "In the middle of the fire. Until it was done. Then he went quiet. Standing up." He swallowed. "He's still standing. On the raft. In the doorway. Facing out."

Su Mingli closed her eyes.

"He was the stubbornest man in the army," she said. "Qi used to say he was more stubborn than a door." She opened her eyes. "I'll tell Zhao Mei."

"I already wrote it down. For her. Everything."

"Then I'll stand next to her while she reads it." Su Mingli looked up. North. At the gold ring hanging in the darkening sky, beside the small white one. And past them. Higher. Where the stars were coming out.

Where two gray rings, side by side, had become one.

"Four days," she said.

You could see it from the wall now.

Not two gray specks. One. Larger. Much larger than either had been. A ring of cracked gray stone turning in the dark beyond the stars, and in it, two fires. Two coals. Gray-red, side by side, like a pair of eyes.

The news had stopped calling it the Twins.

It called it what the Sixth called it, on the thread, when Wanqing asked.

The Mouth.

That night, they met in the old banking hall of the Ossuary. Under the board with its six columns. YES. ASK MY FAMILY. ONLY TO SPEAK. QUIET. CAME HOME. FROM OUT THERE.

Everyone who mattered. Su Mingli. Wanqing in her wheelchair. Lu. Kwan. Sister Ning. Fat Tan. Captain Lin Wen and her mother. On a screen, Marshal Orlov in the Northern Union, with Sokolova standing behind him in white. On another, Ifeoma Adeyemi in her palace by the southern sea. On another, Shirasagi Ren on a mountain in Yamato with seven empty cushions behind her. On another, Chair Adaeze Okafor in the crater on Concordia, under a digital clock counting down.

3 DAYS 22 HOURS 14 MINUTES.

And on the last screen, not a face. Just a gold-veined wall, and in it, a choir of low gold antlered shapes, standing very still. The Sixth. Speaking from the Low Fire, hanging over the end of the world.

...there is no lock, they said. ...we have told you.

...no cold place. No seal. No question that will stop it.

...it cannot be asked.

"Then we hide," said Orlov from his screen. "The dimming. The whole world. We have practiced. It cannot see what is quiet."

...for how long?

Nobody answered.

...it will not leave, said the Sixth softly. ...it has fallen a very long way. It has not eaten. It is starving. It knows there is a rich field here. It heard the first ring die. It heard someone ask.

...if it cannot see you, it will wait.

...it will hang over your world. Listening. For as long as it takes. A day. A year. A thousand years.

...and one day, someone will be loud.

The banking hall was very quiet.

Yuanshu stood under the board with the white staff in his hand and looked at the screens. At the faces. At the Sixth.

"Then we don't hide forever," he said. "We hide long enough."

Everyone looked at him.

"Long enough for what?" said Su Mingli.

He looked at the last screen. At the choir of gold antlered shapes in the gold-veined wall of a ring that had once been a Hunger, and had fallen across the Star Sea in thirty-three days because someone asked it to.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But it's coming here because someone asked. That's what it heard. That's why it turned." He looked down at the staff. "It's not just hungry. It's curious."

"And curious things," said Jiang Lan, very dryly, from the small white skull, "can be asked things. Even if they can't be stopped."

The choir on the last screen did not speak for a long moment.

Then the Sixth, all of them, slowly, inclined their long antlered heads.

...we have an idea, they said. ...you will not like it.
