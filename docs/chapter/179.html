Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 179Above the Sky

czverse9 min read

The keeper came for them at three in the morning, the way it always came, and this time it did not send a thread. It sent a road.

They were waiting on the north wall of Lingzhou at Sector Nine, all of them, in the cold: five hundred and fifty-one dead in their rows along the battlements, the Spear Corps and the forty in white and the two hundred and eleven from the cemetery hill, and seven of the living in the middle of them. Yuanshu with the staff. Lingyue with her grandfather's sword and a borrowed one from the Academy armory on her back. Qin Hao with his notebook in his breast pocket because he said he would not go anywhere without it now. The Mo twins. Bai Lu with her bag. Hu Dabao with his shield on his good arm and his left still in a sling, who had not been asked and had not asked and had simply been standing on the wall at midnight when the rest of them arrived.

Old Seven at the front. Adjutant Xu beside him with his cane.

Su Mingli stood at the top of the stairs behind them with the First Legion banked along the wall in the dark on either side of her, eighteen thousand gray points, and did not say anything.

At three, the small white ring over the end of the world opened.

Not a lens. Not an eye. A door. And out of it, down the dark sky, across the Wastes, over the plain, to the wall, came gold. Not a thread. A road. Wide enough for nine to walk abreast. Laid across the night like a bridge of warm light, from the battlements of Sector Nine up into the north, up, up, to the end of the world, and through the ring, and beyond.

COME.

I WILL CARRY YOU DOWN AFTER. WHATEVER HAPPENS. I PROMISED.

They walked up.

He had been in the field once before.

He remembered it the way you remember a dream: a soft light that came from everywhere and nowhere and never changed, a pale warm ground that crunched faintly under bone feet, and gold columns. Endless rows of them, stretching away in every direction until they blurred into a haze of gold. A woman in a straw hat. A child in a nightdress. Forty-one young soldiers in white, standing in a short row of their own.

The light was the same. The ground was the same.

The columns were gone.

He stood at the top of the gold road where it came down onto the pale ground of the field, and looked out, and could not see anything at all. Only the soft even light and the warm pale ground, going away flat in every direction to the haze. And on the ground, everywhere, as far as he could see, circles. Faint rings in the pale surface, a meter or two across, in long straight rows, like the marks a glass leaves on a table. Millions of them. Where the columns had stood.

They went home.

All of those. You asked them. They went quiet. They went home.

This is what an empty field looks like.

Behind him, the forty from Grid Nine had stopped at the top of the road. They were standing very still, in their white, looking out at the rows of empty rings. One of them, a boy who had been nineteen, took two steps forward and knelt and put his bone hand flat inside the nearest circle on the pale ground, the way you put your hand on the place where someone used to sit.

The few who stayed were two kilometers north.

He felt them before he saw them. Three hundred and eighty-one gold columns in a cluster, close together, the way people stand close in the cold. Old and young. From Port Abiri and Kesse, mostly. Some from worlds he did not know: a thing like a tree, a thing with long thin fingers and four eyes. And on the near edge of the cluster, a little apart from the others, beside three empty rings in the pale ground, a thin tired man in the winter whites of the Ninth Mountain Battalion, with his eyes closed.

Major Zhuo Wenli.

And around them, in a triangle, a kilometer apart, the walkers.

He had seen the faceless one in the round chamber. He had thought he knew.

He had not known.

They were tall. Eight meters, perhaps nine. Long, narrow, upright, standing on two legs that were too long and too thin, with two arms that hung almost to the ground. Smooth. Pale. Faceless: where a face should have been there was only a smooth blank curve, gray-white, like the inside of a shell. And their bodies were not bodies. They were woven. From close to, he could see it: thread. Millions of fine silver-white threads, wrapped and knotted and woven tight over and around each other, the way a rope is woven from string, into the shape of something tall that walked.

They did not move.

Two of them stood with their blank faces turned toward the cluster of columns, stepping: three degrees, still, three degrees, still, the way the scout's head had stepped in the round chamber. Slow. Patient. Reading.

The third was not stepping.

It stood at the near point of the triangle, a kilometer from the gold road, with its blank face tipped up and forward and held perfectly still, pointed at a single column on the near edge of the cluster.

Straight up.

It is holding him.

It has been holding him for a day and a half. I can feel it pulling. Slowly. The way a thread pulls.

The keeper says it is not taking him yet. It is deciding.

When it decides, it lifts.

"Mo Shi," said Yuanshu.

"I see it." Mo Shi was standing beside him at the top of the road with his eyes shut. His face had gone very white. "Three lamps. Two stepping. One holding." He swallowed. "The holding one doesn't step. It's not sweeping. It's just... pointed. It can't see anything except Major Zhuo."

Then the holding one is blind to everything else.

Two lamps. Not three.

The pocket is bigger with two. Su Mingli said so.

"How big?"

"Twelve meters," said Mo Shi. "Moving. Four seconds. Then it jumps." His eyes were still shut. His lips were moving, very slightly, counting. "Next one opens in nine seconds. Two hundred meters left. Then forty meters further. Then..."

"Xing."

"I know," said Mo Xing. He had already taken his brother's hand. With the other, he reached out and took Bai Lu's sleeve. "Two at a time. Dabao, Qin Hao, you're next. Lingyue..."

"I'll run," said Lingyue.

"You can't run into a pocket. It moves."

"I'll run where Shi says," said Shen Lingyue. "I've been doing what he says for a year. I'm good at it."

They went in.

Not all together. The dead went first, because the dead could not be seen. Five hundred and fifty-one of them, banked, in the Banked Field, walking out across the pale ground of the empty field in their rows of nine, between the faint rings where the columns had stood, toward the triangle. Old Seven at the front. Adjutant Xu at the top of the Lattice, humming the plan down it in a note no living ear could hear.

The two stepping lamps did not turn toward them. Did not flinch. Did not know they were there.

The living went second, in the pocket.

He did not remember most of it afterward. He remembered Mo Shi's voice, low and continuous, counting. Now. Left. Six meters. Now. He remembered the fold: a lurch, a smear of soft gold light, and the pale ground a different place under his feet. He remembered Mo Xing's nose starting to bleed on the eleventh fold and not stopping. He remembered Lingyue running, beside the pocket, never in it, always half a step behind Mo Shi's voice, with her grandfather's sword in her hand and her face set like stone.

And he remembered the beams.

He could feel them now. Not see them. Feel them, through the Field: two great slow presences sweeping round, one slice at a time, three degrees, still, three degrees, still, from two blank faces a kilometer apart. Every slice that passed near him he felt the way you feel a draft from an open door. Every time the pocket jumped and Mo Xing folded him into the new one, there was a fraction of a second where he was outside it, and something glanced.

His head turned. Three degrees. Once.

It saw you. One slice.

It learned your name.

That is all. Keep going.

They reached the near point of the triangle in eleven minutes and forty folds.

The holding walker stood above them, nine meters of woven silver-white thread, with its blank face tipped up and forward and pointed at a single column fifty meters away. It did not move. It did not see them. Under its feet, the pale ground was scored with faint lines, as if it had been standing there so long that it had begun to grow roots.

Old Seven was already there.

He stood at the foot of the walker in the blind, with his spear in his one finger and his thumb, and his gray flame banked to a single dull red point, looking up at it. Behind him, the Spear Corps in their rows, three hundred rusted spears grounded on the pale ground. Nobody had told them to stop. They had stopped because he had.

He says: the first one.

He says: I asked for it.

"Lingyue," said Yuanshu.

She was beside him in the pocket, breathing hard, with her grandfather's sword lit pale in her hand. She looked up at the walker. Then she let a drop of silver-gray fire run down the blade to the point, and fall, and it fell onto the rusted head of Old Seven's spear and ran down the shaft into his hand, and his spear lit.

Old Seven stepped back once, onto his heels.

And threw.

It went into the walker's leg, a meter above the ground, where the threads were thickest, and stopped there, quivering, with pale fire running off it in every direction.

The walker did not turn its face. It could not; its lamp was holding Zhuo. But its whole long woven body flinched, all at once, from the feet to the blank face, the way a man flinches who has been stung by something he cannot see.

Where the spear had gone in, the threads had parted. Not many. A handful. A small dark hole in the silver-white weave, smoking faintly at the edges, the way a thread had smoked over Bai Lu on the black glass a long time ago when Lingyue cut it.

And then, while they watched, the threads around the hole reached across it, and knotted, and closed it.

...it heals.

Like the scout. It weaves itself back.

Pale fire cuts threads. One at a time. A spear cuts a handful.

There are millions.

Above them, the walker's blank face did not move. It stayed pointed at Major Zhuo's column, straight up, holding.

But the column moved.

He saw it. Fifty meters away, on the near edge of the cluster, beside three empty rings, the gold column around a thin tired man in winter whites shivered, the whole length of it, the way a candle flame shivers when a door opens. And then, very slowly, an inch at a time, it began to rise off the pale ground.

It has decided.

It is lifting him.
