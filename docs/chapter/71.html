Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 71Grid Nine

czverse9 min read

It took them nineteen more days to reach the edge of the world.

Nineteen days north across country that no map had a name for. The black glass gave way to gray rock, and the gray rock gave way to ice, old blue ice in long frozen waves like a sea that had stopped mid-storm. The days shortened. The cold deepened until breath froze on scarves and eyelashes stuck together in the mornings and Bai Lu checked everyone's fingers twice a day instead of once.

And behind them, all the way, came the dead.

Nineteen thousand four hundred and two. Strung out across the ice for kilometers in a long loose column, gray bones in gray rags, walking at the pace of the living. Not in rows. Not in step. Just walking. Sometimes two of them would walk side by side for a whole day, and Yuanshu would see a skeletal hand reach out and rest, briefly, on another's shoulder.

They did not need to eat or sleep. But every night, when the living made camp, the column stopped too. And sat down on the ice around the camp in a great loose ring, facing outward, a kilometer across. Keeping watch. Nineteen thousand of the dead around a single small fire, under the northern stars.

"They like it," said Jiang Lan, from the staff, on the third night. "Stopping. Having somewhere to stop."

And every day, the ring in the sky grew.

On the first day past the white stone, it had been a faint pale circle on the northern horizon that you could only see if you knew where to look. By the tenth day, it was the size of the moon. By the fifteenth, it was enormous, a vast pale halo hanging over the northern quarter of the sky, so big that you had to turn your head to see both edges at once.

It did not look down at them. Yuanshu could feel that. Its attention was somewhere else. On something inside itself.

It is still looking at the gate.

At the staff. At the place the staff could open.

But it does not know where the staff is. Not exactly. The staff is dead, Yuanshu. Jiang Lan is dead. The only living thing touching it is you.

And you have been very quiet for nineteen days.

He had. He had been quieter than he had ever been in his life. Every day. All day. Walking across the ice behind Yan Shuang with the white staff in his hand, thinking about his feet, about the cold, about nothing.

It was exhausting. It was the hardest thing he had ever done.

And Seed counted every night.

Twenty-six hours.

Twenty-four.

Twenty-one.

Grid Nine stood on a ridge of black rock at the very end of the ice.

They came up the last long slope of frozen waves on the nineteenth afternoon, with the wind screaming in their faces and the light already going, and there it was, silhouetted against the pale enormous ring in the sky. A low concrete blockhouse, squat and windowless, crusted with rime. A radio mast on its roof, leaning. And on a pole beside the door, frozen stiff in the wind like a sheet of tin, the white flag of the Northern Union.

Beyond it, the ridge dropped away, and there was nothing.

Not ice. Not rock. Nothing Yuanshu could see. Just a gray haze, a kind of dim shimmering murk, like heat over a road but cold, filling the whole world north of the ridge from horizon to horizon, and rising up out of it into the sky toward the vast pale ring.

Sergeant Vera Sokolova stopped at the bottom of the last slope.

She stood very still in the wind, looking up at the blockhouse, with her hands at her sides.

Nobody said anything to her. After a while, Yan Shuang walked over and stood beside her. Not touching. Just there.

Then Sokolova walked up the slope alone, and put her shoulder to the frozen door of Grid Nine, and pushed it open.

Inside, it was exactly as she had left it.

A common room. A stove, cold. A long table with chairs pushed back from it at odd angles, as if people had stood up from it in a hurry. Cards on the table, face up, frozen to the wood. A tin mug. A guitar with two strings, leaning against the wall in the corner. Bunks along the back, forty-one of them, in three tiers, with the blankets still on them.

And the ice.

Not on the walls. In the shapes. Where forty-one young men had been standing when the sound came, the frost on the concrete floor was different. Darker. In the outlines of boots. Forty-one pairs. Some facing the door. Some facing the window slit. Some in the middle of the room, close together, as if they had turned toward each other at the last moment.

"We cut them out," said Sokolova. Her voice was very flat. "From the ice. One by one. It took four days. The Marshal did the last ones himself." She looked at the outlines on the floor. "Nobody has been here since."

Yuanshu stood in the doorway with the staff and did not go in. Old Seven stood behind him.

There is nothing here.

No residue. No echo. Nothing.

They were not killed here, Yuanshu. They were harvested. All at once. The bodies died. The rest went up.

There is nothing left in this room to ask.

Sokolova walked to the back of the room, to the bunks.

She went along them slowly, reading the names stenciled on the frames. Then she stopped at a bottom bunk at the end of the second tier, and stood there for a long moment, and knelt down.

BELOV, A.

There was a box under the bunk. A small wooden footlocker, the regulation kind, with a padlock that had never been locked. Sokolova opened it with one gloved hand.

Socks. A spare shirt. A razor. A paperback with its cover torn off. A photograph, frozen to the bottom of the box: a thin woman in a headscarf standing in front of a wooden house, squinting into the sun, not smiling, holding a cat.

And a notebook. School-sized. Cheap. Its cover drawn on in ballpoint pen: a rocket ship, a dog, the word МАМА in careful capitals.

Sokolova picked it up. Turned the pages with her gloves. Stopped at the last one.

She read it. Then she read it again. Then she held it out, without looking up, toward the doorway.

Yuanshu came in and took it.

He couldn't read Northern. He held the page up in the fading light from the door, and Seed read it for him, quietly, in his head. A letter. Not finished. In the cramped, careful handwriting of a nineteen-year-old who had been taught to write neatly by someone who cared.

Mama,

It's very cold here and the food is terrible. Don't worry. I have the scarf.

There's something in the sky. The sergeant says it's an atmospheric effect. Pasha says it's aliens. I think it's beautiful. It's like a ring someone dropped. At night it shines.

Some of the men are acting strange. They stand at the window and look at it for hours. The sergeant says it's cabin fever. I don't know.

I had a dream last night that someone was asking me questions. Over and over. What do I like. What am I afraid of. Who do I love. I told them about you. I told them about the cat and the garden and the way you hum when you cook. I don't know why I'm telling you this.

Mama, if anything happens, I want you to know

That was all. The line stopped.

Yuanshu held the notebook for a long time.

"His mother said yes," he said at last. "In the Ledger. She said he could come home."

"I know," said Sokolova. "I carried her letter." She stood up from beside the bunk. Her face did not change, but her voice did, very slightly. "I would like to take this to her. When we go back. If we go back." She looked at the frost outlines on the floor. "I would like to bring her something that is not ice."

He gave her the notebook.

She put it inside her coat, against her chest, and buttoned the coat over it.

They camped in the lee of the blockhouse that night. Not inside. Nobody wanted to sleep inside.

The dead sat down on the ice in their great ring around the ridge. The fire was small and smoky. The wind came over the edge of the world and howled through the leaning radio mast like someone blowing across the mouth of a bottle.

Yuanshu went to the edge of the ridge alone, with Old Seven a few paces behind, and looked north.

The gray haze filled everything. It moved. Slowly. Shimmering. And now, in the dark, he could see that it was not haze at all.

It was threads.

Billions of them. Silver. Fine as hair. Coming from everywhere. From the east and the west and the south, from beyond every horizon, drifting in over the ice and the rock and the frozen sea, so many and so fine that together they looked like fog. Threads from Lingzhou. From the Northern Union. From Yamato and the Atlantic Federation and the Southern Compact. From every hollow on Blue Star, every turned head, every listening ear.

And they were all going to the same place.

North. Down into the murk below the ridge. And somewhere down there, in the dark, they came together, and twisted, and wound around each other, and rose.

A column.

He could just see it, far off, in the starlight. A single vast pillar of silver light, kilometers wide, rising straight up out of the haze into the night sky like the trunk of a tree made of moonlight. Up and up and up. Thinning as it went, until, at the very top, impossibly high, it touched the center of the enormous pale ring.

The stalk.

Every thread on Blue Star, wound together.

That is the way up, Yuanshu.

That is the first door.

"Yes," said Jiang Lan quietly, from the staff in his hand. "That's where Xuanming was going. Before he got too tired."

Behind him, footsteps on the frozen rock.

Su Mingli came and stood beside him at the edge of the ridge. She looked at the column of silver light for a long time.

"Two days' walk," said Yan Shuang's voice, from further back. "Maybe three. Down there." She had come up too, quietly, her unlit cigarette in her mouth. "The trucks never went past this ridge. Not once. Not in eleven years. The company had a rule." She looked at the haze. "Whatever was down there, they brought the hollows up to the edge, and something came and took them, and they drove home."

"What came?" said Su Mingli.

Yan Shuang was silent for a long moment.

"I never looked," she said. "That was the other rule."

Nineteen hours.

The pulse.

I counted it just now.

At this rate, Yuanshu, the lens opens fully in sixty-one days.

And it has stopped looking at the gate.

He went cold.

Where's it looking?

A long pause. The wind howled through the mast.

Down.

At the stalk.

At the bottom of the stalk.

At something that just arrived there.

Far below them, in the gray murk at the foot of the column of silver light, so far away it was barely a sound at all, something began to wail.

Not one voice. Many.

And then, from the east, another. And from the west, another. Rising up out of the haze from three directions at once, long and slow and wavering, like horns across water, like a hundred thousand throats screaming through closed mouths.

Three of them.

Gathering at the foot of the stalk.
