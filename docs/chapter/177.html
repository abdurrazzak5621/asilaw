Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 177Three Lamps

czverse9 min read

"The head is the lamp," said Seed. "That is what we learned in the round chamber. That is the only thing we know for certain about anything that walks down from above the sky."

It said it out loud. Through the old speaker Fat Tan had wired into the wall of the banking hall a year ago so that Seed could talk to the room without Yuanshu repeating everything, which Seed almost never used, because it said its voice sounded strange coming out of a box. It used it now. Twelve people and five hundred and fifty-one dead were in the room, and it wanted all of them to hear the same words at the same time.

They were all there. Yuanshu on the vault steps. Lingyue beside him with both swords. Qin Hao on Fat Tan's desk with his notebook. The Mo twins on the floor. Bai Lu with her medical bag. Hu Dabao, with his left arm still in a sling and a face that dared anyone to tell him he was not coming. Kwan with her laptop. Su Mingli standing by the bronze door with her arms folded, who had said no, flatly, the night before, because the city was hers and the walkers were not, and who had come anyway to hear the plan, because she said she would like to know what she was not going to be doing. And down the long marble floor of the banking hall, in their rows, the Spear Corps, the forty from Grid Nine in their white, and two hundred and eleven of the cemetery companies, with Old Seven and Adjutant Xu at the front.

On the long white wall of names, Kwan had projected the round chamber.

Specimen Zero.

It read the world the way a lighthouse reads the sea. A beam. Three degrees wide. It read one slice, then turned its head, and read the next. A hundred and twenty slices all the way round.

Between every slice, while its head was turning, it was blind. For zero point six two seconds.

It could not see the dead. Ever. The dead were not in its beam. They walked straight up to it and it did not know they were there.

It could see the living. Every slice. Every living mind it passed over turned its head three degrees toward it, and the scout learned something.

And when it stopped stepping, and held its beam on one mind, straight up, that mind was taken.

"We killed it," said Qin Hao, from the desk. "With all that. In the round chamber. It took us all day and a King and Lu."

Yes.

There was one of it. In a cage. With a wound in its chest that a seed had been healing for thirty-one years. With its face held down by a King of Gravity so it could not point its lamp.

There will be three of them. In their own field. Healthy. With their faces free.

Nobody in the banking hall said anything.

Here is what I think. It is a guess. I am going to tell you why I think it.

The keeper says the walkers are tall, they walk, they do not ask, they do not speak. That is the scout.

The keeper says one of them walked down the sky thirty-one years ago. That was the scout. It was a walker.

So I think the walkers are scouts. Or the scout was a walker. The same kind of thing. Three lamps, walking between the columns, reading the field one slice at a time, looking for the oldest and the brightest.

If I am right, then three things are true.

One. They cannot see the dead. The Spear Corps, the forty, the two hundred and eleven, Old Seven, Adjutant Xu. You can walk up to them.

Two. They can see us. The living. Yuanshu, Lingyue, Qin Hao, the twins, Bai Lu, Dabao. Every slice that passes over us, they learn something. If one of them stops and holds its beam on one of us, that one is taken.

Three. There are three lamps. Not one.

Kwan changed the picture on the wall.

Three circles, overlapping. Three beams, sweeping round, each one three degrees wide, each one stepping, each one blind for 0.62 seconds between steps.

"With one," she said, "the blind gap is a line. A sliver. Mo Shi can call it. With three, the beams cross." She put her hand on the wall where the circles overlapped. "When one's blind, another's looking. Almost everywhere. Almost all the time."

"Almost," said Yuanshu.

Almost.

I ran it. Three lamps, stepping at the rate the scout stepped, from three places in the field. I do not know where they will be. So I ran it from every place. Eleven thousand times.

Here is what I found.

Kwan changed the picture.

The three circles were still there. The three beams. But in the middle of them, where they overlapped, there was something else now. A small dark shape. Not a line. A pocket. Irregular, like a drop of ink on wet paper, bending and shifting as the beams swept round. Getting bigger. Smaller. Moving.

When three lamps sweep, their blind gaps do not line up. Most of the time, at least one of them is looking at any place.

But not every place. There is always somewhere, for a moment, where all three are blind at once. A pocket. It moves. It changes shape. It is never in the same place twice.

It is about four meters across. Sometimes six. Sometimes two.

It lasts, on average, one point one seconds. Then it moves.

If you are standing in the pocket, no lamp can see you.

"And if you're standing a meter outside it?"

Then one of them sees you. One slice. Three degrees. Your head turns. It learns something.

And if it learns enough, it stops stepping, and holds.

"So we stay in the pocket," said Lingyue.

"Six of us," said Mo Xing, from the floor. "In a pocket two meters wide that moves every second."

Yes.

Mo Shi can see it three seconds ahead. He saw the scout's gap. He will see this.

And Mo Xing can fold. Two meters. Four. A person at a time. Faster than anyone on Blue Star.

Mo Shi calls where the pocket will be. Mo Xing folds us into it. Before it moves, Mo Shi calls the next one. Mo Xing folds us again.

Over and over. For as long as it takes.

Mo Xing stared at the picture on the wall. At the small dark pocket, bending and shifting in the middle of three sweeping beams.

"How long will it take?" he said.

I do not know.

"Then how many folds?"

...I do not know that either. Hundreds. Perhaps more.

Mo Xing's nose had started to bleed. Not from folding. From thinking about it. He wiped it on his sleeve and did not look away from the wall.

"I've never done more than forty in a day," he said.

"You did twenty-three on a ship in the Southern Compact with your nose already bleeding," said his brother beside him, without opening his eyes. "And a hundred and fifty-one on a beach in the dark. I counted."

"That was two days."

"Then you've done a hundred and seventy-four in two days," said Mo Shi. "Stop complaining."

"And while we're dancing around in the pocket," said Qin Hao. "What's killing them?"

The dead.

They cannot see the dead. The dead walk up to them. The way they walked up to the scout.

But the scout was in a cage with its face held down, and Old Seven's spear went into the blind gap between slices, in zero point six two seconds, and even then it took the whole day.

These will be free. Walking. Looking for us. Three of them, covering each other's gaps.

The dead can reach them. I do not know if the dead can hurt them. Rusted spears. Pale fire. I do not know what they are made of.

"Then we find out," said Old Seven.

He did not say it. Seed said it. But it was the first time Yuanshu had ever heard Seed say Old Seven said without the He says in front of it; the words came out of the speaker in the wall exactly as the old soldier meant them, flat and plain, and every dead soldier in the banking hall turned their skull toward him.

...he says that is what scouts are for. You find out what the enemy is made of by hitting it and seeing what breaks.

He says he would like the first one.

Su Mingli had not moved from the bronze door the whole time.

When Seed finished, she unfolded her arms, and walked down the marble floor between the rows of the dead to the wall, and looked at the picture on it. Three circles. Three beams. A small dark pocket in the middle, bending and shifting.

"In the round chamber," she said, "I held its face down until it died."

Nobody said anything.

"With gravity. From directly above. A weight so precise it touched nothing else." She looked at the three circles. "It couldn't point its lamp. That was the whole fight. Everything else was just killing something blind."

"You said no," said Yuanshu.

"I said no to going up." She did not look at him. "The city is mine. I'm not leaving it while you're above the sky with half the people who know how to defend it." She put one hand flat on the projected wall, over the three lamps. "I didn't say no to helping."

"You can't hold three faces down from the ground."

"No," said Su Mingli. "I can't." She took her hand off the wall. "But I can teach the girl."

Everyone turned and looked at Lingyue.

Lingyue had gone very still.

"Light runs downhill," said Su Mingli, flatly. "I heard. Everyone heard. You made it heavy. In the Field, your light has weight." She looked at Lingyue across the banking hall. "Gravity is just weight with an opinion about where it wants to go. You've got the weight. I've got the opinion." She folded her arms again. "You've got six days. I've got four of them free. That's enough to teach you how to put a heavy light on top of a lamp and hold it there."

"On three lamps?"

"No," said Su Mingli. "On one. You hold one face down. The other two, you deal with the old-fashioned way." She glanced at Seed's picture on the wall. "Two lamps, the pocket's bigger. A lot bigger. I ran that myself, in my head, just now, while your machine was talking. It's eleven meters with two. And it lasts four seconds."

...she is right.

I did not run two. I only ran three. I assumed three.

With two lamps: the pocket is between nine and fourteen meters. It lasts between three and five seconds.

I did not count Su Mingli either. I am going to stop saying I did not count people. I am going to start asking them first.

Lingyue looked at the King of Gravity across the long marble floor of the old banking hall, with both swords on her back and her split lip and the look she had brought home from Mount Qingyun.

"Four days," she said.

"Four days," said Su Mingli. "Starting now. Bring both swords. You'll break one."
