Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 191The Wave

czverse9 min read

The first beam reached the north edge of Lingzhou at 21:34, four minutes later than Seed had said, and Xiaoyu said "Now," and the north edge of Lingzhou went quiet.

He felt it from the wall. Everyone on the wall felt it. A hush that started at the northern districts, in the old housing blocks under the wall and the market streets behind them, and spread south in a long curved band the width of a beam, following it, the way a shadow follows a cloud across a field. Thirty-one voices on thirty-one channels, calling their streets. Fifty thousand people in the dark, in their kitchens and bedrooms and stairwells, closing their eyes. Thinking about rice. The sound of the sea. Nothing.

The beam passed over them.

Not one head turned.

Ninety-six percent.

Better than the drill. They are frightened. Frightened people try harder.

The beam found nothing to hold. It stepped. Three degrees. Into the next slice.

"Now," said Xiaoyu, on every speaker in the next district. And the next slice went quiet. And the one behind it breathed.

He had nine days of drills in his head and none of them had prepared him for what it was like to watch it work.

It was like watching the sea breathe. Not one wave: eleven. Eleven beams sweeping across the city from eleven walkers a kilometer apart, coming south across the black glass toward the wall. Each beam three degrees wide, stepping. Each step a slice of the city that went quiet the instant before the beam touched it and breathed the instant after it passed. Eleven bands of silence crossing and recrossing Lingzhou in the dark, overlapping, separating, curving, like the ripples of eleven stones thrown into a pond at once.

And under all of it, thirty-one voices and one more, calling, three seconds ahead.

Mo Shi sat cross-legged on the marble floor of the old banking hall in the middle of the chalk city with his eyes shut and both hands flat on the stone, and called the beams. Not imaginary ones now. Real ones. Eleven of them. He had never called more than three at once in the field. Sweat was running down his face. His lips were moving very fast.

Xiaoyu sat beside him with her headphones on and the stopwatch in one hand and the whistle hanging from her neck, and turned his calls into words.

"North Market, now. Breathe. Old Quarter east, now. Tram Yards, now. North Market, breathe. River Gate, now..."

Thirty-one voices on the floor around them echoed her, each to their own district.

And the city danced with the lamps.

Fourteen hundred.

The ones who cannot be quiet. They are lit. Every time a beam passes over a slice with one of them in it, they are the only bright thing in the slice.

And beside every one of them, someone is sitting.

Watch.

He watched through the Field. An old woman in a dark room in the old quarter, dying, her mind bright and frightened and full of her mother's face. The beam came. And on the end of her bed, a King in white robes with her eyes shut, sitting in the old woman's mind beside her, very still, being quiet with her. And the old woman's mind, which could not be quiet on its own, settled against Ren's the way a child settles against someone warm, and dimmed.

Not dark. Dimmed.

The beam passed. It did not stop.

It did not hold.

She was not the brightest thing it could see. Not quite. Ren made her a little less bright, and it was enough.

Thirty-nine more, near Ren. Six hundred more, across the city, with the Quiet sitting beside them.

Not all of them will be enough. But most.

At 23:10, the walkers reached the wall.

They came out of the dark across the last kilometer of black glass in a line, nine meters tall, woven, silver-white in the floodlights from the battlements, with their blank faces level, stepping, three degrees, still. They did not slow down for the wall. The first one reached it at Crossing Six, east of Sector Nine, and stopped, and stood with its long woven legs against the old stone and its blank face level with the top of the parapet, looking over it into the city.

The First Legion was on the parapet in front of it. Eighteen thousand gray points banked to dull red. It did not see them. It could not.

Old Seven was already moving.

The plan had a name in Seed's head and no name anywhere else. Nine days in the old banking hall around the chalk city with Su Mingli and Lingyue and Qin Hao and Orlov, who had arrived with Sokolova and the Second Ice Division on the seventh day in a train that steamed with frost, and Ren on the phone from the old quarter. Seed called it the knot. Nobody else called it anything.

The walkers' threads ran to a knot behind the face. Qin Hao's white fire could undo a knot. Qin Hao could do perhaps two before he was empty. There were eleven walkers.

Pale fire could cut threads. A handful at a time. The knot pulled the rest across to close the cut.

Unless the cuts came all at once.

Two hundred and seventy-seven spears.

Intent-sharing. Every spear in the Spear Corps striking one place at one moment, the way they struck one seam on the Bridge-Breaker. Old Seven's Spear Intent through every one of them at once.

Two hundred and seventy-seven cuts in the same instant, into the knot, all lit pale.

The knot cannot pull across two hundred and seventy-seven cuts at once. I think. I have run it four thousand times on a model of a walker I built from two dead ones in a field.

I am seventy-three percent sure.

Seventy-three percent. With eleven walkers.

Su Mingli lifted her hand.

She was standing on the Sector Nine battlements beside him, in the floodlights, with her coat open in the cold and her face perfectly still. She raised the hand she had used to lift forty thousand tonnes over Tannery Square, and closed it, and the first walker's blank face, looking over the parapet at Crossing Six, went down.

Not gently. Gravity came down on it like a hand on the back of a neck, a weight so precise it touched nothing else on the wall, and the smooth gray-white curve tipped forward past level, past forty-five degrees, until it was pointed straight down at the old stone of the wall between its own long feet, and held.

Its beam swung down with it. Off the city. Onto the stone.

Blind.

And Old Seven went up its back.

He did not climb. He was a skeleton two meters tall with one finger and a thumb on his spear hand. He could not climb nine meters of woven thread. The Spear Corps climbed for him. Two hundred and seventy-seven dead soldiers of the Eastern Line went up the walker's legs and body the way men go up a siege tower, rows of them, banked, unseen, finding handholds in the weave, standing on each other's shoulders, until there was a column of skeletons from the foot of the wall to the back of the walker's head. And Old Seven went up the column of his men like a man going up a staircase, with his spear, and stood on the shoulders of the last of them, at the back of the blank face, in front of the knot.

The Spear Corps raised their spears.

Lingyue's light fell on them.

She was on the watchtower above Crossing Six with her grandfather's sword lit and raised, and she did not shine it. She let it fall. Heavy. A rain of silver-gray drops off the point of a King's blade, falling downhill through the Field onto two hundred and seventy-seven rusted spearheads at once, so that every one of them lit pale.

Intent-sharing.

Old Seven, now.

Old Seven struck.

And two hundred and seventy-seven spears struck with him, in the same instant, from below and beside and behind, all of them aimed by his intent through the line of the Spear Corps the way three hundred had been aimed at one seam on the Bridge-Breaker. Into the knot. Pale fire on every point.

The knot did not pull across.

It could not. There were too many cuts, all at once, in every direction. It pulled at one and the next was already open, and the next, and the next. For a moment it held, a tight bright tangle of silver-white thread behind a blank face with two hundred and seventy-eight spears in it.

Then it came undone.

The walker came apart from the head down, the way the ones in the field had, in a long slow fall of glittering silk over the north wall of Lingzhou. The Spear Corps rode it down. When the loose threads lashed, blind, the way they had lashed in the field, the dead were already letting go and dropping, row by row, to the wall and the glass, because Old Seven had told them to the instant the knot gave.

Three of them did not drop fast enough.

Three.

Unwoven. Gone.

Two hundred and seventy-four.

On the battlements, Su Mingli opened her hand. Her face was gray.

"One," she said.

One. Ten left.

And Yuanshu.

They felt it.

He felt it too.

All along the line, a kilometer apart, from the Sector Nine watchtower to the river gate, ten walkers standing against the north wall of Lingzhou with their blank faces looking over the parapet into the dark city. When the first one came apart, every one of them stopped stepping.

Not holding. Stopped. The way the walker in the field had stopped when its companion came apart. All ten blank faces, at once, turning. Not toward the falling silk. Toward the city.

And then they began to step again.

But not the same way.

...they have changed.

They are not stepping three degrees, still, three degrees, still. Not in order. Not round.

They are stepping at random. Left. Right. Back. Two slices in one direction, then four in the other. Every walker differently.

They have noticed the wave.

In the old banking hall, on the floor of the chalk city, Mo Shi made a sound.

"I can't," he said. His eyes were still shut. His hands were flat on the marble. "I can see three seconds ahead. But I can only see where they're going to step, not why. When they went round, I could see the pattern. Now there isn't one." His voice cracked. "Xiaoyu. I can call them. One at a time. Three seconds. But I can't call them for thirty-one districts at once if they're all going different ways."

Xiaoyu took off her headphones.

She looked at the chalk city on the floor. At thirty-one people with microphones around it, waiting. At ten beams on Seed's projection on the wall, sweeping across Lingzhou now in ten different, random, broken directions, no longer a wave, no longer anything a city could follow.

At the bands of silence, falling apart.

Eighty-one percent.

Seventy-four.

They are finding people. Not holding yet. Glancing. One slice. Learning.

Sixty-eight.

Zhou Xiaoyu put her headphones back on.

"Then don't call districts," she said. "Call me. Just me. Every step. Every walker. As fast as you see it." She picked up the whistle. "And I'll call the city."

"You can't. Ten walkers. Random. Thirty-one districts. Every three seconds. Nobody can..."

"I did Sixty Seconds," said Xiaoyu. "I've been doing this for nine days. I know where every street is. I drew them." She put the whistle in her teeth. Took it out. "Shi. Call."
