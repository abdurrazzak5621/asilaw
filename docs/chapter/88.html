Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 88Pale Fire

czverse9 min read

The arm was Sergeant Huo's idea.

He brought it up himself, the second night back in Lingzhou, in the courtyard behind the Ossuary, where the seventy-two of the Third Battalion who had walked up the stalk and down again now stood their watch in rows among the potted plants Fat Tan kept insisting on buying. Old Seven was sitting on the edge of the dry fountain in the middle of the courtyard, with his spear across his knees and his one hand resting on it, looking at nothing.

Sergeant Huo walked across the courtyard and stood in front of his captain.

Then he held out his left arm.

The one with three fingers.

Yuanshu did not understand at first. He was sitting on the back steps of the Ossuary with the white staff, writing names. Jiang Lan was dictating. Liu Mingde. Ferryman. Raised at the river crossing. Said thank you. Went quiet in the first week.

He looked up.

Sergeant Huo stood in front of Old Seven in the lamplight with his left arm held out, bones and rags of gray greatcoat, three fingers on the hand, palm up. Steady.

Old Seven looked at it.

He is offering it.

His arm. To his captain.

He says: you lost yours carrying a child out of a fire. I lost two fingers thirty-one years ago carrying ammunition up the eastern wall, and I never did anything with the rest of it.

He says: take it. I would like it to be useful.

Yuanshu put down his pen.

He walked across the courtyard. He stopped beside them. The old spear captain sitting on the edge of the dry fountain. The sergeant standing in front of him with his arm out.

"Sergeant Huo," he said quietly. "Do you know what you're asking? If I take your arm, it doesn't come back. You'll have one."

He says: yes. He says he has walked for thirty-one years with two arms and never needed both.

He says his captain needs his spear arm and his other arm.

He says: please.

Yuanshu turned to Old Seven.

"Captain Qi," he said. "Your sergeant is offering you his arm. It's his choice to give. It's yours whether to take it." He paused. "Do you want it?"

Old Seven looked up at his sergeant. At the three-fingered hand, held out in the lamplight. At the gray flame in Huo's chest, small and steady.

For a long time, he did not move.

Then the old soldier lifted his one bony hand off his spear, and reached out, and took his sergeant's hand.

Not to take the arm. To hold it. The way he had held it in the river of the dead, the first night, when Huo said I'm so tired.

And held it for a long time.

Then he nodded. Once.

It took Yuanshu an hour. He had never done anything like it.

He knelt in the lamplight on the stones of the courtyard with the white staff beside him and his hands on two of the dead, and went quiet, all the way down, and asked the Death Law, very gently, to do something it had never been asked to do. Not to raise. Not to release. To give.

Bone to bone. Ember to ember. A soldier's arm, and everything it remembered, passing from one old soldier to another.

Seed held the shape of it. Jiang Lan, from the staff, gave dry instructions. Not like that. The joint is wrong. Did nobody teach you anatomy? And the seventy-one other soldiers of the Third Battalion stood in their rows among Fat Tan's potted plants, perfectly still, watching.

Seed had run it first, in the quiet: four thousand attempts, bone to bone. In most of them the two embers fought over the arm like two dogs over a coat. In every one that worked, Huo let go first. So Yuanshu asked him to, and Huo did.

When it was done, Old Seven stood up.

Two arms. One his own, long and white and old. One a little shorter, a little grayer, with three fingers on the hand, and on those three fingers, faint under the dust, a pattern of small white scars from a spear shaft.

He looked down at it. He flexed the three fingers. Slowly. Open. Closed.

Then he picked up his spear in both hands.

And Sergeant Huo, with one arm, came to attention in front of him, and saluted, with the hand he had left.

New technique: Graft. Bone to bone, between two of the dead who both say yes.

Death Law: 26.9% → 27.5%.

He says it fits.

He says it was always going to fit. He spent thirty years learning to hold a spear the way his captain taught him.

He says the arm already knew how.

Lingyue came to collect her spar on the third night.

She found him on the wall. Sector Nine. Where the Academy's training grounds ran behind the battlements and the Wastes ran away in front of them, black glass under the stars, toward a small white ring hanging on the northern horizon like a ring someone dropped.

He had been sitting there for an hour. Looking north. Past the small white ring. Higher. Where the sky went dark, and four faint gray specks turned in the black, and one of them was very slightly bigger than it had been last night.

"You're in my spot," said Shen Lingyue.

"You said that last time."

"It was true last time too." She sat down on the parapet beside him, with her sword across her knees. "Two hundred and eleven days."

"I know."

"Everyone's asking everyone. Su Mingli has a list. The Council has a list. Sister Ning has a list. Fat Tan has a list of the lists." She ran her thumb along the flat of her sword. "Your mother asked me yesterday. In the hospital. With your sister as a witness. Your sister was very serious about it. She had a clipboard."

"What did you say?"

"What do you think I said?" She didn't look at him. "I'm going."

She stood up.

"Spar," she said. "You promised. That draw doesn't count."

He stood up. He picked up the training spear, the one leaning against the battlements, and set his feet.

"No," said Lingyue. "Not that one."

She nodded at the white staff, leaning against the parapet where he'd left it.

He looked at it. Then at her.

"Don't you dare," said Jiang Lan, from the small skull. "I am not a spear. I am a schoolteacher. I have been a schoolteacher alive and dead for longer than your grandmother, and I have never once been used to hit anyone."

"Not to hit," said Lingyue. "To hold." She drew her sword. "The spire. In the bay. My light cut its roots. Qin Hao's fire burned them. But neither of us could touch the spire itself, because it was full of people, and every time we hurt it, it burned them faster." She held the sword up. It was not lit. "And out there, there's no one to burn. Just it. And you. And the dead." She looked at him. "And my light burns the dead. I spent four hours in a canyon bending it around your skeletons. I spent three hours in a river cutting threads with the tip of my sword so I wouldn't touch a single bone." Her voice was very level. "Out there, it can see the dead. It'll go for them. Old Seven. Huo. Zhao. Lin. And they'll be standing right next to me." She lifted her chin. "I need a light that burns it and not them. I've been thinking about it for two weeks. I think I know how. I need you."

She lit the sword.

White-gold. Bright as a strip of noon. The light poured off the blade and lit the battlements and the stones and the dark plain beyond.

"Put the staff against the blade," she said. "And ask the Death Law to go into it. Not to raise anything. Just to... be there. In the light. The way the dead are there, in the river. Quiet."

"This is a terrible idea," said Jiang Lan.

"Probably," said Yuanshu.

He picked up the staff. He went quiet. He reached out, very carefully, and laid the small white skull at its top against the flat of the burning blade.

And asked.

For one second, nothing.

Then the light changed.

The white-gold went out of it. Not dark. Not dim. Pale. A soft, cold, silvery-gray, like moonlight on frost, like the flame in Old Seven's chest, like the glow inside the staff. It ran up the blade from the point where the staff touched it, and spread, and filled the sword from hilt to tip. And it did not blaze. It did not burn the eyes. It just glowed, very steady, very quiet, like a candle in a still room.

Lingyue stared at it.

"Oh," she said.

It is still light.

It is still her Light Law. All of it. I can feel it. As hot as before. Hotter.

But it is wearing the Death Law. Like a coat.

Yuanshu. I think the dead will not feel it as fire.

I think the dead will feel it as one of their own.

From the shadow of the stairs at the end of the walkway, Old Seven stepped out.

He had been there the whole time, of course. He always was. Two meters ten of white bone, with two arms now, one of them a little grayer than the other, and his spear in both hands.

He walked toward them along the battlements. Toward the pale gray sword.

"Seven," said Lingyue sharply. "Wait. I don't know if..."

The old soldier did not wait. He walked up to the glowing blade, and stopped in front of it, and raised his three-fingered hand. Huo's hand.

And laid it flat against the burning sword.

Nothing happened.

The pale gray light lit his finger bones from inside, like a lamp shining through paper. It ran up his wrist. Into his arm. Into the gray flame in his chest, which brightened.

He did not burn.

He stood there with his hand on the sword, looking at it, his skull tilted, his flame burning steady and bright.

Then he took his hand away, and stepped back, and grounded his spear, and inclined his skull. Very slightly. To Lingyue.

He says it feels warm.

He says it feels like standing next to a campfire with your squad, on a cold night on the wall.

He says it is the first time any light has ever felt warm to him.

Lingyue stood very still on the battlements with the pale gray sword in her hand.

Then she turned it, slowly, in the starlight, watching the soft silver-gray fire run along the edge.

"Pale fire," she said quietly. "That's what we'll call it."

"I didn't agree to be a sword," said Jiang Lan.

"You're not a sword," said Lingyue. "You're a teacher. You just taught me something."

A long, dry silence from the staff.

"...hm," said Jiang Lan. "Well. That's all right then."

They sparred until dawn.

Not to win. To learn. Lingyue with the pale fire and Yuanshu with the staff against her blade, the two of them moving together along the battlements, finding how long the Death Law would stay in the light when the staff left it, how far the pale fire would reach, how hard she could swing before the silver went white-gold again. Old Seven watched. So did Sergeant Huo, with one arm. So did, after a while, the night sentries, and the Academy students on early training, and Principal Yue Changfeng in his tracksuit, standing at the bottom of the wall with his saber over his shoulder and his mouth slightly open.

At dawn, the sword held its pale fire for four minutes after the staff left it.

At dawn, Lingyue put him on his back on the battlements with the flat of her blade against his collarbone, and stood over him, breathing hard, with the sun coming up red over the Wastes behind her.

"I win," she said.

"That's not fair. I was holding a schoolteacher."

"I win," said Shen Lingyue again, and for the first time since he had known her, she did not try to stop herself smiling. "That one counts."

She held out her hand.

He took it. She pulled him up.

And they stood together on the wall at Sector Nine in the red dawn, looking north, past the small white ring, at four gray specks in the paling sky, and did not let go of each other's hands for a while.
