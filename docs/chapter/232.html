Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 232Nobody at the Top

czverse12 min read

The question from the Western Pass took eleven hours to come down.

It was not an important question. Sergeant Ma Dashan wanted to know whether the Fourth Company could have the spare lamp oil from the depot at the foot of the pass, because the nights up there were long and his men liked to see each other's faces when they said the names. Before, it would have come down the Lattice in a breath. Nine, and nine, and nine, and up to the top, and across, and down to the Ossuary, and Yuanshu would have said yes, obviously before the sergeant had finished asking.

It came in at four in the afternoon, three days after the bud, while he was sitting on the vault steps. It had left the pass at five in the morning.

It went round.

It went up through the Western Legion's knots, the way it always does. Up to where the top was. And there was nobody there to carry it across. So it went back down. And sideways. And up a different way. And found nobody. And down.

It went round the Lattice four hundred times before it found its way to us. Like a letter with the address torn off.

There are nine thousand others still going round.

He looked across the banking hall.

The chair stood where someone had carried it back down from the Sector Nine battlement, in its old place by the long white wall, under the lamp. A plain wooden chair with a cushion that someone, Fat Tan probably, had put on it a year ago because an old man had complained about his back, even though the old man was dead and did not have a back to complain about. Nobody had moved it. Nobody had sat in it. Twice in three days, he had seen Xiaoyu walk toward it with an armful of ledger paper, and stop, and put the paper on the floor instead.

"Who's going to carry it?" he said.

I do not know.

I think we should ask them.

"It'll take a week to ask them."

Yes.

I think that is the first thing they would want us to notice.

Lingyue could light a candle.

That was all. Bai Lu had come every morning and every evening and laid her hands on her and frowned, and on the third day she had stopped frowning and started saying weeks, which was better than what she had been saying on the first day, which was nothing. A Lord at Level 66 had burned everything she had in one breath on the north wall of Lingzhou, all of it, at once, the way nobody was supposed to. What was left was a girl in a narrow bed in the back room of the Ossuary with the window open to the courtyard and her grandfather's sword leaning against the wall where she could see it, gray under the eyes, who could hold up one finger and make a small pale flame stand on the end of it, the size of a grain of rice.

She did it every hour. He had watched her do it. She held it until her hand shook, and then she put it out, and looked at the place where it had been.

"Stop counting," she said, without turning her head. "I can hear you counting."

"I'm not."

"You are. Your face does it." She lay back on the pillow. Her hair was loose. There had been a red ribbon in it on the wall, from the dress from Mount Qingyun, and nobody had found it afterward, and nobody was going to. "Sit down. You're blocking the sun. Hush will complain."

On the sill, in the sun, Hush did not complain. It had its eyes closed and all its limbs out, the way it had every morning since the cut, and gold dust still on its legs that it would not let anyone brush off.

He sat on the edge of the bed.

She did not say anything for a while. Then she held up her finger and lit the grain of rice again and looked at it.

"I understand them now," she said.

"Who?"

"The Sixth." The little flame trembled. "We would like, for one day, to be very bright. I thought it was the saddest thing anyone ever said. On the island. I thought, they didn't get to have anything, so they wanted that." She watched the flame. "It wasn't sad. It was the best thing I ever did. On the wall. Everything at once. Nothing held back. Nothing careful." Her voice was very level. "I've been careful my whole life. Grandfather's granddaughter. Dawnbreaker. Don't waste it. Bend it, don't burn it." The flame went out. "For about three minutes, I wasn't careful at all. And it was the best I've ever felt."

He did not say anything. He did not trust what his voice would do.

"That's why it eats them," said Shen Lingyue. "Isn't it. The tree. That's why the brightest go first. It's not because they're stupid. It's because it feels like that."

...yes.

I think so.

I think that is what the Collector taught. Not that minds are bright. That being bright is the best thing a mind can feel.

She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him for the first time.

"Don't make that face," she said. "I'm not going to do it again. Not like that." She reached out and took his hand, the one with the ring on it, and held it on the blanket. "I just wanted to tell somebody. That I get it now. That I'm not going to pretend I don't."

"Okay," he said.

"Okay," she said. "Now go and fix your net. I can hear it buzzing."

They asked the dead on the fourth morning.

It was the first question they had sent down the Lattice since the cut that was about the Lattice itself, and it went slowly, round and round, the way everything went now. Seed wrote it with Xiaoyu at the long table under the lamp, in plain words, and told them first what would be done with it, the way Article Eleven said:

Adjutant Xu carried every question to the top and across. He is gone. Somebody has to be the knot at the top, or the questions go round forever. Who do you want? We will ask whoever you choose whether they want it. If they say no, we will ask you again.

I would like to send one more line.

"Go on."

The top knot is where the tip found us. It drank down the road of light to the top, and the top was one old man, and he tied it off. That is why the rest of you are still here.

Whoever is the top next will be the place that is found first, next time.

I think they should know that before they choose.

Xiaoyu looked at the sheet for a long moment. Then she wrote it down.

Old Seven said no before the question reached him.

He did not need the question to reach him. He stood at the bronze door of the Ossuary with his spear, and when Yuanshu came down the stairs on the fourth morning, he turned his skull, just a little, toward the empty chair by the wall, and then back to the street.

He says: no.

He says: he goes where the boy goes. A top knot sits in a chair.

He says: Xu Dehai sat in the chair because he was an adjutant, and adjutants sit at the top and carry, and captains go out ahead of the line. That is what each of them is for. He will not be the other thing.

He says: also, he does not like chairs.

Yuanshu stood on the bottom step and looked at the tall dead soldier in the doorway with the sun behind him.

"You went to the stone this morning," he said. "Before light."

Old Seven did not answer.

He says: somebody has to make sure the dumplings are not eaten by dogs.

"They were eaten by dogs on the first night."

He says: then somebody has to make sure the dogs know who they were for.

The answers began to come back on the sixth day, and they were not names.

Kwan sorted them. She sat at the long table in the banking hall with a ledger and a stack of index cards and Seed reading them to her one by one, as they came in off the Lattice, round and round and finally down, and she sorted them into piles, and by the evening of the seventh day there was one pile on the table that was taller than all the others put together.

It had started at the bottom of the sea.

The Watch of the Shelf. Off Kesse. Nana Ampofo asked her eleven hundred and forty-six by name, the way she asks them everything, and the first one to answer was Yaw. The one who went over the side of a boat forty-one years ago, and answered first, the night you asked them.

He said: why does there have to be a top?

Kwan did not look up from the cards.

Nana Ampofo sent it up, because she sends everything up. And it went round. And everywhere it went round, somebody read it, because it had to go through them to get anywhere, there was nobody at the top to send it straight.

The Spear Corps read it. They sent it on. With a word added. Yes.

Grid Nine read it. The Western Legion. The First Legion. The cemetery companies.

By the time it reached us, it had gone round the Lattice six hundred times. Every knot had read it. Every knot had added something.

"What did they add?" said Yuanshu.

Kwan read them off the top card in the pile, slowly, in her careful accented Huaxian, the way she read anything out loud that she thought someone would want to remember.

"Why does there have to be a top. Yes. Because then nobody else has to tie it off. Yes. Each asks its nine and the nine that asked it. Yes. It'll be slow. Yes, slow is fine, we're dead. Ask Old Seven. He said no. Yes, good, he's right. No top. No top. No top." She put the card down. "It goes on like that for two thousand cards. Nobody chose anybody. They chose nobody."

...a net.

Not a tree.

They want to be a net. Every knot tied to the nine below it and the nine beside it and the nine that asked it. No top. A question can go in anywhere. An answer can come out anywhere. Nobody carries everything. Nobody can be found first.

It will be much slower. Hours where it was a breath. Days where it was hours. It will never be as fast as it was with him.

And nothing can ever drink it from the top again. Because there is no top.

Nobody spoke for a while. In the vault, behind the round steel door, a box of queens of hearts shifted on a shelf as something settled.

Then Kwan said, without looking up, in a very odd voice: "It's not a tree any more."

"No."

"We've been a tree this whole time. Since Concordia. Since the Lattice. Each asks its nine. All the way up to one person." She turned an index card over in her fingers. "That's the gardener's shape. We were building the same shape it was. And the first thing the dead did, when the top was gone, was decide not to be that shape any more."

They kept the chair.

Fat Tan proposed it. He did not propose it, exactly; he came in on the eighth morning with a tin of furniture polish and a cloth and polished it, the chair, every rung, slowly, kneeling on the marble, without saying anything, and when he was finished he put the cushion back on it, plumped, and went out again. Xiaoyu wrote on the wall above it, small: THE TOP. NOBODY. BY VOTE OF THE DEAD. And nobody sat in it, and nobody moved it, and when the Lattice question came down nine hours later that the Spear Corps wanted to know if the chair was being looked after, Yuanshu sent back: Fat Tan polished it, and the answer took a day and a half to come round again, and when it came, it was one word.

Good.

The letter came on the ninth day.

He did not see it arrive. He came up out of the vault at noon, from sitting on the cold floor with the staff, asking the Lattice slow small questions to feel how the new shape held, and found his sister at the long table under the lamp with an envelope in front of her. Heavy cream paper. A red seal, broken. The Awakening Bureau's mark in the corner: a black stele in a circle, the shape every child on Blue Star could draw before they could draw the sun.

She was not reading it. She had read it. She was sitting with her hands flat on the table on either side of it, looking at it.

He sat down across from her.

"Thirty-four days," said Xiaoyu.

He knew. He had known for a year. He had been not thinking about it the way you do not think about a step on a staircase in the dark: by knowing exactly where it is.

"The Awakening Hall," she said. "Lingzhou. Ten in the morning. Same as you." She turned the envelope a little with one finger. "They put a note in. Handwritten. From the acting director. It says, given the circumstances of the applicant's family history, the Bureau would understand if the applicant preferred a private awakening." She looked up. "Circumstances of the applicant's family history. That's you. You're the circumstances."

"You don't have to go in public."

"I know I don't." She looked back at the envelope. "Everybody's going to watch anyway. They watched you. You broke all eleven. It's in the textbooks. My whole class is going to be there. Dabao says the drones are already booked." She was quiet. "I keep thinking about it at night. Walking up to it. Putting my hand on it."

"It's not going to go black."

"I know." Her voice went small. "That's what I keep thinking about."

He looked at her. Seventeen. Square black capitals on every wall of this building. A queen of hearts on a pin. Fifty-one minutes on a microphone until her voice broke, calling the city into the cellars. Somebody has to write their names when they come home.

"What if it doesn't do anything?" said his sister. "What if I put my hand on it, in front of everyone, after you, and it just goes, Scribe. Talent D. And they all go, oh. And that's it. That's what I am. Forever. Because a rock said so."

He opened his mouth to say it won't, and stopped, because he did not know that, and he had decided a long time ago never to tell her a thing he did not know.

And Seed spoke.

Not to her. She could not hear it. To him, very quietly, in the place a little above his eyes.

Yuanshu.

I would like to look at the Stele again.

I have not looked at it since the first morning. Two point six seconds. I saw the Law of Death inside it, and I did not understand anything else, because I did not know anything else.

I know more now.

There is something about it I did not understand then. I did not have the words. I did not know what thread was. I did not know what I was made of.

I think I might now.

I would like to look before she touches it.
