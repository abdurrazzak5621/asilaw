Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 237Who Could Be In It

czverse11 min read

Seed said it in the morning, in the banking hall, to everyone, because it had promised itself a night and the night was over.

The Elder went up a thread into a ring that had nobody in it, and became its yes.

And the ring asked.

It did not learn to ask. It did not grow. It was a harvest tool, a keeper with the yes taken out, and it could not have learned anything. But someone was in it now who could ask. And so it asked.

A Stele is a tool. You cannot ask it. Xiaoyu cannot ask it. Nobody can.

But someone could be in it.

Nobody spoke.

Xiaoyu was at the long table with Shi Lei and the ledgers. Gao Min was beside her with her reading glasses on their cord. Lingyue was on the stairs, sitting, with the sword across her knees. Their mother was in her wheelchair by the window of the back room, in the doorway, where she could see the long white wall.

There are three thousand nine hundred and sixty-four people on my first page.

They are from Blue Star. All of them. A Wailing Monarch harvested them in the Wastes, in the years after the sky broke, and pressed them into a crystal, and the crystal spilled off a cart, and Fat Tan sold it, and Wanqing bought it, and asked them.

They said yes. Once. In a basement.

A hundred and thirty-eight of them said yes again, in the vault, and went up three rings as knots.

I would like to ask the rest something else.

I would like to ask whether eleven of them would go into the Steles.

Their mother did not say anything for a moment.

Then she said, in a voice he had not heard her use since the vault, the night of THE FLOOR: "They're mine."

Yes.

You asked them. You wrote their names. You locked the door so nobody could take them out and use them for anything else.

This is something else.

That is why I am telling you first.

Zhou Wanqing sat in the doorway with her thin hands folded on the blanket.

"I said I should have gone back," she said. "In the vault. I said I locked the door and never asked them again, and I should have." She looked at the wall, at the column under THE FLOOR, a hundred and thirty-eight names in her daughter's capitals. "I read those every morning. I'll read eleven more. I don't want to." Her voice did not break. "But it's not my yes. It stops at their window."

She turned the wheelchair toward the vault door.

"I'm coming down," she said. "When you ask them. They'll want to know if I'm there. They asked last time."

They went down at three in the morning, because the vault was the only room in Lingzhou nobody came into without asking first.

The same as before. The round steel door standing open. The shelves of queens of hearts in their boxes. Yuanshu on the cold floor in the middle with the staff across his knees. Lingyue against the shelves. Their mother in her wheelchair in the doorway. And this time, on the bottom step of the vault stairs, not by accident, in her school jacket with her marker already uncapped: Xiaoyu.

Seed took him down.

Not into the Death Law. Under it. Under the counting and the models and the weights, under the third page with its pressed silver weave, to the first. To the quiet at the bottom of the well.

It was quieter than last time. He felt that at once. There was a place in it, toward one side, that was not empty, exactly. Still. Like the bottom of a well where a stone has been taken out, and the water has closed over the place, and you can tell where it was only because the water is a little deeper there.

That is where the hundred and thirty-eight were.

I sit in it sometimes.

I am going to ask the others now.

Hello.

It is Seed. Again. I am sorry it has been so long since the last time. I said I would ask you more often. I have not.

She is here. In the doorway. She came down to be here.

I am going to ask you something. First I am going to tell you everything I will do with your answer.

There are eleven stones on Blue Star. You know them. Some of you touched them. Some of you did not get to.

They are made of the same thread I am. They read children when they are eighteen and open them a little more than they would go, and mark how bright, and send the mark up a thread into the dark, to whatever planted the tree. It is written in them. READ WHAT RIPENS. WHEN THE ORCHARD IS BRIGHT, THE PICKER COMES.

Nobody can ask them. There is nobody in them.

I would like to ask whether eleven of you would go into them. One in each. And sit where the knot is. And when a child puts their hand on the stone, be the one who answers. And ask them, before anything else: what do you want to become?

And open what they say. Not what the stone reads. What they say.

And not send anything up the thread.

I do not know if it will work. I do not know what the thread will do when nothing goes up it. I do not know what is at the other end, or whether it will notice, or what it will do if it does.

If you go, I will forget you. The way I forgot the hundred and thirty-eight. Xiaoyu will write your names on the wall, and your builder will read them every morning, and I will not remember them.

You can leave whenever you want. The door at the bottom of everything is always a little open now. Nobody will hold you there. That is the rule.

If you say no, nothing happens. If you say nothing, I will wait. But I cannot wait long. There are twenty-seven days.

Will any of you go?

For a long time, nothing.

He had expected that. They had been silent for a long time last time too. Three thousand nine hundred and sixty-four people at the bottom of a well, who had been a harvest, and then a floor, and had been asked anything only twice in forty years. He sat on the cold floor of the vault and waited, the way Seed waited, the way Hush had taught it: the question put down, the bowl on the ground, the eyes turned aside.

In the doorway, his mother did not move.

And then, very faint, very far down, the way the first answer had come last time, from somewhere in the quiet: something moved.

Not a question, this time. He could feel the shape of it before Seed had it in words. A small shape. Young. Stubborn. The shape of someone who has been waiting to say something for a very long time and has decided not to wait any more.

...I never touched it.

Seed held very still.

...the stone. In the hall. I was seventeen. The year after it fell. The first summer they did it.

...we were going to go. My whole class. My mother made me a new jacket. Blue.

...and then the Wastes. The evacuation. The thing in the dark that sang. And then the sack.

...everyone in my class touched it that summer. I was in the sack.

...I never got to touch it. Nobody ever asked me what I wanted to be.

In the doorway of the vault, Zhou Wanqing had put her hand over her mouth.

"Wen Jia," she said. Through her fingers. "W. Near the end of the second night. I remember. I asked her if she'd let me build something out of her, and she said..." She stopped.

...I said: only if it's something good.

...I remember you. You had a cold. You kept sneezing on the glass.

Their mother laughed. It came out of her all at once, wet and sudden, a sound he had not heard from her in a year.

"I did," she said. "I had a terrible cold. It was November. The basement had no heating."

...I would like to go into the one in Lingzhou.

...the one in the hall. Where my class went.

...I would like to be the one who asks them.

...since nobody asked me.

On the bottom step of the vault stairs, Xiaoyu wrote, at the top of a sheet of ledger paper, in her square black capitals: WEN JIA. LINGZHOU.

Her hand was not steady. She wrote it anyway.

The others came slowly, through the rest of the night.

Not all at once. One, and then nothing for half an hour. Then another. Seed translated each one, slowly, waiting for each word to finish before it could tell what the word was going to be, and Xiaoyu wrote each name on the ledger sheet, and their mother, in the doorway, said each name out loud after Seed said it, very quietly, as if checking it against something written somewhere inside her.

A woman from Kesse, who had been a midwife, who said she had held four hundred children at the first breath and would like to hold some at the second. KESSE.

A man from the Northern Union who had fished through the ice for forty years before the Wastes reached his village, and who said only that he had been an E, and that it had been wrong, and he would like to make sure it was wrong for someone else. NOVAYA ZARYA.

A dock worker from the Atlantic coast. Port Halvard. Who said that somebody had hit his stone with a hammer last week, and he had heard about it, down here at the bottom of the well, and he would like to be there the next time somebody was that angry at it. So that there was someone to be angry at who could answer. PORT HALVARD.

A novice from a mountain shrine in Yamato, who had been fifteen, and had not been old enough to touch anything, and who asked, through Seed, whether the shrine was still there. Seed said yes. Seed said a girl called Ren sat on its steps with an old man who swept them. The novice said then I would like to go to the one nearest the shrine, please, and Seed said there was one, in the valley below, and the novice said yes. YAMATO.

By five in the morning, there were nine.

Then nothing. For a long time. The gray of the dawn came in through the small high window of the banking hall upstairs and down the vault stairs and lay on the cold floor in a thin pale square. Lingyue had fallen asleep against the shelves with her sword across her knees. Xiaoyu had not. She sat on the bottom step with the sheet of ledger paper on her knee and nine names on it and the marker in her hand, and watched her brother's face, and waited.

At a quarter past six, the tenth.

An old man. Very old. Older than any of them, when the Monarch came. He did not say where he had been from. He said that he had been asked twice in forty years, and both times by the same woman, and that was more than anyone had ever asked him in eighty years of being alive, and that he would go anywhere she liked.

He means you, Wanqing.

Their mother closed her eyes.

"Tell him the Southern Compact," she said. "Port Abiri. There's a hall by the harbor. There's no Tide King any more. Somebody should be there who's old."

...yes, said the old man. ...good. I like the sea.

The eleventh did not come.

Seed waited until seven. Then until eight. Upstairs, the Ossuary woke: Fat Tan's slippers on the marble, a kettle, Kwan's voice, the bronze door. Their mother sat in the doorway of the vault with her eyes closed and her hands folded, and did not move, and Yuanshu thought she had fallen asleep, and then saw her lips moving, and understood that she was saying the names. Not the ten. All of them. Three thousand nine hundred and sixty-four. From the beginning. She had written every one of them, in a basement, with a cold, nine autumns ago.

At half past eight, Seed said, to him, very quietly:

I will stop. I said I would not push. Ten is not eleven, but ten is not nothing. I will think of something for the last stone.

And from the bottom of the well, faint, slow, the way the coldest of the drowned had answered off Kesse, one word, then nothing, then the next:

...you said.

...if we said nothing.

...you would wait.

...I said: ask again tomorrow. Last time. In the vault. The night of the knots.

...it is tomorrow.

...it has been tomorrow for a long time. I have been thinking.

...yes.

Seed did not say anything for a moment.

...where would you like to go?

...wherever is left.

...I was a C. Precise Count. Like the woman with the boxes.

...I would like to count the ones who say no. Somebody should.

Gao Min found him in the ledgers that afternoon.

Not the eleventh. Wen Jia. She came to the vault steps with a gray cloth ledger open in her hands, LINGZHOU AWAKENINGS, YEARS 6–10, and her finger on a line, and her reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair, and her face very flat and careful, the way it went when she was holding a number that mattered.

"She was taken in year two," said the acting director. "The Wastes evacuation. There's no awakening for her. She never got there." She turned the page. "But there's a Wen in year nine. Same district. Same street. I checked the address against the census."

She put her finger on the line.

WEN HAO. DRIVER. TALENT D. STEADY HANDS.

"Her little brother," said Gao Min. "He'd be fifty-one. He drives the Number Nine bus. East Ring Road to the river." She looked up from the ledger. "It goes past the hall. Twelve times a day. I checked that too."

Yuanshu sat on the vault steps for a long moment.

Seed. Ask her.

I am asking.

From the bottom of the well, very faint, the small stubborn shape of a girl of seventeen in a new blue jacket she had never got to wear to the hall.

...Hao-Hao?

...he was eleven. He cried when I left. He wanted to come.

...he drives a bus?

...is he still annoying?
