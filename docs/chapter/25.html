Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 25Grid Nine

czverse8 min read

They did it in an aircraft hangar.

It belonged to the Defense Corps: a long curved shell of corrugated steel just inside the Sector Twelve wall, built for helicopters and empty since the last one was sold for parts. Rust streaked the inside of the roof in long orange lines. Pigeons lived in the rafters. When the great sliding doors rumbled open on their rails, the sound echoed down the length of it like a train going through a tunnel.

Marshal Orlov pulled the ice in himself.

He walked backward through the doors with the rope over his shoulder, and the house-sized block of ice slid in after him on its runners, scraping on the concrete, throwing the morning light all over the curved steel walls in rippling blue-white bands. It came to rest in the middle of the hangar. The temperature in the whole building dropped ten degrees. The pigeons went quiet.

Orlov let the rope fall, and laid one bare hand flat against the ice.

It began, very slowly, to soften. Not melting into water. Thinning. Going cloudy, then clear, then cloudy again, the way breath fogs and clears on glass. A thin cold mist rolled off it and spread across the concrete floor, knee-deep.

Like the cemetery, Yuanshu thought. On the first night.

Sister Ning sat on a folding stool at the edge of the mist, ten paces from the ice, in her plain gray tunic. She had brought one candle. It was lit. Its flame stood perfectly straight in the cold still air.

Beside her, on a second stool, sat a young officer of the Northern Union embassy with a camera on a tripod and a stack of the forty-one letters on his knees, one hand resting on top of them. The Accord said recorded by an independent witness. It did not say only one.

Su Mingli stood by the hangar doors with her arms folded. Captain Lin Wen stood beside her. Up on a steel girder near the roof, among the silent pigeons, the gray ash shape of Hundred-Arrow Lin crouched with her bow across her knees, watching.

Yuanshu walked into the mist.

Forty bodies.

Thirty-eight wills, faint but present. The cold preserved them.

Two I am marking. Rows three and five, at the left end. Their mothers said no.

I will not touch them. I will not even listen.

He stopped in front of the ice, close enough to see his own breath fog against its surface. Through it, forty young faces looked back at him. Forty open mouths.

"My name is Zhou Yuanshu," he said.

The hangar was so quiet that his voice went all the way to the far end and came back.

"Your Marshal brought you here. He walked nineteen days. Your mothers asked him to find out what happened to you." He swallowed. "I'm asking whether you'll let me look. Only what happened that night. Only to tell them. Nothing else. And then you can rest. You won't have to stand up. You won't have to fight. You can say no."

He laid his palm on the ice.

For a long moment there was nothing. Cold. Silence. The hiss of the mist.

Then the ice under his hand grew warm.

It spread outward through the block the way it had spread through the gravestones of the eastern field. Not fast. One face, then another, then another, a slow faint warmth moving through the rows, until thirty-eight of the forty frozen soldiers were lit faintly from inside, as if someone had set a candle behind each of them.

Two stayed dark. Row three and row five, the left end.

On her stool, Sister Ning leaned forward.

Thirty-eight. All yes.

Some of them are saying it very loudly.

One of them, in the second row, is saying it over and over. I think he has been waiting for nine months for someone to ask.

Show me, Yuanshu thought. Gently. Like Old Seven.

Reading.

The hangar went away.

Snow.

Wind like knives. A concrete outpost on a ridge of black rock at the edge of the world, with a radio mast on top and the Northern Union's white flag frozen stiff on its pole. Inside, a common room with a stove. Card games. A guitar with two strings. Forty-one young men in thick white uniforms, bored, cold, homesick, laughing at something someone had said about a sergeant's mustache.

This is not one memory. It is thirty-eight, laid over each other.

They are showing me the same night from thirty-eight places at once.

Then the stove flame bent sideways.

There was no wind in the room. The flame simply leaned, the way flames lean toward an open door, and every man in the room felt it at the same moment: a pull. Not on their bodies. Behind their eyes. As if something very far away had started, very gently, to breathe in.

The radio operator stood up.

Somewhere outside, north, a long way off, something began to make a sound.

Yuanshu couldn't hear it. Seed wouldn't let him. It came to him through thirty-eight memories already filtered, softened, turned down to almost nothing, and even so, even so, it made every hair on his arms stand up. A long, rising note, not quite a voice, not quite an animal. Like wind through a broken window if the wind were alive and starving. It went up and up and up and didn't stop.

I am turning it down.

I am turning it down as far as it goes.

This is the softest I can make it.

In the memory, a soldier by the window clapped his hands over his ears.

It didn't help. The sound wasn't coming in through his ears. It was pulling. And something was coming out of him. Out of all of them. He could feel it the way you feel your own breath leaving your mouth on a cold day: a pale thread of something rising from behind his eyes and going out through the window, north, toward the sound.

All of them. Forty-one pale threads, lifting out of forty-one young men, going north.

Their mouths opened.

It lasted one second.

That was the thing. That was the thing Yuanshu would remember for the rest of his life, standing in the mist with his hand on the ice. In thirty-eight overlapping memories it was always the same.

It lasted one second.

One second of the most terrible feeling a human being can have: the feeling of yourself being pulled out of your own body by something that does not care what you are, like water sucked out of a bucket. One second of screaming.

And then nothing.

Then the threads were gone, north, into the dark, and what was left in the common room of Grid Nine was forty-one bodies with their mouths open, still standing where they'd been, the stove flame slowly straightening, the two-stringed guitar sliding off a chair onto the floor.

One second.

It hurt for one second.

Then they were not there anymore to be hurt.

The memory went on.

Not theirs. They weren't there. But their eyes were. Bodies keep seeing, a little, for a while.

Hours later, in gray dawn light, the outpost door opened. Snow blew in. Figures came through it, stamping their boots. Men. In hooded orange coveralls, with breathing masks, and big insulated bags slung over their shoulders. One of them had a scanner, and he swept it across the frozen common room, across the open mouths, and the scanner beeped and showed a number, and he said something through his mask and the others laughed.

On the back of his coverall was a logo. Black and silver.

NORTHERN GATE REMAINS RECOVERY.

They went from body to body with the scanner. At one body near the door, a thin boy with a narrow face in the second row, the scanner beeped differently. Higher. The man with the scanner stopped, and looked at the reading, and called the others over, and they all looked at it for a long time.

Then two of them picked the boy up, carefully, like something fragile and valuable, and carried him out into the snow.

The door closed.

Private Andrei Belov.

The scanner beeped differently for him.

I do not know why yet.

I think I am afraid to know.

Yuanshu came back to the hangar on his knees in the mist.

He did not remember kneeling. His hand was still flat on the ice. Through it, the faint warmth was still glowing in thirty-eight faces.

And their mouths were closing.

Slowly. One by one. Row by row. Thirty-eight open, screaming mouths, frozen for nine months, softening, the jaws easing shut, the faces slackening into something that looked, almost, like sleep.

On her stool at the edge of the mist, Sister Ning had both hands over her own mouth. Her candle was guttering.

They want me to tell you something.

All of them. The same thing. Over and over.

"Tell our families."

And one of them, in the second row, the one who kept saying yes, wants me to add something.

"Tell my mother it was only one second. Tell her the guitar was mine and I'm sorry I never learned the third string."

Yuanshu bowed his head against the ice, and said the words out loud, every one of them, for the camera on its tripod and the young officer with the letters on his knees, who was weeping openly now, the tears freezing on his cheeks.

Then he let them go.

Rest. Lie down. You can stop now. It's over.

The warmth in the ice flickered, and went out, gently, face by face, like a row of windows going dark at the end of a long night.

Marshal Kirill Orlov had not moved from his place by the block of ice the whole time.

He did not move now. He stood with his bare hand flat against the clear blue side of it, looking at forty young faces that had stopped screaming, and his old paper-colored face did not change at all.

Except that, very slowly, a single drop of water ran down the ice under his palm, and then another, and then the whole block began, for the first time in nineteen days, to melt.
