Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 73What Lingyue Chose

czverse7 min read

The Monarchs came up the ridge at midnight.

Yuanshu lay on the ice beside the frozen wall of Grid Nine with his cheek against the cold and his eyes closed and the white staff under his arm, and felt them come. Not heard. Felt. Through the ice, through his ribs, through the roots of his teeth. Three vast weights climbing the slope out of the haze, each step a slow, grinding shudder, like a glacier moving.

Around him, on the ice, nine living people lay in a tight circle, the way the dead had shown them in the river. Face down. Eyes closed. Hands touching. Lingyue's fingers in his left hand. Bai Lu's in his right.

Around them, in rows, eleven thousand four hundred hollow people lay on the ice where the dead had set them down, wrapped in whatever the dead had found: blankets from the bunks, tarpaulins from the trucks, their own coats. Breathing. Slowly. All at the same rhythm. Thinking nothing at all.

And around all of them, a wall.

Nineteen thousand four hundred and two of the dead, standing shoulder to shoulder on the crest of the ridge in a great ring, facing outward. Gray bones and rags. A soldier beside a grandmother beside a man in a postman's jacket beside a woman in a gray wedding dress. Not moving. Not making a sound.

The thing that ate minds could not see a single one of them.

They are at the top of the ridge.

All three. Spread out. One east, one west, one directly in front of the blockhouse.

They are stopping.

The shuddering stopped.

For a long moment, there was nothing. The wind. The leaning radio mast, creaking.

Then, all three at once, the Monarchs began to scream.

It was worse than the river. Worse than anything. Three of them, so close that the sound wasn't a sound anymore, it was a pressure, a weight, a solid thing pushing down on the ridge from three directions. The ice under Yuanshu's cheek hummed. The concrete wall of the blockhouse behind him cracked, with a sound like a gunshot, from roof to ground. Somewhere a window slit blew out in a spray of frozen glass.

It was trying to make them think. To make them afraid. To make them loud.

He held the quiet. He held Lingyue's hand. He thought about the ice under his cheek.

Threads.

Hundreds of them. From all three. Sweeping over the wall of the dead. Through the dead. Down onto the ridge.

Feeling.

Something fine and cold brushed the back of his neck, and moved on.

It went on for hours.

The screaming rose and fell. The threads came over the wall in long sweeping waves, like surf, like searchlights, feeling across the ice, across the rows of hollow people, across the nine living in their tight circle. Again. And again. And again. Not finding anything. Not stopping.

At the second hour, a thread touched Mo Xing's hand and lingered.

He didn't move. He didn't think. Beside him, Mo Shi had both arms around him and his forehead pressed into his brother's shoulder and was counting, very softly, under his breath, the way they'd counted together as children in the dark. One. Two. Three. One. Two. Three. The thread slid away.

At the third hour, Qin Hao began to shake. Not from fear. From cold. His fire wanted out. Yuanshu could feel it, a hot coal burning in the dark beside him, getting brighter. Sergeant Sokolova, on Qin Hao's other side, put her bare hand flat on the back of his neck, and began, very quietly, to hum. A Northern lullaby. The coal dimmed.

At the fourth hour, the Monarchs stopped screaming.

All three at once. Silence.

And in the silence, they started to dig.

They are not sweeping anymore.

They are putting their legs down. Into the ice. Into the rock of the ridge. Digging in. Settling.

Yuanshu, they are not leaving.

They know something is here. They cannot find it. So they are going to wait until it makes a sound.

However long that takes.

He lay on the ice in the dark and understood.

Nine living people could not stay silent forever. Not days. Not weeks. Someone would sleep and dream. Someone would cry. Someone would think about home. And when they did, three Monarchs would hear it, and their threads would come down the ridge in a flood.

And there was something else.

Three of them. Here. At the ridge.

And nothing at all at the bottom of the stalk.

He squeezed Lingyue's hand. Twice. The signal they had agreed on.

Very slowly, without opening his eyes, he turned his head on the ice toward Su Mingli, a meter away. And Seed did the thing it had done for Lingyue at the white stone: reached out through his fingertips, along the ice, into Su Mingli's outstretched hand, and spoke. Not out loud. Not through the air. Directly. Quietly.

The stalk is unguarded.

All three are here. Waiting.

If he goes now, down the far side of the ridge, through the haze, he can be at the foot of the stalk before dawn.

Su Mingli's hand did not move on the ice. Her face did not change.

Then her fingers, very slowly, closed around his.

And squeezed. Once.

Go.

Jiang Lan had been clear about the rules.

"One," she had said, on the ridge, the night before, when he asked. "One living. That's all I can carry. Up a thread, unseen, as quiet as the dead. It's hard enough with one. Xuanming could barely manage it with himself." A pause. "The dead can come. As many as you like. It can't see them up there any more than it can down here."

"Just me," he'd said.

"Just you. And whoever's dead enough to follow."

Lingyue had heard. She had been sitting on the other side of the fire. She had not said anything at all. She had just looked at him across the flames for a long time, and then looked at her sword, and then gone to sleep.

He let go of Bai Lu's hand. Carefully. One finger at a time.

Then he let go of Lingyue's.

Or tried to. Her fingers closed. Hard. Holding.

He lay very still on the ice. Beside him, she lay with her face turned toward him, her eyes still closed, her breath coming slow and even, frost on her eyelashes. She did not open her eyes. She did not move anything except her hand.

And then she leaned in.

Just a few centimeters. Across the ice. Until her mouth was beside his ear, so close he could feel her breath, warm, in the freezing dark. And she whispered. So softly that it was barely a sound at all. So softly that the Monarchs, waiting at the crest of the ridge with their legs dug into the rock, could not possibly have heard.

"Yes."

He didn't move.

"In the Ledger," breathed Shen Lingyue. "That's what I chose. YES." A pause. Her fingers tightened. "And there's a box. Who may raise you? I typed one name." Her breath was warm on his ear. "Nobody else. Only you."

He couldn't speak. He couldn't think. He held the quiet so hard it felt like holding his breath underwater.

"So don't make me use it," whispered Lingyue. "Come back. That's an order. From the Dawnbreaker."

She let go of his hand.

And turned her face away, on the ice, and went perfectly, beautifully still.

...

I did not hear that.

I would like it noted that I did not hear that, and that I will never mention it.

I will be keeping it anyway.

He went down the far side of the ridge on his belly, with the staff under his arm.

Behind him, the nine living lay in their circle on the ice. Eleven thousand four hundred hollow people lay in their rows. Nineteen thousand dead stood in their wall. And three vast shapes of cloudy gray crystal crouched on the crest of the ridge with their legs dug into the rock and their ten thousand mouths shut, waiting, listening, for a sound that was not going to come.

He slid down the frozen slope in the dark, into the haze, and stood up.

Old Seven was already there. Standing in the gray at the bottom of the slope, spear grounded, flame low, as if he had always been there. Beside him, Iron Wall Zhao, broad as a doorway, with his great empty shield of rusted iron. And a drift of ash and dark air with an iron arrowhead for a heart.

And behind them, in rows, in the gray, seventy-two soldiers of the Third Battalion. Sergeant Huo at the front.

Nobody had told them to come.

Yuanshu looked at them for a long time.

"It's a long way up," he whispered. "I don't know what's at the top. I don't know if we come back down."

Sergeant Huo raised his three-fingered hand. Not a salute. A wave. The kind you give someone on a platform when you're getting on the train with them.

They say they have walked a long way already.

They say this time they would like to know where they are going.

He turned north.

Through the gray, eleven kilometers away, rising out of the murk into the night sky like the trunk of a tree made of moonlight, the stalk was waiting.
