Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 238Number Nine

czverse10 min read

The Number Nine bus left the depot on East Ring Road at 5:40 every morning, and Wen Hao had driven it for twenty-six years, and in twenty-six years he had never once let anyone ride it for free.

"Fare," he said.

Yuanshu stood on the step with the staff in one hand and his sister behind him and did not know what to say. It was six in the morning. The bus was empty except for a cleaner asleep against the back window. The man in the driver's seat was fifty-one, thick in the shoulders, gray at the temples, in a blue Transit Authority jacket with the elbows gone shiny. He had a thermos wedged beside the gearstick and a photograph taped above the speedometer, too faded to make out, and he was looking at Yuanshu the way a man looks at a famous face on a bus at six in the morning: as if it were a problem he would rather someone else had.

"Two," said Xiaoyu, from behind her brother, and held out the coins.

Wen Hao took them. He gave her two paper tickets. He looked at the staff.

"That too?"

"It's... no," said Yuanshu. "It doesn't need a seat."

"Good. Sit near the front. You're blocking my mirror." He shut the doors. "If you're going to say something, say it before the river. I go back on my own after the river. I like it quiet."

They did not say anything before the river.

He could not. He sat on the side seat behind the driver with the staff across his knees and Xiaoyu beside him, and watched Lingzhou go by in the gray light: East Ring Road, the shuttered shops, the trees, the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall going past on the left at 5:52 with a crowd on its steps already, parents and children, more than last night, standing in the cold. Wen Hao did not look at it. He drove past it the way you drive past something you have driven past twelve times a day for twenty-six years.

At the river, the terminus. A turning circle of cracked concrete under the old bridge, a bench, a shelter, the water going by gray and wide. The cleaner woke up and got off without looking at anyone. Wen Hao turned the engine off and poured himself a cup of tea from the thermos and sat in the driver's seat with it in both hands, looking out through the windscreen at the water.

"Well," he said.

"It's about your sister," said Yuanshu.

Wen Hao did not move.

"Wen Jia," said Yuanshu. "Year two. The Wastes evacuation."

The tea did not spill. It did not even shake. Steady hands. Talent D. Twenty-six years of driving a bus through a city full of rifts.

"I know who my sister is," said Wen Hao.

He did not turn round. He sat looking at the river.

"Forty years," he said. "Nobody's said her name to me in forty years. My mother stopped saying it the second winter. My father never did. I was eleven." He drank some tea. "They told us the Wastes took her. The thing that sang. They said there wasn't anything left." He put the cup down on the dashboard, very carefully, in a ring stain where a cup had been put down every morning for twenty-six years. "I saw you on the television. The rock. The orchard. The sack." He was quiet for a moment. "Is she in the sack?"

"Yes."

The driver of the Number Nine bus sat very still in his seat.

"In you," he said.

"In what I've got," said Yuanshu. "The bottom of it. With four thousand others. My mother asked them, nine years ago, if they'd let her build something. Your sister said yes." He held the staff. "She said: only if it's something good."

Wen Hao made a sound. Not a word. The sound a man makes when something he put down a very long time ago is handed back to him without warning.

"That's her," he said. "That's exactly her. Only if it's something good." He put his hand over his face. "She said that about everything. Our mother would say, Jia, carry the rice, and she'd say, only if it's something good for dinner."

He took Yuanshu down at the terminus, under the old bridge, with the engine off and the river going by.

Not far. Not into the Death Law. Under it, the way Seed went, to the quiet at the bottom of the well. He could not take Wen Hao with him. Nobody could go there who did not have it behind their eyes. But Seed could carry a voice up, the way the staff had carried Jiang Lan's for two years, the way it had carried the keeper's and the Elder's and Hush's. Through the bone. Into the air of a bus at six in the morning.

She is here.

She knows. I told her you are here.

She wants to speak first. She says she is older. She says that is the rule.

And through the staff, in the empty bus, small and stubborn and seventeen, a voice that had not been heard on Blue Star in forty years:

...Hao-Hao.

The driver of the Number Nine bus took his hand away from his face.

...you got old.

"I'm fifty-one," said Wen Hao. His voice did not work properly. "You're... you're..."

...I'm seventeen. I'm always going to be seventeen. You're older than Dad now. That's disgusting.

He laughed. It came out of him like something breaking. He sat in the driver's seat of the Number Nine bus with his hands on the wheel, laughing, and then not laughing, with his shoulders shaking and his face wet, and Xiaoyu, on the side seat, very quietly, got up and went to the front and stood beside him and did not touch him, and did not say anything, and stayed.

...do you still have it?

"Have what?"

...the jacket. The blue one. Mum made it. For the hall. I never wore it.

Wen Hao was quiet for a long moment.

"It's in a box," he said. "In my flat. On top of the wardrobe. Mum gave it to me when she moved to the home. She said, you keep it, I can't look at it." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I can't look at it either. I just keep it."

...good.

...I want to tell you something. Before I go.

He went very still.

"Go," he said. "Go where?"

She told him herself. Seed did not help. It carried the words up through the staff one at a time, slowly, the way they came from the bottom of the well, and Wen Hao sat in the driver's seat and listened to his sister tell him that she was going to go into the black stone in the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road, the one his bus went past twelve times a day, and sit at the bottom of it, and be the one who answered when a child put their hand on it. Forever. Or until she wanted to stop. And ask them, before anything else, what they wanted to become.

When she had finished, he did not say anything for a long time.

The river went by.

"No," said Wen Hao.

...Hao-Hao.

"No." He turned round in the seat. For the first time, he looked at Yuanshu: at the staff, at the place a little above his eyes, where everyone looked when they wanted to talk to the thing in his head. "You've had her for nine years. Something had her for thirty before that. In a sack. In the dark." His voice was very controlled. "And you found her, and the first thing you do is ask her to go into a rock. Forever. In the dark. Again."

Yuanshu did not look away.

"Yes," he said. "That's what I asked."

"Why?"

"Because somebody has to be in it. Or it goes on grading children for something to eat. And she said yes."

"She's seventeen."

...I'm fifty-seven, said his sister, through the staff. ...I'm just seventeen too.

Wen Hao turned back round and looked out at the river for a long time.

"She said yes in a basement," he said, to the windscreen. "Only if it's something good. She was in a sack. She'd have said yes to anything that wasn't a sack."

...that is true.

It was not Wen Jia. It was Seed. Yuanshu felt it speak through the staff before he could stop it, and did not try to.

That is true. I have thought about it. I think about it every day.

She was asked in a basement after thirty years in the dark. She would have said yes to anything.

So I asked her again. In the vault. Before I asked her this. Whether she wanted to stay where she was, or rest, or go back to being asked nothing. I told her she could go through the door whenever she liked.

She said she would think about it.

She thought about it for nine years. She told me last night.

...Hao-Hao.

...can I ask you something?

Wen Hao did not turn round.

"What."

...what did you want to be?

...when you were eleven. Before. You used to say. Every night, at dinner. You used to drive Mum mad.

The driver of the Number Nine bus sat with his steady hands on the wheel.

"A teacher," he said at last. "I wanted to be a teacher. Like Mr. Hu on the third floor. He had a bicycle with a bell."

...and?

"And I went up the steps when I was eighteen and put my hand on the rock and it said D. Steady Hands. Driver." He shrugged, very slightly, without letting go of the wheel. "So I drove. Everybody said, well, it's a good D. Steady. You'll always have work. Nobody gets eaten on a bus." He was quiet. "I never thought about it again. It said what I was. You don't argue with the rock."

...nobody asked you.

"Nobody asks anybody. That's what the rock's for."

...I'm going to.

...every one of them. Every summer. Every child who goes up those steps. Before it reads them. Before it opens anything. I'm going to say: what do you want to become?

...and if a boy says a teacher, it'll open teacher. Not driver. Not D. Not steady hands. Teacher.

...that's what I want, Hao-Hao. That's what I want to be.

...I'm asking you to let me.

The river went by.

A long way off, up East Ring Road, a bell rang: the first tram of the morning at the junction by the market. A gull landed on the roof of the bus shelter and looked at them through the windscreen and flew off again. Wen Hao sat with his hands on the wheel and his eyes on the water.

Then he reached up, and peeled the faded photograph off the dashboard above the speedometer, very carefully, where it had been taped for twenty-six years, and held it in his steady hands.

Yuanshu could see it now. Two children on a step. A girl of fifteen or so with a sharp chin and her hair in a tail, laughing at something off to one side. A boy of nine on the step below her, scowling, holding a toy bus.

"You don't need me to let you," said Wen Hao. "You never did. You never let me let you do anything."

...I know. I'm asking anyway. That's the new rule.

He looked at the photograph for a long time.

"Twelve times a day," he said. "I go past that hall twelve times a day." He put the photograph in the breast pocket of his Transit Authority jacket, behind the shiny elbows, over his heart, and buttoned the pocket. "I'm going to stop the bus. Every time. Right outside. I don't care what the Authority says. I'm going to stop and open the doors and wait for a count of ten. Every time. So you know it's me."

...yes.

...that's good. That's something good.

"And on the day," said Wen Hao, "I'm bringing the jacket."

He drove them back.

He would not take any more money. He said the return was on the house because the house had had a bad morning. He drove the Number Nine back up East Ring Road from the river in the gray light with the two of them on the side seat behind him and the staff across Yuanshu's knees, and did not say anything, and at 6:41, outside the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall, with the crowd on the steps, he stopped the bus.

Not at a stop. There was no stop there. He pulled in to the curb, and put the handbrake on, and opened the doors, front and back, with a hiss, and sat with his hands on the wheel.

The parents on the steps turned and looked at the bus.

Wen Hao counted to ten. Out loud. Not loudly. Yuanshu heard him, from the side seat, the numbers going by under his breath like the stops on a route he knew by heart.

Then he closed the doors, and took the handbrake off, and drove on.

...I heard.

...tell him I heard.

"She heard," said Yuanshu.

Wen Hao did not turn round. He drove. But his hand came off the wheel for a moment, the left one, and touched the buttoned pocket over his heart, and went back.

"Twelve times a day," he said. "Twenty-five days. That's three hundred."

He drove on up East Ring Road toward the depot.

"That's a lot of tens," said the driver of the Number Nine bus, to nobody, to the windscreen. "She'll get sick of me."

...no, said his sister. ...I won't.
