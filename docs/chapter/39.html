Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 39The Charter

czverse7 min read

On the forty-third day after the Ossuary opened, Xiaoyu climbed a stepladder in the old banking hall, uncapped a new black marker, and wrote a number on the board that took her two tries to fit.

1,004,216.

The hall went quiet.

It was a Sunday afternoon. The queue outside on East Market Road was shorter than it had been in the first week, but it was still there, still going around the corner into Tannery Lane, still full of people talking to strangers. Inside, under the high arched ceiling, every desk was busy. Somebody was coming out of the vault room with red eyes. Somebody else was going in. Iron Wall Zhao sat on his stool by the bronze door with a four-year-old asleep against his shield, because the child's mother was at a desk and he had been the nearest thing to a wall.

Xiaoyu came down the stepladder.

For a moment nobody did anything.

Then somebody at the back, one of the volunteers, started clapping. Slowly. And then the person next to them. And then the whole banking hall was clapping, volunteers and visitors and people in the queue at the door, a sound that went up into the arched stone ceiling and came back down, and Xiaoyu stood at the bottom of the ladder with the marker in her hand and burst into tears, and pretended she hadn't.

One million, four thousand, two hundred and sixteen.

YES: 38%. ASK MY FAMILY: 25%. ONLY TO SPEAK: 18%. QUIET: 19%.

The Quiet column has grown every week.

Sister Ning says that is how you know people believe the Ledger is real. You only say no to something you think is listening.

Catherine Moore came at four.

She walked in through the bronze door past Iron Wall Zhao, nodded to him politely as if he were a doorman at a good hotel, and came across the marble floor with a slim folder under her arm. She looked like she hadn't slept. She looked, Yuanshu thought, the way Su Mingli had looked coming back from Concordia: tired, and very sharp underneath it.

"It's official," she said. "The Council's going to hear it."

They went up to the old manager's office on the second floor, which had a desk, two chairs, a window over East Market Road, and the smell of a hundred years of paper. Su Mingli was already there. So was Director Lu, standing by the window with his hands behind his back. Sister Ning came up the stairs a few minutes later, slowly, with her candle.

Catherine Moore opened the folder.

"Ninety days from now," she said. "On Concordia. A full hearing of the Council, in the crater, with all five delegations and the press. The Provisional Accord expires at the end of the year. They have to replace it with something permanent, or let it lapse." She laid two documents side by side on the desk. "There are two proposals."

The first was thin. Four pages. Plain language. On the cover, a title somebody at the Council had given it, which made Sister Ning raise both eyebrows.

The Concordia Consent Charter.

"It's your Ledger," said Catherine Moore. "Turned into international law. Every nation that signs keeps a registry like this one. Every person can choose. The four choices, word for word, your sister's wording. Quiet means quiet, that exact phrase, in Article Three. Raising without recorded consent is a crime in every signatory nation, by any method." She tapped it. "Including cores. Including companies. Including governments."

"Who wrote it?"

"Huaxia submitted it." She glanced at Su Mingli, who looked out of the window as if she had no idea what anybody was talking about. "With co-sponsors. The Northern Union."

Yuanshu looked up.

"Marshal Orlov went home," said Catherine Moore. "He walked into the Council of the Northern Union with the Grid Nine footage and forty letters from forty mothers, and he put them on the table, and he sat there until they voted. It took six days." She almost smiled. "I'm told he didn't get up once. Not even for meals."

The second proposal was thick. Eighty pages. Dense. Careful. Its title was much less friendly.

The Remains and Residue Data Act.

"The Atlantic Federation," said Catherine Moore. "With support from two of the smaller island states." She turned the pages without reading them. "It says the Ledger is a good idea. It says registries should exist. And then it says that any record created under international law, including consent recordings, memory readings, and the registries themselves, belongs to the signatory states. Not to the dead. Not to the families. To the states. To be used 'for public safety, research and the prevention of awakener-related catastrophe.'"

"WARDEN," said Yuanshu.

"WARDEN," she agreed. "Meridian has a demonstration scheduled for the hearing. They're going to show WARDEN predicting what a high-rank awakener will do, in real time, on stage." She closed the folder. "They've asked the Council for permission to use you as the subject."

The office was very quiet. Out the window, down on East Market Road, Dabao's mother was shouting the price of pork buns.

"What does the Council need from me?"

"Testimony," said Catherine Moore. "Witnesses. Evidence. Not a speech, please. The last thing anyone needs is an eighteen-year-old lecturing five heads of state." She glanced at him. "No offense."

"None taken."

"They'll want to hear from the dead," said Sister Ning quietly, from her chair by the door. "Won't they."

Catherine Moore looked at her for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "That's going to be the hard part."

They worked out a list.

Fang Yu, the firefighter, with her father's note. The young paramedic from Jinhua Tower with her crushed candle. Captain Lin Wen, for her mother. Zhao Mei, the nurse. Mrs. Pan from Heishui. Fat Tan and his ledger, under Bureau protection. Qin Hao, for the Western Pass. Marshal Orlov, if he would come, and if he would agree to sit in a chair that wasn't a chair in front of a Council he'd once walked out on.

And Gao Yinping.

"She'll come," said Lu, from the window. "She called me yesterday. She wants to testify about the cores. About what she didn't know." He paused. "She said, and I'm quoting, 'If I have to stand in front of the whole world and say I was the one who bought them, I'd rather it was for the right side.'"

"And from the Institute?" asked Catherine Moore. "The Council will want a scientist. Someone who can explain what the cores actually are. What hollows are. Under oath."

Lu didn't answer right away.

He turned from the window. He looked at Yuanshu.

"Deputy Director Gu has volunteered," he said. "He sent me a letter this morning. Handwritten. Very warm. He says it would be an honor to testify in support of the Charter. He says Wanqing would have wanted it." He took off his glasses and didn't clean them. "He's the best qualified person in Huaxia. If we say no, the Council will ask why."

"Watch the people who are kindest to you."

If he testifies, he will be in the room. On Concordia. Next to you. Next to the five Kings. Next to the WARDEN demonstration.

If he does not testify, he will be somewhere else. And we will not know where.

Yuanshu looked at the window, at the gray light over East Market Road, for a long time.

"Say yes," he said.

Su Mingli turned her head sharply.

"Keep him where we can see him," said Yuanshu. "Somebody kind once told me that a man who lives alone should have something to keep alive." He looked at Lu. "I think we should give him something to keep."

Lu looked at him for a long moment. Then he put his glasses back on, and nodded once.

That evening, when the Ossuary had closed and the volunteers had gone home and the banking hall was dark except for one lamp over the board with its four columns of numbers, a woman came to the bronze door.

She was dressed in white. Not a uniform. A plain white coat over plain white clothes, like someone who had come from a funeral in a country where people wear white to funerals. She was perhaps forty. Her face was very calm.

Iron Wall Zhao stood up from his stool.

She bowed to him. Deeply. The way you bow at a shrine.

Then she looked past him, through the glass panels of the bronze door, at the board inside with its numbers.

Yuanshu came down the marble stairs and opened the door.

He recognized her. He had seen her once before, on a small screen propped on someone's knees, outside a cemetery, among the candles. Standing behind the Yamato delegation at Concordia. The woman in white, who had said, Yamato abstains. We have a question first. We'll ask it ourselves.

"Yamato has its question," she said, in careful, quiet Huaxian. "We would like to ask it in person. Before the hearing." She looked at him with very calm eyes. "We have dead of our own, Zhou Yuanshu. Very old dead. Older than your Black Tide. Older than your Steles." She paused. "Some of them have been waiting a long time for someone to ask them anything."

She bowed again.

"Yamato would like to know," she said, "whether you would come and listen."
