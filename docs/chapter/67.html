Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 67The First Yes

czverse8 min read

Her name was Jiang Lan, and she had taught second-year arithmetic in a village school in the eastern hills for nineteen years before a Rift opened under the playground.

She told him this through the rock, in her dry, brisk, slightly impatient voice, while he knelt with his bare hands on the warm ground and the steam rose around him and everyone at the fire behind him held their breath.

"Sixty-eight years ago," she said. "A Tuesday. I got the children out. All twenty-three of them. Then the floor went." A pause. "I don't recommend it."

"He found me three days later. A boy. Fifteen. Skinny. Ears too big for his head. He'd just woken up to his law that morning and didn't know what it was. He was walking through the rubble crying because he could hear all of us, under the stones, and didn't know how to make it stop." Her voice softened a little. "He found me, and he sat down on the rubble over where I was, and he said: 'Excuse me. I'm very sorry. I think I can make you stand up. Would you like me to?'"

"Nobody had ever asked me that. Nobody had ever asked me anything, really, except whether the homework was due Friday."

"I said yes."

Lu Xuanming.

She was his first.

"I was his first," said Jiang Lan, as if she'd heard Seed. Maybe she had. "And I stayed. Forty years. I walked beside him through three wars and the Black Tide. I told him when he was being stupid, which was often." A pause. "And I told him to ask. Every time. He always did. Until the last night. When there wasn't time."

The rock under his palms was very warm now.

"He came here to die. To this stone. He sat down right where you're kneeling, with his back against the marker, and asked me one more thing." Her voice went quiet. "He asked if he could make something out of me. Out of my bones. Something that would remember everyone he'd ever raised, so that whoever came after could find them again. So that someone, someday, could ask all the ones he never asked."

"I said yes. Again. Twice in one life. Not many people can say that."

Yuanshu bowed his head over the warm rock.

"Thank you," he said. "For waiting."

"Don't thank me yet," said Jiang Lan. "You haven't asked the real question. And I haven't decided."

"What's the real question?"

"Not 'is anyone there.' I'm always there. I'm a staff. It's what I am now." The dry voice sharpened. "The real question is what you want me for. Because he made me with two doors, young man, and he never decided which one to use. He left it to me. And I'm not opening either of them for someone who doesn't know."

Two doors.

The last page. "It opens the way the threads come down. Whoever holds it can walk up the thread into the place where they are kept. Awake. Unseen. And whoever holds it can open that place wider. From this side. All the way."

"So," said Jiang Lan. "Ask me properly. Tell me what you want."

He thought about it.

He knelt on the steaming ground with his hands on the rock and didn't rush. Behind him the fire crackled. Somewhere very far away, on the edge of hearing, so faint it might have been the wind, there was a long low moaning from the south. The Monarch, walking toward the wall.

He thought about the river of the dead. Two hundred thousand, scrape, scrape, scrape, north, for thirty-one years, because nobody ever told them they could stop.

He thought about a field of gold columns under a sky with no sun. Billions of kept faces. A small woman in a white lab coat standing alone with her arms spread wide, shining like a struck match, while the whole vast sky turned to look at her.

Don't come back up here until you can ask it properly. All of it.

"I want to ask the ones he raised," said Yuanshu aloud. "All of them. What they want. If they want to stop, I want to let them stop. If they want to walk, I want them to walk with someone. Not after something."

"Go on."

"And then I want to go up." His voice didn't shake. "The first door. Up the thread. Into the field. Awake. Unseen. As quiet as the dead." He swallowed. "My mother's up there. She went in on purpose. She's been holding her breath for seven years and she let it go so I could get out. I want to bring her home." He paused. "And I want to ask it. The thing that keeps them. Properly. All of it. Not one question. Every question. Until it understands what want means."

"And the second door?"

"Never," said Yuanshu.

The rock was silent.

"Never," he said again. "Not for anything. Not to win. Not to save anyone. Not if it's the last way left. If someone opens that place wider from this side, it comes down all the way. Here. To everyone." He pressed his palms into the warm stone. "You can make me promise. You can make it a condition. If I ever try, break."

For a long time, Jiang Lan didn't answer.

Then she laughed. A short, dry, delighted bark of a laugh, the laugh of a teacher whose worst student has just, unexpectedly, gotten something right.

"He'd have liked you," she said. "He was never that sure of anything in his life." A pause. "All right. Yes. Third time. Write it down."

YES.

With a condition. "If you open the second door, I break."

Sergeant Sokolova is writing.

Behind him, Yuanshu heard the scratch of a pencil on paper, very fast.

"And young man," said Jiang Lan. "He's not here anymore. Not really. Xuanming. He's down there with me, but he chose quiet thirty-one years ago and he's kept it. Don't wake him. He was so tired." Her voice softened. "But if you could... just tell him. Quietly. That someone came. That's all he ever wanted."

Yuanshu closed his eyes.

Lu Xuanming, he thought, very softly, down into the dark beneath the white stone, to a faint, quiet presence that did not stir. Someone came. Someone asked.

You can rest.

Nothing answered. But the warmth under his hands deepened, just for a moment, the way a sleeping person breathes out when someone pulls a blanket up over their shoulders.

The ground opened.

Not violently. Gently. The rock at the foot of the white stone, the rock Mercer had dug through to two meters and found nothing, simply went soft, the way frost goes soft in the sun, and parted, and sank away into a narrow dark shaft going straight down. Warm air breathed up out of it, smelling of dust and old paper and, very faintly, of chalk.

At the bottom, three meters down, in the light of the fire, a man was sitting.

Or what had been a man. Bones, gray and fine as ash, sitting cross-legged with his back against the deep foundation of the survey marker, in the rags of a long dark coat. His skull was bowed. His hands rested in his lap.

And across his knees lay a staff.

It was white. Pure white, white as the stone above, white as snow under a full moon, a little shorter than a spear, made of something that was bone and was not quite bone: long pieces fitted together end to end, joined with silver, smooth as polished ivory, so that it looked like a single unbroken length. At the top, a small white skull, no bigger than a child's fist, with tiny silver inlay around its eye sockets like reading glasses.

It was glowing. Very softly. Gray-white. Like a candle seen through paper.

I can feel them.

In the staff. Every one he raised. Ninety million names. Like a library.

And two hundred thousand of them still burning. Very far away. Northeast. Walking.

She remembers all of them.

Yuanshu climbed down into the shaft.

He went carefully, with his bare hands on the warm rock walls. At the bottom, he knelt in front of the seated bones of Lu Xuanming, and bowed, low, until his forehead touched the dust. Then he straightened up, and reached out, and laid his hand on the white staff.

"May I?" he said aloud.

"You already asked," said Jiang Lan, from the staff, very close now, dry and fond. "Twice. Pick me up, for heaven's sake. It's been thirty-one years. I'd like to see the sky."

He picked it up.

It was light. Much lighter than it looked. And warm, like a hand.

He climbed out of the shaft with the staff in one hand.

He came up over the lip into the firelight, onto the frozen plateau, and stood up beside the white stone with the small skull at the top of the staff glowing softly above his shoulder.

Everyone was looking at him. Su Mingli, perfectly still. Lingyue, with her hand on her sword. The Mo twins, gripping each other's sleeves. Bai Lu. Qin Hao. Sokolova with her notebook. Yan Shuang. Old Seven and Third Battalion in their rows. Gu Hanshan on his camp stool, with his face in his hands.

And Julian Mercer.

He was looking at the staff.

Straight at it. At the white bones and the silver joins and the small glowing skull. His pleasant, handsome face had gone perfectly still. His pupils had grown very wide.

He can see it.

In your hand. A living hand.

Yuanshu, he can see it now.

"Oh," said Julian Mercer softly. "Oh, there you are."

And he smiled.

It was not his usual smile.

His lips drew back. And back. And back, further than lips should go, past the corners of his mouth, up his cheeks, toward his ears, his face splitting open along a seam like a ripe fruit. And behind his very good, very white, very even teeth, there were more teeth. And behind those, more. Rows of them. Going back and back into a throat of cloudy gray crystal, cracked and seamed, and in every crack and seam, small, human, open, a mouth.

All of them screaming.
