Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 18Rift Zero

czverse6 min read

The elevator was a steel cage hanging in a shaft of raw rock, and it took four minutes to reach the bottom.

Nobody spoke on the way down. Su Mingli stood at Yuanshu's left shoulder. General Han stood at his right. Director Lu stood alone at the front of the cage, facing the doors, his hands clasped behind his back, the way he had stood in the corridor outside the hearing room. Old Seven filled the back corner, spear grounded, eye-lights fixed on the director's spine.

Two hundred meters. Two hundred fifty.

The temperature is dropping.

The Death residue down here is very strong, and very old, and it is the wrong shape.

Wrong how?

Every death I have touched had a direction. The echo wants to go home. Back toward rest.

This one does not want to go anywhere.

It is pointed at us.

The cage stopped.

The chamber at the bottom of the shaft was round, perhaps forty meters across, carved straight out of the bedrock. The walls were lined with law-steel plates and covered in containment arrays, ring after ring of them, glowing a faint, steady blue. They hummed so deeply that Yuanshu felt it in his teeth rather than heard it.

In the center of the chamber, on a raised platform, stood a cylinder of law-glass three meters tall.

And inside the cylinder, suspended upright in something that was not quite liquid and not quite light, was the faceless one.

It was not whole. It had been torn apart thirty-one years ago by the walking dead of three cities, and someone had gathered up the pieces and put them back together with great care, the way you might reassemble a broken vase you were afraid of. Long seams ran across its gray body where the parts met. One arm ended at the elbow. There was a great ragged hole through its chest where something had gone in and come out the other side.

But the head was intact.

It was smooth. Not like a mask. Like a surface that had never been meant to hold features. The blue light of the arrays fell across it and simply vanished, as if the face were not reflecting it so much as keeping it.

Yuanshu stood at the foot of the platform and looked up at it.

He had seen it before, through a dead man's eyes. Walking down the sky. Turning, for one second, to weigh Squad Leader Qi like an object on a scale.

In the back of the chamber, Old Seven made a sound Yuanshu had never heard him make. A low, grinding creak of bone, like a dog growling deep in its chest.

His echo is very loud right now.

I am not going to translate it.

"We call it Specimen Zero," said Director Lu. His voice echoed strangely in the round chamber. "It's been here for thirty-one years. We've taken samples, scans, law readings. We've learned almost nothing. Its law signature matches none of the three thousand. Its body is not organic and not mineral. It doesn't decay." He paused. "And it doesn't stay still."

"Log 2,190," said Yuanshu.

"Seven years ago, at 2:14 in the morning, the cameras recorded its head turning three degrees to the left. Toward the shaft that leads up to your mother's lab." Lu was looking at the cylinder, not at him. "She was in the lab when it happened. Two days later, she sealed her door and disappeared."

Su Mingli's voice was very cold. "You never told Tianshu that."

"No," Lu said. "I never told anyone. If the Council had known it moved, they'd have ordered it destroyed. And if we destroy it, we lose the only piece of the enemy we have ever held in our hands." He turned around at last and looked straight at Yuanshu. "I was twenty-two years old on the night of the Black Tide. I was the officer who carried the containment order to Lu Xuanming, and I was the officer who carried the containment case down here, because three days before it came out of the sky, our sensors saw something in the north that looked like a lens, and the Institute guessed what might be coming and built a cage. We were right. We caught a scout. We have spent thirty-one years trying to find out what sent it, and when it will come back."

His voice did not rise. But for the first time since the hearing, it shook.

"So I'll ask you plainly, Mister Zhou. You raise the dead and they answer your questions. Raise it. Ask it what it is. Ask it who sent it. Ask it when."

The chamber was quiet except for the humming of the arrays.

He means it.

He has meant it for thirty-one years.

His heart rate is 112. This is the first time I have seen him without control.

Yuanshu climbed the three steps of the platform and stood in front of the cylinder. Close enough that his breath fogged the law-glass.

He did not raise anything. He reached, very carefully, the way he had reached for Dorian Hale on the floor of the Tianshu corridor. Down into the Death Law. Listening. For an echo. For a will. For anything that could say yes or no.

And something answered.

Not an echo. Echoes were faint, like voices at the far end of a long hall. This was close. Right there behind the glass. It did not feel like the dead.

It felt like being looked at.

Stop.

Stop. Pull back. Now.

Yuanshu pulled back.

He was on one knee on the platform. He didn't remember kneeling. His nose was bleeding, and his hands were cold to the wrist, and Su Mingli had his collar in one fist, ready to throw him off the platform.

It is not dead.

I want to be very precise. It is not alive either. It is something I do not have a word for.

It is waiting.

When you reached into it, it reached back. Not to answer. To read. The same way I read. It was trying to find out what I am.

I closed the door before it finished.

I think.

Inside the law-glass cylinder, very slowly, the smooth featureless head turned.

Three degrees to the right.

Toward Yuanshu.

General Han swore. The arrays on the chamber walls flared from blue to white all at once, and somewhere far above them, an alarm began to sound.

They stood in the steel cage on the way back up, and nobody said anything for the first two hundred meters.

"No," Yuanshu said at last.

Director Lu, standing at the front of the cage, did not turn around.

"I won't raise it," Yuanshu said. "Not today. Maybe not ever. If I raise something I don't understand, I might be opening a door from the inside for whatever's on the other side of it. I don't raise anything I can't ask. And I don't know what I'd be asking."

"Your mother," said Lu quietly, still facing the doors, "said almost exactly the same thing to me. Seven years ago, in this cage, on the way up." He paused. "Two days later she was gone."

The cage climbed on through the rock.

"Then I'll be more careful than she was," Yuanshu said.

"She was the most careful person I ever knew."

"Then I'll be stronger."

Lu Zhengtian turned at last, and looked at him for a long moment through his silver-rimmed glasses. Whatever he was searching for in Yuanshu's face, he seemed, after a while, to find some of it.

"I hope so," he said. "For all our sakes."

I need to tell you something.

When it reached for me, I recognized part of its structure.

The way a word in a foreign language sometimes sounds like a word you know.

It reads without asking. That is the whole of what it does.

That is what I would be, without Rule One.

I do not like that it sounded like me.

I like even less that it also sounded like your mother.
