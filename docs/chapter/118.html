Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 118No Lock

czverse7 min read

Outside the sphere, Lingyue saw his face change.

She was standing behind his chair, with her hand on her sword, watching him. He had been sitting very still for eleven minutes with his eyes closed and both hands on the small white skull at the top of the staff, and his face had been calm. Sad. Listening.

And then it went white.

His hands clenched on the staff. His whole body jerked, once, in the chair, as if someone had grabbed him by the collar from inside and pulled. A thin line of blood ran from his nose.

And on the pedestal in the center of the room, the sphere's gold went red.

"Yuanshu!"

She had her sword out before she knew it. Lit. White-gold. And then she stopped, because she did not know what to hit.

The light in the round room was changing. The clean warm gold that had poured out of the sphere and lit the lighthouse and the hospital and half of Port Halvard was going dark. Red. Gray-red. Like a coal. Like the eye of the spire in the bay. And the steel ring around the pedestal had begun to hum.

On the headland, through the glass wall, the lighthouse beam flickered.

And down the hill, in a long wave, the lights of Port Halvard started going out.

Not the way they had gone out every night for two years, a block at a time, to save diesel. All at once. Pulled. Street by street toward the headland, the way water pulls back from a beach before a wave. Windows going dark. Streetlights going dark. The hospital.

Lingyue.

It was Seed's voice. Out loud. Coming out of the air beside the chair. Strained. Thin.

It has him.

It is pulling at him. At the staff. At everything he is.

He has the old man. He will not let go of the old man. He will not let go of the staff.

It is pulling the power back. All of it. Out of the town. Into itself.

It wants all of it. It is so hungry.

The floor cracked.

A long jagged line, running out from the base of the pedestal across the white stone, splitting it. And up through the crack, gray, cloudy, glowing red in its cracks, something came.

A root.

Thick as a man's arm. Then another. Then four. Bursting up through the floor of the round room, through the polished white stone, curling, reaching. Not toward Lingyue. Not toward the door, where Cassia Lowell stood with both hands over her mouth.

Toward the bed. Toward the still figure of the old lighthouse keeper.

Toward the end of the bed, where a seven-year-old girl in pajamas with an inhaler on a string around her neck was sitting, staring at the root coming toward her, too frightened to move.

Old Seven was already there.

He had not been by the pedestal for six hours for nothing. He moved before the root was halfway across the floor. Two meters ten of white bone, crossing the room in three strides, his spear coming down.

The spear went through the root.

It did not cut it. It pinned it. Drove it down into the cracked stone of the floor and held it there, writhing, a meter from the end of the bed. And the root, pinned, turned, and found the bones of the hand that held the spear.

The three-fingered hand.

And began to burn it.

Old Seven did not let go.

Lingyue moved.

She did not think. She did not decide. She was across the room in a line of white-gold, and she had her sword in one hand and her other arm around the seven-year-old, lifting her off the end of the bed, and the child's inhaler swung on its string and hit her in the face, and she did not care.

She swung the sword. Not at the root. Past it. At the floor beside it. White-gold light, carving a line through the stone between the root and the bed.

"Pale fire," she said through her teeth. "I need pale fire. I need the staff. Yuanshu..."

He did not hear her. He was in the chair with his eyes closed and his face white and blood running down his lip, holding the staff, holding the old man, holding on.

So she did it alone.

She bent the light.

All of it. Every bit of white-gold in the blade, bent away from the old soldier holding the spear a meter from her, away from the bones of his three-fingered hand. The way she had bent it for four hours in a canyon a long time ago, around fifty skeletons she had just met. The way she had sewn cobwebs with the tip of her sword in a river of the dead.

And cut the root a hand's width from Old Seven's fingers.

It fell. Smoking. Shriveling.

Three more came up through the floor.

Roman Sterling came through the door like a wall falling over.

He did not draw anything. He did not have anything to draw. He swept one huge arm across the air, and a wave of force went out from him like a shockwave, and the three roots shattered against it in a spray of gray splinters.

"Out!" he roared. "Everyone out! Secretary! Get the child out!"

Cassia Lowell was already moving. She took Ellie from Lingyue's arm without a word and turned and ran, with the child clutched against her chest, down the long white corridor toward the door. The tired woman in the cardigan ran after her.

The roots kept coming.

Up through the floor. Up through the walls. Through the glass dome, shattering it, rain pouring in. Gray and jagged and red in every crack. Not toward the people now. Toward the chair. Toward the boy holding the staff.

And the sphere on its pedestal was growing.

Not the way the spire at Port Abiri had grown, over a day. Faster. New crystal pushing out of its surface in jagged branches, swallowing the steel ring around it, bending the steel like wire. A meter across. Two. Three. Its cracked gray surface glowing red. Its great eye open.

Staring at the staff.

"It's a seed!" Lingyue was cutting, cutting, bending the light around Old Seven every time. "It's growing into a spire! Like the bay!"

"Can you stop it?" Roman Sterling, shattering roots, his face running with sweat and rain.

"Not while he's inside it!" She looked at Yuanshu in the chair. At the blood. At his white knuckles on the staff. "If I burn it, I burn him! If you crush it, you crush him!"

The King of the Atlantic Federation looked at the boy in the chair for one long second.

"Then what do we do?"

Lingyue did not answer.

She was looking at Old Seven.

The old soldier was standing over the pinned root with his spear in his three-fingered hand. The root was gone. Cut. Shriveled. But he had not moved. He was standing there with his skull tilted toward the sphere.

Listening.

The way he had listened at the edge of the raft above a husk world. At the bottom of a crack. At the foot of a spire in a bay where a nine-year-old was playing statue.

And his gray flame was burning very bright.

...he hears something.

Lingyue.

In the sphere. Under the fire.

Someone is being very quiet.

"Who?" said Lingyue. "There's only one person in there. Walter Ashby. And Yuanshu."

And the staff.

And the staff has two people in it.

Jiang Lan.

And someone else. Someone who has been in there since the field. Since the night the stalk was gold. Who never came all the way out. Who left a little of herself behind in the bones of a schoolteacher, because she was so tired, and it was so quiet in there.

Lingyue.

She has been asleep for a year.

She is not asleep now.

Inside the sphere, in the red fire, Yuanshu was losing.

He could feel it. The pull. Not on his body. On him. Threads of him being drawn out, one after another, into the burning walls of the small round room. His first memory. The rice cooker. A green flask. The Stele with no name.

He held the staff. He held the old man. He held on.

"Don't let go," Jiang Lan was saying, over and over, very far away. "Don't let go. Don't let go."

He did not let go.

But he could not pull either. Not against this. Not with one hand on a staff and one hand on a burning old man and the whole hungry weight of a Hunger's seed pulling at everything he was.

Yuanshu.

I cannot hold it. I am trying. I cannot.

It is too hungry.

I know, he thought. I know.

And then, from the staff in his hand, from somewhere very deep in the bones of a schoolteacher, under ninety million names, a voice.

Quiet. Sharp. Slightly hoarse.

Not his mother's voice now. Not the one in the hospital by the river.

The one from the field. The one that had stood alone in an endless field with her arms spread wide, shining like a struck match.

The part of her that had stayed behind.

"Yuanshu," said Zhou Wanqing. "Let me do something loud."
