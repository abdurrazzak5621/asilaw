Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 274The Old Chord

czverse10 min read

The Old Chord came down on the twentieth day, all seven of them, and they came down the keeper's road the way the very old come down stairs: slowly, one step at a time, with long pauses, as if each step were a decision.

He had never seen anything so old. Not the Cantor, who was nine hundred years and milk-glass. Not Sister Kiyo, paper over bone, twelve hundred years under a gate. The Old Chord were older than either. Seven Singers, five meters tall, so old their glass had gone not white but gray, the gray of the rings, the gray of walker-thread, so that no light showed through them at all. Many-throated; but some of the throats in each great ring had closed, Seed said, and would not open again. They had sung the tithe up the thread before the Cantor did, for longer than the Lamplit had existed. They had taught her the song.

They had pushed eight hundred and ninety-seven children off a beach in the dark toward a mouth that was not there. Forty-one of them had burned out in the thread before they saw the lamp.

They came down through the roof of the Awakening Hall onto the platform, in front of the warm black stone, and stood there.

The hall was full. It had been full every day for twenty days. The thousand and seventy children of the Hollow Bell, standing in the aisle with their own small lights inside them. The holders. The Candle's children. The city. And at the foot of the steps, in her wheelchair, his mother, and beside her, four meters of milk-glass, the Cantor.

Nobody spoke.

They are asking for the boy who refused the tithe.

They say it in the old sentence. It is the only one they have. Ichiro says they are bending it as far as it will go.

They say: you who would not take.

He went up the steps. Not quickly. He stood on the platform in front of seven gray Singers taller than anything in the hall, with the staff in his hand, and did not say anything, and waited.

They say: we pushed them.

They say: the eight hundred and ninety-seven. Down the thread. Into your stone. Because the song said the tithe must be given whole, and you would not take it, and we thought you were testing us.

They say: forty-one burned in the thread. Before they saw the lamp.

They say: we heard them burn. We heard them go dark. We heard the boy at the bottom of the thread ask them what they wanted, and we heard them say chosen, and then we heard one of them say: I saw the lamp.

They say: we have never heard one of the chosen say anything but chosen.

The seven gray Singers on the platform did not move.

They say: we have sung the tithe up the thread for longer than there have been Lamplit. We chose the brightest. We dressed them in light. We sang them to the hand. Every age. Thousands of ages. And the hand took them, and passed us by, and the song said we had done well.

They say: we never once heard one of them say: I saw the lamp.

They say: we do not know what we have done.

He did not answer at once.

He stood on the platform and looked at seven beings older than anything he had ever spoken to, gray as the rings that had come to prune everything Seed touched, standing in front of a stone that asked. And he thought about the Cantor at the window of the back room, bent, singing one wavering note to a child in a drawer. Three of them were her own. And the Old Chord had taught her the song.

You did what you were taught, he thought. By the song. By a hand in the dark. For longer than there were people to teach anything else.

Forty-one children burned in a thread.

Both of those were true. He did not know what to do with both of them at once.

Neither do I.

I have run it. There is no number for it.

I think you do not have to know what to do with it. I think you only have to ask them what they want.

What do you want? he said. Up through the staff, into the old sentence, which Ichiro had bent as far as it would go to fit the question. I'm telling you first. Whatever you say, I'll tell the hall. Exactly. I won't do anything to you. Nobody here will.

The seven were quiet for a long time.

Then the one at the center, the oldest, the grayest, with half her throats closed, bent. Slowly. The way a reed bends. Toward the stone.

She says: the stone. The one that asks.

She says: on the Hollow Bell, there are seven stones. In the Hall of Throats. Black. Warm. They read the little lamps when they stop glowing and mark how bright, and the brightest are chosen, and dressed in light, and sung up.

She says: the Old Chord have sat beside those stones and sung for longer than anyone remembers.

She says: we are very old. Some of our throats have closed. In a few hundred of your years, we will stop.

She says: when we stop, we would like to go into the stones.

He went very still.

She says: the way the girl went into yours. The one who never got to touch one. Who asks.

She says: we would like to sit in the stones of the Hollow Bell, where we sang the chosen up, and ask every little lamp that touches them, for as long as the stones last, what it wants to become.

She says: we sang them up for ten thousand years. We would like to ask them for ten thousand more.

She says: it will not be enough. Nothing will be enough. The forty-one are dark.

She says: but it is what we want.

She says: you asked.

The hall was silent.

At the foot of the steps, the Cantor of the Hollow Bell had not moved. Her great ring of throats was opening and closing, and no sound came out.

Then, from the front of the aisle, Ninth. Three meters tall, translucent, with her own small light inside her, the one who would have been sung up ninth, whose age-mates had been pushed off a beach in the dark, forty-one of whom had burned before they saw the lamp. She came up the aisle, slowly, and up the steps of the platform, and stood in front of the oldest of the Old Chord, who had taught the Cantor the song that had dressed her in light.

She did not say anything. She looked up.

And sang her note.

Not the one from the first morning. Not the one she had sung up the thread. Not the one she had sung back to a child's first cry. A different one. Lower. Harder. A note that, Ichiro said afterward, from the fog at Shiomi, through Ren, if it had been a bell, would have been the bell the shore rings when a boat comes in late and has done something wrong at sea.

The headman's bell. You came in. The lamp was lit. That is enough for one night.

Not forgiveness. Ichiro was very clear. He said there was no forgiveness in it; that the fog-code did not have forgiveness, and neither did the note. It only said: you came in.

The oldest of the Old Chord stood on the platform in front of the stone, five meters of gray glass with half her throats closed, and listened to it.

And then, from one of the throats that still opened, very small, very old, wavering, for the first time in longer than the Lamplit had existed: a note.

Not in the song.

Death Law: no change.

Level: no change.

I am telling you because you asked me to tell you the numbers. Even today.

There are no numbers for today.

The Singers left on the twenty-fourth day.

Not all of them. He asked; the Cantor asked; Ninth went through the aisle of the Awakening Hall and asked every one of the thousand and seventy, one at a time, in their own notes, which were not a language yet but were becoming one. Most of them wanted to go home. To the Hollow Bell. To the Hall of Throats with its seven black stones. To their mothers, who had sung them up and did not know yet that they could come back. To teach the little lamps, the Cantor said, that there was more than one song.

Two hundred and six wanted to stay.

They wanted a sky. They wanted to swim in the river by the old ferry steps, the way the Candle's children did. They wanted to stand in a courtyard and not sing for a while. Ninth was one of them. She said she had put a question down, the way Hush had told her, and she would like to stay where it was and see what grew.

The Old Chord went home. All seven. To sit beside the stones and sing, the Cantor said, until they stopped, and then to go inside them and ask.

And the Cantor went with them.

She came to the window of the back room on the last morning and bent, four meters of milk-glass, until her great ring of throats was level with the sill. Xiaodeng was awake in the drawer. The Cantor sang her note to her, the low slow wavering one, the lullaby from before the hand. Xiaodeng looked up at her and made a sound back.

She says: on the Hollow Bell, a little lamp glows for one day.

She says: this one glowed for nine hours before she came. And lit a hall. And a thread. And a thousand of my children found the shore by it.

She says: tell her, when she is old enough to be asked.

She says: tell her the Hollow Bell owes her a song. Her own. Not the old one. When she wants it, she has only to ask, and we will sing it.

At dusk, the shell over the city sang.

Not the song. Not take them, and pass us by. Not any sentence at all. Eight hundred and sixty-four Singers who had come down a road of light to see a lamp, and the Cantor, and the seven of the Old Chord, and the tens of thousands of the choir who had sung one sentence for ten thousand years and gone silent for twenty-four days because they did not know any other, opened their throats as the vast pale shell turned over Lingzhou and began, slowly, to rise. And each of them sang their own note.

They did not sing together, or in a chord. Tens of thousands of different small notes, from tens of thousands of different throats, rising up over the old quarter and the river and the north wall and the long curved roof of the Awakening Hall, none of them meaning anything, none of them for anything. Each one saying only: here I am.

It was not beautiful the way the song had been beautiful. It was not anything like that. It was ragged and strange and full of notes that did not go together, and some of the old ones wavered, and some of the young ones cracked.

Nobody on Blue Star wanted it to stop.

The shell went up into the dark, singing, smaller and smaller, until it was a point of pale light among the stars over the north, and then nothing. And on the north wall, three thousand people stood with their faces turned up long after it had gone, listening to a sound that only Seed and Tam could still hear.

It is still singing.

It will sing all the way home.

It will be ragged the whole way.

Kwan found them three nights later.

She came down the stairs into the banking hall at two in the morning with the laptop held open in front of her like a tray of hot food, and he knew from her face, the way he had known from her face on the night of the three gray rings, and the night of the gardener, and the night of the Candle.

He was on the vault steps with the staff. Xiaodeng was asleep upstairs in her drawer, and Lingyue beside her, and Xiaoyu had gone to bed at one after asking the Collector what it wanted and writing on the back of Gao Min's hand. Ninth was in the courtyard under the stars.

Kwan turned the laptop round.

The keeper's eye. The Shallows. Seed's map: the arm, the twelve gold dots of the fields, the gray husks of the pruned ones, the dark place where the branch was with its small gold point. The small white ring of the keeper, a third smaller.

And at the far edge of the Shallows, very far, where the arm began to curve away toward the deep water and the places Seed's map had nothing, crinkles.

Not one. Many. Small, the way the surface of water crinkles when something is dropped into it from very high up. In a line. Coming out of the dark one after another, the way lamps come on along a street.

He counted them. He did not have to. Seed did.

Ninety.

It is spring.
