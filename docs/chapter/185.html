Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 185The Boat Bell

czverse9 min read

The monks found the words on the ninety-sixth night of listening, and it was the nun who had asked about the bean paste who heard them first.

Ren called at two in the morning. Yuanshu was awake. He was usually awake at two now; the Field did not sleep, and he had learned to sleep in pieces, on the vault steps, the way soldiers on a wall sleep. He put the phone on speaker on the marble beside him, and Seed listened, and the old banking hall listened, and on the line from the mountain in Yamato, under Ren's careful breathing and the dripping cedars, he could hear the monks.

All of them. Talking at once. The old dry chorus, overlapping, the way a crowd talks when it has heard something it cannot hold.

"She heard it," said Ren. "Sister Kiyo. Under the three hundred and fortieth gate. She says the boat bell isn't one note. It's four. Very quick, very soft, under the long silence. Nobody else could hear it. She says she used to ring it herself when she was a girl, before she came up the mountain, so she knows how it's supposed to sound." A pause. "She says it's not ringing the way it's supposed to."

She is right.

I have it. Four notes. I thought it was one. It is four, very close together, and the second and third are wrong.

Not wrong. Different. Changed on purpose.

"She says," said Ren, "that when she was a girl, the fishermen had a way of talking with the boat bell. When the fog was too thick for lanterns. Short words. Four notes for each. You could change the middle two and say something." Her voice was very steady. "She says she taught her brother. He was a fisherman. He went down to the boats the evening the ring came, carrying rice."

Nobody in the banking hall said anything.

"She says," said Ren, "he was taken."

It took them until dawn.

Sister Kiyo, under the three hundred and fortieth gate, a woman who had been eighteen when she came up the mountain twelve hundred years ago and sat down beside her brothers and stopped eating, gave Seed the fog-code a word at a time. Four notes. The middle two changed. Rice. Boat. Home. North. Fog. Rocks. Wait. Come. A fisherman's whole language in a few dozen words, made for shouting across water in the dark.

Seed laid it over eleven minutes of the bell, and over every recording since the night after the field, and found it.

Under the long silence after the name. Very faint. Very fast. Four notes, then four, then four. Over and over, once in every interval, so quietly that nobody on Blue Star could have heard it unless they had been listening to that bell twelve hundred years ago in a fog.

I am going to read it. Then I am going to tell you what I am not sure of.

It says:

SHIOMI. WE SEE YOU.

FOG. DO NOT RING.

THE ONE WHO GROWS IS LISTENING.

WAIT. WE WILL COME QUIETLY.

Kwan read it four times off the laptop screen, under her breath.

"Fog," she said. "Do not ring."

"It means: don't answer," said Ren, on the line. "Sister Kiyo says. In the fishermen's code, fog means: something is out there that you can't see. Keep quiet. Don't ring the bell back. Don't light a lantern. Sit still in your boat and let it pass."

"The one who grows," said Yuanshu.

There is no word for gardener in the fog-code.

There is a word for grow. For the rice. For the terraces.

I think they used the nearest thing they had.

"They know about the gardener."

They know about something that grows things and is listening. I think it is the gardener. I am eighty-one percent sure. I would like to be more sure. The fog-code does not have many words.

"And they saw the field."

They said: we see you. They changed the interval the night after the field. They added the boat bell the night after the field.

I think they were watching. Through something. The way we watch through the keeper.

I think they saw a walker come apart, and another walk away, and they understood what it meant before we did.

And they started ringing faster. And they told us: don't answer. Something is listening for an answer.

Sister Kiyo asked for him at dawn.

Not for Ren. For him. Ren said so, on the phone, with something odd in her voice. "She says she wants to speak to the boy who asks. Directly. She says she's heard about him for a year and a half from under her gate and she'd like to know what he sounds like."

He did not have the staff in Yamato. He was two thousand kilometers away on the vault steps of an old bank. But Ren was a King of Spirit, and she put her hand flat on the stone of the three hundred and fortieth gate and the phone against it, and carried his voice down.

"Sister Kiyo," he said. "I'm Zhou Yuanshu."

"Yes. You think very loudly."

The voice came up through Ren, through the stone, through the phone. An old woman's voice in old Yamato, very dry, very clear, with something in it that Seed translated as amusement and then, after a moment, corrected to fondness.

"My brother's name was Ichiro. He was twenty-two. He was the best fog-ringer in Shiomi. He could ring a whole sentence in the time it took the bell to stop shaking."

"He went down to the boats that evening with a sack of rice for the night fishing. I saw him go from the hall. I was already sitting. I had already stopped eating. I did not go after him."

"The threads came down. He was not quiet. He was never quiet in his life. He talked to the fish."

"He was taken."

Yuanshu did not say anything.

"I have been under a gate for twelve hundred years thinking that he was in a gold column somewhere above the sky, with his eyes shut, until something came and took the column away."

"And now someone is ringing the boat bell in the fog-code. Our code. That I taught him. With the middle notes changed the way he always changed them, a little too fast, because he was always in a hurry."

Her voice did not shake. It was twelve hundred years old and very dry and it did not shake at all.

"It's him."

"Or it is someone he taught. Out there. Somewhere. Ringing my brother's bell in my brother's way."

"I would like to answer him."

The banking hall was very quiet.

He sat on the vault steps with the phone on the marble beside him and the staff across his knees, and looked at the long white wall, and the column at the end of it in Xiaoyu's capitals. SHIOMI. Someone is ringing. Someone is listening.

"He said don't," he said. "Fog. Do not ring. Something's listening."

"Yes. He would. He was always telling me what to do."

"I am telling you what I want. You asked. Not my brother. You. You always ask."

"I want to ring the boat bell back. Once. Four notes. In the fog-code. So that he knows someone heard."

"If you ring it," said Yuanshu, "the gardener might hear it too. Your brother said so. And Article Eleven says that when one person's answer is acted on in the name of all, all have to be asked." He was quiet. "A bell from Blue Star is Blue Star's bell. Not mine. Not yours. Eight billion people would be ringing it."

"Yes."

"I know. The granddaughter read me your Article. All of them. I liked the eleventh. I liked that you wrote a rule for yourself."

"So I am not asking you to let me ring it. I am telling you what I want, because you asked. And I would like you to take it to your Council of five chairs, and ask all of them, the way your rule says."

"And if all of them say no, I will sit under my gate and listen to my brother ring for another twelve hundred years. I am good at that."

"And if all of them say yes."

She stopped.

"Then I would like it to be four notes. Not a shout. Four notes, very quiet, in the fog-code, the way he would ring it."

"The word for: we hear you."

"In the fog, that is what you ring when you cannot see the other boat, but you want them to know they are not alone on the water."

He took it to Concordia.

Not that day. Six days later, in the crater, in person, with the recording and the fog-code and Sister Kiyo's words in Seed's careful translation, and Ren on the sixth screen from the step of the broken hall with her hand on the wood. He told them all of it. Fog. Do not ring. The one who grows is listening. Wait. We will come quietly. And an old woman under a gate who wanted to ring four notes back.

General Han listened to all of it without moving.

When it was done, Okafor looked down the table.

"Article Eleven," she said. "Where the answer of one would be acted upon in the name of all, all shall be asked." She folded her hands. "Mister Zhou. You want to ask Blue Star."

"I want to ask Blue Star whether it wants to ring four notes into the deep water," said Yuanshu. "In a fishermen's code twelve hundred years old. Very quietly. To someone who told us not to. Knowing that something might hear it." He looked down the table. "And I want to tell them first what we'll do with the answer. If they say no, Sister Kiyo goes on listening. If they say yes, she rings four notes. Once. That's all. Nothing else. No message. No name. No who are you. Just: we hear you."

"Eight billion people," said Elias Brandt. "Asked. About four notes."

"Yes."

"That has never been done."

"No," said Yuanshu. "Nothing in Article Eleven's ever been done. We wrote it six weeks ago."

General Han Yizhou leaned forward.

"How long would it take?" he said. "To ask eight billion people."

...I have run it.

Every nation has a way to ask. Votes. Radios. The Quiet has chapters in every city. The Ossuary has a Ledger with millions of names in it, and the Ledger knows how to ask, because it was built by people who asked.

Forty days. If everyone helps.

And it is the right length. Forty days is long enough for everyone to hear what four notes might bring, and decide.

"Forty days," said General Han. He sat back. "My son's name is on that wall because someone asked a column above the sky what it wanted, and it said: home." He folded his hands. "I'd like to be asked about this. I think everyone on Blue Star would."

Okafor looked at him for a long moment.

Then at the five delegations, and the empty chair for Yamato, and the sixth screen, where Shirasagi Ren knelt on a broken step with her hand on the wood.

"Forty days," said the Chair. "We ask. All of them. We tell them everything we know and everything we don't." She took off her reading glasses. "And then we ring, or we don't. Four notes." She looked at Yuanshu. "The first thing Blue Star has ever decided all together, Mister Zhou. And it's whether to say we hear you to a fisherman."

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

"Good," said Adaeze Okafor. "It's a better first thing than most."
