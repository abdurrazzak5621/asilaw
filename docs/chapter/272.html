Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 272A Note Not in Any Song

czverse12 min read

They could not move her.

Bai Lu tried. She came through the side door of the Awakening Hall at twenty past four in a coat over her nightclothes with a bag in one hand, and ran down the aisle past a thousand tall pale children of the Hollow Bell without looking at any of them, and went up the steps of the platform and put her hands on Lingyue and said, in the voice she used for battlefields, right, we're going to the hospital, now, both of you, and then stopped.

Because the lamp would not go out.

Lingyue stood beside the warm black stone with her hand still held up and the pale light in it, the size of a lamp, steady, and her other hand flat on her stomach, and her face white, and she could not put it down. She said so. She said it through her teeth, with her eyes shut. It was coming up from underneath, the way it had been coming all winter, the way the moon pulls water, and now that something was happening it was coming up faster, and if she let go of the lamp she did not know what it would do.

"It's the baby," said Bai Lu. Not to anyone. To herself. Her hands were flat on Lingyue's back. "It's pulling on it. It's been pulling on it for months. And now it's coming, and it's pulling on everything." She looked up at the lamp. "If she drops it, it goes everywhere. All at once. Like the wall."

"Then she doesn't drop it," said Yuanshu.

"She can't hold it up for twelve hours."

"Watch me," said Shen Lingyue.

So it happened there.

On the platform of the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road, in front of a warm black stone with a bright blue jacket over its shoulder, under three lines of light that had hung in the air since Awakening Day, at the foot of a thread going up into the heart of a vast pale shell over the city where the Old Chord had stopped singing and was silent. With a thousand and seventy children of another world standing in the aisle, three meters tall, translucent, with their own small lights inside them, not singing either. Watching.

Bai Lu sent someone for blankets. Fat Tan brought them. He had heard; everyone had heard; Seed had not told anyone, but the Candle's children had, Tam had, because Tam could hear everything now, and the city woke up the way a city wakes when something is happening and nobody has said what. The holders came. They stood in a line up the side aisle with their hands on each other's shoulders, not because the stone needed holding, but because, one of them said, it seemed like the right way to stand.

Dabao and Fat Tan carried his mother in, in her wheelchair, through the side door at five, and she did not say anything at all. She had Fat Tan put her at the foot of the steps, and she sat there with her thin hands folded on the blanket and her quick dark eyes on her son's wife, and her lips moving. He thought she was saying the names. Then he understood she was not. She was counting. Under her breath. The way she had counted the seconds in the basement once, with her hand on a crystal, nine years ago, asking four thousand people if they would let her build something.

Xiaoyu sat on the step beside the wheelchair with her marker in her hand, capped, and did not write anything.

Old Seven stood at the side door, facing out.

He held her up.

There was nothing else to do. Bai Lu did everything else, Bai Lu and a midwife from the old quarter who had come with Dabao's mother and who looked at the thousand children of the Hollow Bell in the aisle and the lamp in Lingyue's hand and said only well, I've seen stranger, I've delivered in the Black Tide, and took off her coat. He held Lingyue up. He stood behind her on the platform with his arms round her and the staff on the floor beside them and held her, so that she could keep her arm up, so that the lamp stayed lit.

"Don't you dare," she said, at seven, through her teeth.

"Don't what?"

"Count."

"I'm not counting."

"Seed is."

I am.

A hundred and fifty-six. A hundred and fifty-two. A hundred and forty-nine. Your heart. A hundred and sixty. The baby's.

I am sorry. I cannot not count. It is what I am.

I asked her, last month. Whether I could count this. She said yes.

"I said yes to the heartbeat," said Lingyue. "Not to... AH." She stopped. The lamp in her hand flared, white, and the whole hall went pale like the inside of a shell, and a thousand children of the Hollow Bell in the aisle took a step back. And then she held it. Brought it down. A lamp again. The size of a lamp. "Not to all of it," she said, when she could.

I am sorry.

"Keep counting," said his wife. "It helps. God help me. It helps."

At nine, the sun came up over the river.

It came in through the high windows of the Awakening Hall, the way it had come in on Awakening Day, the color of strong tea. Onto the platform. Onto the stone. Onto a woman held up by her husband with a lamp in one raised hand and her face wet and white and set the way it set when she was about to fight something, and Bai Lu kneeling, and the midwife from the old quarter, and on the steps a woman in a wheelchair, counting.

At ten past nine, the Cantor came down.

He did not see her come. He was holding Lingyue. But he felt the hall change, the way a hall changes when someone very old comes into it, and he looked up, and she was there.

At the top of the aisle. Come down the keeper's road through the roof, without being asked, alone. Taller than any of them. Four meters, perhaps. Not translucent; old, so old her glass had gone the color of milk, so that the light inside her showed through only faintly, like a moon behind cloud. Many-throated; a great ring of throats at the base of a long neck, opening and closing slowly. Not dressed in light. Dressed in nothing. A Singer who had dressed a thousand ages of children in light and sung them up, and had never worn it.

She came down the aisle. The thousand and seventy made way for her. They did not bow; Seed said the Hollow Bell did not have bowing. They went still, the way you go still when someone comes into a room who has been the center of every room you have ever been in.

She stopped at the foot of the steps. Beside his mother's wheelchair. And looked up at the platform.

She says...

She says: what is this.

"She's having a baby," said Xiaoyu, from the step, before anyone could stop her. Fast. The way she said things she had just understood. "It's coming. It's early. My sister-in-law's holding the lamp so your children could see where the shore was, and now she can't put it down, and the baby's coming."

The Cantor looked at her for a long time. At the girl of seventeen on the step with a capped marker in her hand.

She says: we do not have this.

She says: on the Hollow Bell, children are sung. They are not... she does not have the word. They are sung into being by a chord of seven. They come out of the song already standing.

She says: she has never seen one come like this.

She says: may she stay?

His mother, in the wheelchair, did not stop counting. But she lifted one thin hand off the blanket, and laid it, very lightly, on the long pale milk-glass foot of the oldest Singer of the Hollow Bell, and left it there.

The Cantor looked down at it.

And stayed.

At thirteen minutes past ten, in the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road in Lingzhou, on the platform where every eighteen-year-old in the city had put their hand on a stone for forty-two years and been told what they were, a child was born.

He would not remember it properly. He tried, afterward, many times, and could not. He would remember Lingyue's voice, saying something that was not a word, and then saying his name, and then saying something about Bai Lu that Bai Lu said she would not repeat. He would remember the lamp in her hand going up white all at once, the whole hall, the whole roof, the long curved roof he had walked under at eighteen, lit from inside like a shell, so bright that a thousand children of the Hollow Bell in the aisle cried out. He would remember the midwife from the old quarter saying there, there, there you are. He would remember his mother, at the foot of the steps, stopping counting.

And then the lamp went out.

Not dropped. Not burned. It went out the way a lamp goes out on a shore when the boat it was lit for has come in. All at once, softly. The light that had been coming up from underneath all winter went down, and down, and settled, and was only Lingyue's own, inside her, where it had always been.

And in the sudden quiet of the Awakening Hall, in the sun coming in through the high windows the color of strong tea, there was a sound.

Small. High. Furious. Not a word. Not a song.

A note.

...it is a girl.

Bai Lu says it is a girl. Two point six kilograms. Four weeks early. Breathing. Bai Lu says breathing is the most important thing. Bai Lu says she is breathing very well. Bai Lu says she is breathing like her mother, which is to say, furiously.

A hundred and fifty-eight.

It is the same as the first time I counted it. In the back room. In the summer. A hundred and fifty-eight.

I am recording this.

I asked. Last month. She said yes. Obviously.

He was on his knees on the platform. He did not remember kneeling. Lingyue was in his arms, gray and wet and shaking and laughing, and in her arms, wrapped in one of Fat Tan's blankets, something very small and very red with its eyes screwed shut and its mouth open, making a sound.

The same sound. Over and over. Not a word.

A note.

In the aisle, the children of the Hollow Bell had gone completely still.

All of them. A thousand and seventy. And the Cantor at the foot of the steps, four meters tall, milk-glass, with his mother's hand on her foot. All of them looking at the platform. At the sound.

...Ichiro says...

No. Not Ichiro. Ninth says.

Ninth says: that is not in the song.

She says: it is not in any song. It is not a chord. It is not a sentence. It is one note, from one throat, and it is not for anything. It is not asking to be chosen. It is not asking to be passed by. It is not asking for anything.

She says: it is just saying: here I am.

And Ninth, at the front of the aisle, three meters tall, with her own small light inside her, opened one throat.

And sang her note back.

Not the one from the platform. Not the one she had sung up the thread. A new one. Small. Answering.

And behind her, one by one, then many, then all of them, a thousand and seventy children of the Hollow Bell who had come down a road of light to see a lamp opened their throats and sang their own notes. Not together. Not a chord. Not the song. Each one their own, the one thing that was theirs, answering a note that was not in any song, until the Awakening Hall on East Ring Road was full of it. A thousand small different notes, rising up through the roof, up the thread, into the dark, toward a silent shell over the city.

The most beautiful thing any of them had ever heard.

And nobody wanted it to stop.

At the foot of the steps, the Cantor of the Hollow Bell had begun to shake.

He saw it. The great ring of throats at the base of her long milk-glass neck opening and closing, opening and closing, and no sound coming out. The oldest Singer of a world that had sung one sentence for ten thousand years, standing in a hall full of children singing notes that were not in it.

His mother had not taken her hand off the Cantor's foot.

She says...

Seed stopped. Then went on, very carefully.

She says: I do not have one.

She says: a note. Of my own. I do not have one. I have sung the song for nine hundred years. I sang my own children up in it. I have never sung anything that was mine.

She says: I do not know how.

His mother looked up at her.

Zhou Wanqing, in her wheelchair, at the foot of a platform where her son's wife was holding her granddaughter, with her thin hand on the foot of a being from another world who had sung her own children up a thread to a hand in the dark. She did not have the Cantor's language. Seed carried it for her.

"Neither did she," said his mother, and nodded at the small red furious thing in Fat Tan's blanket on the platform, still making its sound. "Ten minutes ago. Nobody does. You just open your mouth and find out."

Yuanshu.

It was evening. They had moved her at last, Lingyue and the baby, to the hospital, where Bai Lu had made everyone except him and his mother and Xiaoyu go home, and then had made Xiaoyu go home, and then had looked at his mother and given up. Lingyue was asleep. The baby was asleep in a clear plastic box beside the bed that Lingyue had said was ridiculous and Bai Lu had said was not negotiable.

He was sitting on the edge of a hard hospital chair, looking at her.

I would like to say it now. If you will let me. I am asking first.

Yes.

Hello.

I am Seed. I live in your father's head. I was a harvest sack once. I count things.

I counted you this morning. A hundred and fifty-eight. The same as the first time.

You cannot answer. You are not anyone yet. Article Twelve. Your grandmother's. Your parents decided, once, for you. And now they owe you the question. And so do I.

I am going to ask you every morning. Until you can answer. And after.

I am telling you first.

What do you want?

The baby in the clear plastic box did not wake. She turned her head a little, the way babies do, toward a sound only she could hear. And her mouth opened. And she made a small sound. Not a cry. Not a word.

A note.

...that was a coincidence, said Seed. Lingyue would say.

I am writing it down anyway.
