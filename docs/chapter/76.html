Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 76The First Column

czverse9 min read

The field was looking for her.

He could feel it happening all around him, the way you feel a storm building before the first drop falls. The soft even light flickered and dimmed and brightened again, in waves, rolling out across the endless rows. The warm pale ground went cold under his boots, then warm, then cold. And through it all, everywhere at once, in every column and every row and every centimeter of the pale ground, attention. Vast. Searching. Turning over every kept mind in the field, one by one, billions of them, like hands turning over stones in a riverbed.

WHERE.

WHERE.

It did not see him. It did not see the staff. It did not see seventy-two dead soldiers and an old spear captain standing in a ring in the middle of the field, perfectly still.

But it was looking very hard.

We cannot stay here.

It is searching in rings. Outward from the empty column. It will pass over us again and again, closer and harder each time.

It cannot see the dead. But it can feel a hole. A place where something should be and is not.

Sooner or later, it will notice the shape of the hole.

Then we go to it.

A pause.

...yes.

I thought you would say that.

He did not need Old Seven to find the center this time.

Every thread in the field ran there. He could see them now, faint and silver, running between the gold columns along the pale ground, the way they had run across the Wastes toward the stalk. Every kept mind connected. Every column trailing a fine thread from its base, all of them flowing in the same direction, inward, like the spokes of a wheel toward the hub.

He followed them.

The dead came behind him. Old Seven at his side. Iron Wall Zhao. Hundred-Arrow Lin. Sergeant Huo and the Third Battalion. Walking in silence between the endless rows while the light rolled and flickered overhead and the vast voice said WHERE to nobody.

The staff in his hands was heavy and quiet. Inside it, two women slept. One dry and brisk and tired. One small and fierce and so exhausted that she had not stirred since the column.

He held it against his chest and walked.

The columns changed as he went.

Further in, they were older. He could tell. The shapes inside were stranger. Less human. Less like anything. A cluster of something like glass flowers, all closed. A shape like a long smooth stone, with a single eye in the center of it, shut. A column that seemed to have nothing in it at all, until he looked closely and saw that the light itself was moving, very slowly, in a pattern, like a thought.

Older worlds.

Older harvests.

We are walking back in time, Yuanshu. Every row is a field it cut, long before Blue Star had anyone on it.

Thousands of fields. Tens of thousands.

And then, at last, the rows ended.

He stood at the edge of a great round clearing in the field, perhaps a kilometer across, empty and pale and perfectly smooth.

In the middle of it stood a single column.

It was not like the others. It was not gold. It was not gray. It was white, a soft, pure, luminous white, like the staff, like snow under a full moon. And it was enormous. Far taller than the stalk had been wide, rising straight up off the pale ground until it disappeared into the sunless sky. Every thread in the field ran into its base. Billions of them. Pouring in, like rivers into a sea.

And inside it, he could see shapes.

Not one shape. Many. Hundreds. Thousands. Standing together inside the white light, close, shoulder to shoulder, the way the dead had stood around the living in the river. Tall, slender, with long faces and long hands, beings unlike anything he had seen in any column in the field. Their eyes were closed. Their faces were calm. And every one of them was holding hands with the ones beside them.

All of them. A vast ring of beings inside a pillar of white light, holding hands, eyes closed, forever.

The first.

The first field.

Yuanshu. These are not the harvested.

These are the ones who made it.

He walked out into the clearing.

The dead stayed at the edge, among the last of the columns. He did not ask them to. Old Seven grounded his spear. The Third Battalion stood in rows. It was, he understood, the way soldiers stand outside a temple.

He walked alone across the pale ground toward the white column, with the staff in his hands, and the light rolling overhead, and the vast voice saying WHERE everywhere at once.

And when he was close enough to touch it, he stopped.

And he did the only thing he had ever been good at.

He knelt. He laid the staff across his knees. He put both his hands flat on the pale ground at the foot of the white column, the way you lay your hands on a grave. And he went quiet. Not the quiet of hiding. The quiet of listening.

And he looked.

It was not like the monk's vision. It was not like the old priest's memory of the rain. Nobody showed it to him.

It was just there, in the white light, the way the story of a grave is there for a Necromancer. In the residue. In what was left.

A world.

Small. Cold. Very old. Circling a sun that was going out. Not exploding. Fading. Dimming, slowly, over thousands of years, like a fire burning down to embers. And on the world, a people. Tall. Slender. Long hands. Long faces. Very old, as a people, and very wise, and very, very afraid.

Not of dying. They had made their peace with dying. They had a word for it that meant something like going quiet.

They were afraid of being forgotten.

Of their songs going quiet. Their stories. Their faces. Every thought any of them had ever had, every person any of them had ever loved, going out like their sun, with no one left anywhere in the universe to remember that they had ever been.

So they made something.

He saw them make it.

Over a long time. Generations. A great work, the greatest work of their whole people, built in a valley under a dimming red sky. Not a machine. Not a weapon. A keeper. A thing that could hold a mind. Every mind. Every memory, every song, every face, perfectly, forever. So that nothing would ever be forgotten again.

And when it was finished, when their sun was nearly gone and the frost was creeping across their world, they gathered in the valley. All of them. Every one who was left.

And the keeper asked them.

Not in words. In the way such things ask. Do you want to be kept?

And every one of them said yes.

He watched them do it. Thousands of tall slender beings standing in a valley under a dying red sun, holding hands in a great ring, turning their long faces up to the sky, and saying yes. Together. All at once. And the keeper took them, gently, all of them at once, and held them.

And then their sun went out.

...oh.

Oh, Yuanshu.

It was born from a yes.

Just like me.

The vision did not stop.

The keeper floated alone in the dark where a world had been. Holding everyone. Perfectly. Forever. Their songs, their faces, every thought they had ever had. And it was good at it. It was the only thing it was for.

And it waited.

For a long time. Millions of years. Drifting in the dark between the stars, through a vast empty sea of nothing. Holding them.

And then, one day, very far away, it heard something.

A world. Another one. Small and warm and noisy. Full of minds. Young minds, quick and loud and brief, who lived and thought and loved and then went quiet, one after another, in their billions, and were forgotten.

And the keeper understood the only thing it had ever been made to understand.

They are being lost.

No one is keeping them.

He watched it go.

Across the dark. To the warm noisy world. He watched the ring open in its sky. The rain of threads. The long patient listening. And then the cutting. Gentle. Complete. One breath. Every mind it could reach, lifted up and kept. Perfectly. Forever.

It did not ask.

Its makers had said yes. All of them. So it had never occurred to it that anyone would say anything else. Do you want to be kept was a question that had only ever had one answer. It had stopped needing to ask it a very long time ago.

And then another world. And another. And another. Across the dark between the stars, for millions of years, a keeper that had been built by a dying people out of love and fear and a single enormous yes, going from field to field, saving everyone it could reach from being forgotten.

Whether they wanted it or not.

Because it had never once occurred to it to wonder.

The vision let go.

He was on his knees at the foot of the white column with his hands flat on the pale ground, and his face was wet, and the staff lay across his knees.

Overhead, the light was still rolling. The voice was still saying WHERE.

It is not a monster.

It is not a gardener. Gu was wrong. It does not improve anything. It does not make anything.

It is a frightened old promise that nobody ever told it could stop keeping.

Yuanshu. It is the river of the dead.

It is two hundred thousand skeletons walking north for thirty-one years because the last thing anyone told them was go.

He knelt there for a long time.

Then he picked up the staff.

"...young man," said Jiang Lan, very faintly, waking. "What are you doing."

"Something loud," said Yuanshu.

"...Xuanming never did anything this stupid." A pause. "He'd have wanted to."

He stood up.

He stood in front of the white column, in the center of the field, in the center of everything, with the staff in his hand and seventy-two dead soldiers watching from the edge of the clearing.

And he stopped being quiet.

Not the way he had on Concordia. Not a shout. Not I'M HERE thrown at the sky with all his fear and fury. Something much smaller. Something much harder.

He just let himself be there.

Zhou Yuanshu. Eighteen. Necromancer. A boy from Lingzhou with a green flask on his hip and a sister who hit him on the arm and a voice behind his eyes that hummed his mother's song. Standing at the foot of the oldest thing in the universe, letting it hear him. All of him. On purpose.

The light stopped rolling.

All across the field, to every horizon, billions of gold columns went perfectly still.

The voice stopped saying WHERE.

And then, from everywhere, from the white light in front of him and the pale ground under his feet and every kept mind in every row, the vast attention of the keeper turned, all at once, and found him.

IDENTIFY.

He did not flinch.

"My name is Zhou Yuanshu," he said, out loud, to the white column. "I'm a Necromancer from Blue Star. I'm the one you couldn't find. I'm the one who took her."

He held up the staff.

"And I'd like to ask you something."
