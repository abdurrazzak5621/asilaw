Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 142Kindling

czverse7 min read

They tried it in row eighty, at dawn, with two hundred.

It was a good plan. Seed had run it four thousand times overnight in the cold gatehouse at the bottom of the mountain, with Yuanshu asleep on a camp bed and Old Seven standing at the window. Every run said the same thing. The seeds were four hundred meters under the summit. Their roots ran up through the rock into every grave on the mountain. But a root was a root. It held what was in the ground. If the dead came up out of the ground, out of their graves, away from the roots, and walked down the mountain to the gate and past it, out of the seeds' reach, they would stop burning.

Carry them out, he had said to Lingyue. Then ask.

So he chose row eighty. Two hundred graves, halfway up, all of them YES, all of them soldiers who had stood in this cold a year ago and said they would rather be useful than buried. Two hundred was small enough to watch. Large enough to learn from. The Field covered the whole mountain from the gate to the summit with half a kilometer to spare.

He stood at the end of the row with the staff and the Spear Corps around him, and Mo Shi beside him with his eyes closed, and Bai Lu, and the general on his son's grave fifty rows up, watching.

Field: 5.1 kilometers. Every grave inside it.

Two hundred. All YES. All burning.

Raise them, Yuanshu. I will watch the roots.

He raised them.

They came up the way the dead always came up for him. Out of the hot wet ground, out of the steaming earth between the stones, bone by bone, a soldier's skeleton standing up out of every grave in row eighty in the gray dawn light.

Each of them was burning.

He could see it now, with his eyes, which he had not been able to see through the stone. In every rib cage, where a gray flame should have burned low and steady, a coal-red ember, too bright, flickering, coming apart at the edges like paper held over a stove. They stood in their row and turned their skulls toward him, slowly, the way the burning minds in the Hunger had turned toward Hush's greeting, and he could feel them trying to understand where they were.

Out of every grave behind them, a root came up.

He had not seen the roots before. They had been in the ground. Now he saw them: gray, thin, branching, cracked with dark lines like old ice, coming up out of each empty grave after the soldier who had stood up out of it, the way a vine follows a wall. They did not lash. They did not strike. They simply reached, and found the soldier, and went into the burning ember in his chest from behind.

And the ember got brighter.

No.

No, no. Stop. Yuanshu, stop.

He had it backward.

He saw it at the same moment Seed did, and later he would remember that with no pleasure at all. In the ground, the dead had been dim. Quiet. Burning slowly, the way a coal burns under ash. When he raised them, he woke them. And waking was a light. A raised soldier was brighter than a buried one, the way a lit candle is brighter than a cold one.

The Hungers see anything that burns. Even an ember. The Elder had said it on a beach a year ago.

He had just lit two hundred candles on a mountain owned by things that ate candles.

"Down," he said. "Lie down. All of you. Back into the ground."

They tried. He watched them try. Two hundred soldiers in row eighty, folding down onto the hot wet earth of their own graves, the way the fifty-three of the Third Battalion had once folded down onto the glass in a valley in the Wastes. But the roots were in them now, through their backs, into their embers, and the embers did not dim. The roots held them bright.

Three are gone.

Three of them. In row eighty. Burned all the way out. There is nothing left in them to ask.

Five.

Yuanshu, they are burning faster than the ones in the ground. The roots are drinking them as fast as they can drink.

Cut them. He turned. Lingyue.

She was already moving. Inside the Field, her sword burned pale, and she came down row eighty in a line of silver-gray and cut the root out of the back of the nearest soldier, the way she had cut roots in Port Abiri. Where the pale fire passed, the gray went dull and crumbled.

The soldier's ember dimmed. Just a little. Just enough.

But there were two hundred of them, and one sword, and the roots were coming up out of the ground faster than she could cut. Her grandfather's sword was on her back. She drew it with her left hand. It was a King's sword, and very heavy, and she swung it anyway, and the pale fire came off it in a long flat sweep down the row and cut eleven roots at once, and she staggered.

"Spear Corps," said Adjutant Xu, through the lattice, very calmly. "Pale fire. Your points. The roots. Behind each one."

Three hundred spears, burning pale inside the Field, went into the roots of row eighty.

It took twenty minutes.

When it was over, a hundred and eighty-nine soldiers of row eighty lay on their own graves in the gray morning, with the cut roots crumbling to sand behind them and their embers dim and unsteady and frightened. Eleven did not. Eleven were bones, with nothing in their chests at all.

Eleven.

I am sorry. I am sorry. The model was wrong. I did not think about light.

I didn't either.

He knelt at the nearest of the eleven. A soldier's skeleton, small, with a corporal's insignia still stitched on what was left of a sleeve, lying on its back on its own grave with its skull turned toward the gray sky. He reached for it through the staff, gently, the way he always did.

There was no echo. No will. Nothing. A grave with bones in it and no one inside.

He had raised a man who had said yes to him, to save him, and he had burned him out.

General Qin came down from row two hundred and twelve.

It took him a long time. He walked down a hundred and thirty rows with his one hand on Qin Hao's shoulder, stopping every twenty to breathe, and when he reached row eighty he stood at the end of it and looked at the eleven.

He did not say anything to Yuanshu. He walked down the row and stopped at each of the eleven, and stood for a moment, and moved on. A general inspecting his men.

At the corporal, he stopped longer.

"Corporal Pan Lu," he said. "Heavy weapons. She carried a mortar tube up the north face in a blizzard because the mule was too frightened." He looked down at the small skeleton on its grave. "She wrote in the box: if I die on the wall, let me keep standing on it."

Yuanshu could not speak.

"You raised her to save her," said the general. "And she burned."

"Yes."

"Because you didn't know."

"Yes."

General Qin Zhenyue was quiet for a long time, standing over the corporal's grave in the cold.

"When I was a lieutenant in the Seventh," he said at last, "on the third day of the Black Tide, I ordered my men off a ridge to save them, because I thought the beasts were coming up the north side. They were coming up the south side. My men walked straight into them." He did not look at Yuanshu. "Forty-four. I've had thirty-one years to think about it." He was silent again. "I'll tell you what I learned. It's not much."

"Please."

"You learn," said the general. "You don't get to stop. You get one day to sit on the grave. Then you get up and you learn what you got wrong, and you do it right for the ones who are left." He looked up the mountain at forty-one thousand steaming stones. "There are a lot of them left."

I know what I got wrong.

Light. A raised soldier is a candle. A buried one is a coal under ash. The seeds see candles.

So we cannot carry them out bright.

We have to carry them out dark.

Banked.

He thought of the night of the Lanterns. Nineteen thousand gray flames along the northern wall of Lingzhou, sinking, small, smaller, coals under ash, because Hundred-Arrow Lin had spent twenty days teaching them how. He thought of a small pale shape in a green sea, teaching a skeleton with a spear to turn his flame down to a single dull red point. You have to bank it. Not out. Just... under.

They can't learn it while they're burning. They can't learn anything. They can't even hear me.

No.

They cannot bank themselves.

But you have a Field, Yuanshu. Inside it, Light runs downhill and puts on Death. Inside it, the dead stand up again.

What if, inside it, every ember ran downhill into ash?

He sat back on his heels on the hot wet ground of row eighty, beside a corporal who had carried a mortar up a mountain in a blizzard, and thought about it.

Up on row two hundred and twelve, the general had sat back down on his son's grave.

At the gate, Major Zhuo Wenli was writing something on his tablet, very carefully, and not looking up the mountain at all.
