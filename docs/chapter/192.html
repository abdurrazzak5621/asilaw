Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 192The Hundred-and-First

czverse11 min read

Xiaoyu called the city for fifty-one minutes without stopping, and for fifty-one minutes it held.

Not the way it had. Not a wave. Something rougher. Ten walkers along the north wall of Lingzhou with their blank faces looking over the parapet, stepping at random, left, right, back, four slices one way and two the other, and Mo Shi on the marble floor of the banking hall calling every step three seconds ahead in a voice that had gone flat and fast and toneless, and a sixteen-year-old girl beside him with a whistle in her teeth turning every call into a street.

"Tram Yards, now. Breathe. North Market east, now. Old Quarter, Lantern Lane, now. Breathe. River Gate, now. Tram Yards, now..."

Not thirty-one voices. One. On every speaker in the city at once. And two million eight hundred thousand people in the dark learned, in the first ten minutes, that it was no longer their district's voice they were listening for. It was hers. Her voice saying the name of their street.

They learned it.

Seventy-eight percent.

Eighty.

Eighty-three.

They are listening for their street names. Only their street names. When she says it, they go quiet. When she says breathe, they breathe.

She is calling eleven hundred streets. One voice. Every three seconds.

I did not count this. I could not have. Nobody could.

Qin Hao took two in the first half hour.

He did it the way he had in the field, with Su Mingli holding the face down and Lingyue's light falling on him and white fire under his feet, up the back of a walker at Crossing Nine to the knot behind its blank face. Both hands. The white fire going in like water into a tangle of string. And the walker coming apart in a long slow fall of silk over the wall.

The second one was at the river gate. He went up it white-faced, with the fire on his arms guttering, and Ma Lianhua's First Legion held the walker's legs with their bare bones while he climbed, because Su Mingli was still holding the first one's face and could not hold two. He reached the knot. He put his hands on it. The fire went out.

He held on anyway.

He has nothing left. His fire is out. He is holding the knot with his bare hands, nine meters up, on the back of a walker whose face is not held down.

It does not know he is there. It cannot see him. He is behind the lamp.

But if it turns its head...

It turned its head.

Not to look. It was stepping. Random. Left. Its blank face swung three degrees, and the back of its head swung with it, and Qin Hao, nine meters up with his hands on the knot, swung with the back of its head out over the river.

And the white fire came back.

Yuanshu felt it through the Field. Not from his Law. His Law was empty. It came up from somewhere else in Qin Hao, the place where you keep the things you would rather die than fail at. A colonel who had held the inner gate of the Western Pass for four hours with ordinary fire. An old general with one arm, saying his son would have liked to burn something that laughed. A notebook in a breast pocket with FIRST written in it, underlined twice.

White. All the way down both arms.

The knot came undone.

He fell with the silk. The First Legion caught him.

Three.

He is alive. He is unconscious. Bai Lu is with him.

He will not do it again tonight. Perhaps not this year.

Eight left.

At 00:12, the Spear Corps took another at Crossing Six, the way they had taken the first: Lingyue's light holding its face down from the watchtower, two hundred and seventy-four rusted spears going up its back in a column of bone, Old Seven's intent through all of them at once into the knot. Two of them did not let go fast enough when it came undone.

Four. Seven left.

Two hundred and seventy-two.

At 00:21, one of them stepped over the wall.

At the old quarter. Where the wall bent south toward the river, and the old housing blocks came right up under it, and the lanes were narrow and crooked and the buildings were low. A walker standing against the wall there lifted one long woven leg, slowly, the way a man steps over a garden fence, and put it down on the other side. In the city. In a lane. And then the other.

And stood inside Lingzhou, nine meters tall, in the dark, among the low tiled roofs of the old quarter, with its blank face level, stepping.

It is inside.

At close range, its slice is narrow. Fifty meters wide. A street. But it is very bright. It reads everything in the slice.

And the old quarter is where the fourteen hundred are thickest.

Ren is there.

"Ren," said Yuanshu.

"I know." Her voice came up through the Field, through the staff, very faint, very calm. "I can see it from the window. It's two streets away." A pause. "Mister Zhou, there are thirty-eight people in this street who can't be quiet. I'm sitting in all of them. If I get up, they're bright."

"Can you hold?"

"I can hold them," said Shirasagi Ren. "I don't know if I can hold them and me."

The walker in the old quarter stepped. Three degrees. Left. Into Lantern Lane.

"Lantern Lane, now," said Xiaoyu, on every speaker in the city.

Lantern Lane went quiet.

Almost.

It is in the slice. The beam is on the lane. Everyone in the lane has gone quiet except the thirty-eight. And the thirty-eight are dim, because Ren is sitting in them.

And Ren is not dim.

She cannot be. She is holding thirty-eight minds quiet from the inside. A King of Spirit, at full strength, in a slice fifty meters wide.

She is the brightest thing in the lane.

It has stopped stepping.

He felt it hold.

Straight up. On a small room in a house on Lantern Lane, where a King in white robes sat on the end of a dying woman's bed with her eyes shut. He felt Ren's head turn. Three degrees. Then all the way.

She did not let go of the thirty-eight.

It is lifting her.

She is not fighting it. If she fights it, she lets go of them. She is letting it lift her. Slowly. So that it stays on her. So that it does not move to them.

She is doing what the Abbot's Sleepers did. She is holding still so that something can't find anyone else.

Yuanshu, she will be taken.

"Lin," said Captain Lin Wen, on the Sector Nine watchtower.

He heard it. Through the Field. Through Seed. A living woman's voice, very quiet, on the watchtower above the wall, speaking to a drift of ash and dark air beside her with an iron arrowhead for a heart.

"Mother."

Hundred-Arrow Lin was looking at the old quarter.

She had been looking at it since the walker stepped over the wall. A kilometer and a half south-west, among the low tiled roofs, nine meters of silver-white thread standing in a lane in the dark with its blank face held straight down on a single house. She was a shade of ash with a bow that was not really there. She had stood on this watchtower thirty-one years ago, alive, in the Black Tide, and put a hundred arrows into a hundred beasts and not missed, and died here, in the footprints she had worn in the floor.

She says she can reach it.

She says a kilometer and a half is nothing. She says she put an arrow through the eye of a rift-crawler at two kilometers in the Black Tide with a cramp in her hand.

She says one arrow will not do anything. A handful of threads.

She says: but I am not one arrow.

"Hundred-Arrow," said Yuanshu. "The knot needs hundreds of cuts at once. More than the Spear Corps."

She says she knows. She heard Seed say it in the bank.

She says the hundred-and-first comes back to her string every time she fires it. A heartbeat later. She says she has never tried to find out how fast.

She says: I would like to find out now.

"Orlov," said Yuanshu.

The Marshal of the Northern Union was on the wall at Crossing Four, in his greatcoat, with his gloves off, holding the legs of a walker in ice to the knee so that it could not step, while Sokolova's Second Ice Division froze the stone around its feet. He did not turn his head.

"I hear."

"The knot in the old quarter. Can you freeze it?"

"From here?" A pause. "It is two kilometers."

"Can you?"

"I am two hundred years old," said Marshal Orlov. "I have frozen a river from further." Another pause. "Not the knot. The threads around it. So they cannot pull across. For perhaps three seconds." He was already turning. "Tell the archer three seconds."

Captain Lin Wen had not let go of the drift of ash beside her. She had her hand in it, where a hand would be, the way she had stood every night on this wall since the Lanterns.

Lin says: there is a cost.

She says Seed told her, in the bank, about the field. The threads lash when the knot comes undone. They cut what they touch.

She says the hundred-and-first will be in the knot when it comes undone. Coming back to her string. A heartbeat late.

She says: it is my anchor.

She says she is telling you so that you know what she will do with your answer, if you give her one. She says she learned that from the bank too.

She says she is not asking you. She is telling her daughter.

On the watchtower, in the floodlights, Captain Lin Wen looked at the drift of ash beside her for a long time.

"I know," she said.

Her voice did not shake. She was a captain of the Defense Corps. She had been six years old when her mother died on this watchtower, and she had stood on it every night for a year and a half since a boy had knelt by the north slit and pried an iron arrowhead out of a crack in the floor.

"Go on, Mother," she said. "I'll stand here."

At 00:26:14, Marshal Orlov froze the threads around the knot of the walker in Lantern Lane.

Not the knot. The threads. Two kilometers away, a sudden white rime on the silver-white weave at the back of a blank face, the threads going stiff, crackling, the way a rope goes stiff in a hard frost. The knot pulled at them. They did not move.

Three seconds.

Lingyue's light was already falling. From the Sector Nine watchtower, from the point of her grandfather's sword, lit pale, a single heavy drop onto the iron arrowhead nocked on a bow that was not really there.

Hundred-Arrow Lin drew.

And fired.

He did not see the arrow. Nobody saw it. He saw the bow move, a flicker of ash and gray light, and the hundred-and-first was on the string again a heartbeat later, lit pale, and she fired, and it was on the string, and she fired, and it was on the string, and the flicker of her bow became a blur and then not a blur, a steady gray shimmer, like the air over a road in summer, and the sound of it was a single long note like a wire singing in the wind.

Twenty a second.

Forty.

Ninety.

She has never done this. Nobody has. The hundred-and-first comes back to her string and she fires it before it has finished arriving.

A hundred and fifty.

Two hundred.

Into the knot. From a kilometer and a half. Every one.

Two kilometers south-west, in Lantern Lane, the knot of a walker frozen stiff in a white rime took two hundred and eighty-one cuts of pale fire in two point six seconds.

It could not pull across. It could not move.

It came undone.

He felt it go. The walker in Lantern Lane unraveling from the head down in a long slow fall of silk over the low tiled roofs of the old quarter. The beam that had been holding a King in white robes straight up on the end of a dying woman's bed letting go. Ren's head coming down. The thirty-eight minds she had never stopped holding, still dim, still safe.

And the threads lashing.

Blind. Everywhere. The way they always lashed. Through the air over the old quarter, through the falling silk, through the place where a small iron arrowhead, lit pale, was coming back toward a bow on a watchtower a kilometer and a half away, a heartbeat late.

...oh.

On the watchtower, the gray shimmer of the bow stopped.

The drift of ash and dark air beside Captain Lin Wen stood very still. Then it began to come apart. Not violently. Softly. The way smoke comes apart in still air when the fire under it has gone out. Ash and gray light, drifting. Thinning. The shape of a tall narrow woman with a bow that was not really there, standing in the footprints she had worn into the floor thirty-one years ago, going pale at the edges.

Captain Lin Wen did not take her hand out of it.

She says: Wen.

She says: I got it.

She says: you were six. You said you'd be an archer. You were a terrible archer.

She says: you were a very good captain.

And then there was no drift of ash on the watchtower. There was a captain of the Defense Corps standing in the floodlights with her hand held out in empty air, where a hand would be, and on the stone at her feet, very small, the footprints a woman had worn into the floor of a watchtower by standing in them for a long time, a long time ago.

Five.

Six left.

Hundred-Arrow Lin. The Hundred-and-First. Arrow Intent twenty-four percent.

She was the third commander I ever helped forge.

I am writing her name at the top of the wall. Not at the bottom. At the top. Above the first column. Where Xiaoyu cannot reach it without the tall ladder.

I am going to ask her to get the tall ladder.
