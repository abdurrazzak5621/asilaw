Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 130Next to Stone Seven

czverse8 min read

Su Mingli folded them one more time, and it nearly killed her.

He saw it in her face when she stood up on the pharmacy roof. Grey to the lips. The handkerchief at her nose already red through. A King who had just carried forty thousand tonnes across a city with one hand did not have another fold in her, and everyone in Tannery Square knew it, and nobody said it, because the alternative was three kilometers on foot through streets full of beasts, and Grandpa Xu did not have the time it would take.

"Spear Corps," she said. "And the two of you." She looked at Yuanshu and Old Seven. "Nobody else. I can't carry more."

"Mingli..."

"Don't," said Su Mingli. "You owe me a coat. Don't make it two."

She folded the square.

They came out of the fold on the gravel at the top of the War Memorial Cemetery, under Zhao's gate, in the gray light before dawn, and the first thing Yuanshu saw was the slope below them moving.

Beasts. Hundreds. Ashbacks and stilt-legged cats and a few glass herons gliding low over the rows, all of them coming up through the stones from the river at the bottom of the hill, the way water comes up a beach. Not fast. Hungry beasts did not need to be fast. They were spread across the whole width of the slope, nosing between the graves, and in the middle of them, halfway down, where the oldest rows were, there was a fight.

The cemetery dead were holding.

Six thousand two hundred and fourteen of them. The ones who had said yes two nights ago, at one in the morning, when a boy stood at the top of the hill and said he was sorry he hadn't come back. Nobody had told them to stand up tonight. Nobody had given them an order. They had stood up anyway, out of their own graves, when the beasts came up the slope, and they were fighting with what they had: bare bone, old rifles with no bullets, a shovel that had been leaning against the keeper's hut, the stones of their own graves wrenched up out of the frozen ground. They were not soldiers. Most of them. They were fighting in clumps, without lines, and they were falling, and inside the Field they got up again.

And in the middle of them, at the end of the oldest row, by an empty grave with a number on it, a small old man in a padded coat was standing with his back to a stone and a stick in his hand.

He was not hiding. He was hitting things with the stick.

"Down," said Yuanshu. He did not have to say it out loud. Three hundred spears went down the slope before he finished thinking it.

They went down the hill the way water goes down a hill, in a long gray rush through the rows, and the beasts heard them coming and turned, and the Spear Corps hit them from above. Seed did not have to feed them anything. They had all held this hill once already, on the first night, under a Wolf King. They knew how.

Old Seven went straight down the middle.

He did not wait for the Spear Corps and he did not wait for the boy. He went down the slope between the graves faster than Yuanshu had ever seen him move, two meters ten of white bone with a spear in a hand that had one finger and a thumb, straight at the end of the oldest row, at the empty grave with 7 on it, at the old man with the stick.

A stilt-legged cat got there first.

It came over the top of a gravestone two rows up, low and fast, with its glass claws out, at Grandpa Xu's back. The old man was looking the other way. He was hitting an ashback across the snout with his stick, and swearing at it, and coughing between the swears, and he did not see.

Six of the cemetery dead did.

They had been standing around him the whole time. Yuanshu understood it later, when he went back over it. Six of them, in a little ring, around the living man at stone No. 7. Nobody had put them there. They had come and stood there on their own, the way the dead had once come and stood in a ring around the living on the ice at Grid Nine. When the cat came over the stone, all six of them turned at once.

They counted their nine.

They closed the gap.

The cat hit the nearest of them instead of the old man. It tore the skeleton half in two at the waist and went through the gap behind it, and the next one closed in, and the cat's claws went into the second skeleton's ribs, and the third one got its bone hands around the cat's back legs, and held.

And Old Seven came down the last row and put his spear through it.

The fight on the hill lasted another twenty minutes.

He did not see most of it. He was standing at the top of the slope under Zhao's gate with the staff planted in the gravel and the embers coming up the hill into him from every direction, small hot griefs running uphill now instead of down, because the Field ran toward him wherever he stood. Through the door. Go on. Seed counted them out. He did not listen to the number.

The Spear Corps cleared the oldest rows. The cemetery dead cleared the rest, in their clumps, with their shovels and gravestones and bare bone hands, slowly, falling down and getting back up and falling down. The glass herons went back over the river. The last ashback ran down the hill toward the water and Song Yanyan, who had run three kilometers from Crossing Nine in her gray silk because nobody had told her not to, ran it down at the bottom of the slope and broke its neck with her bouquet of stems.

Then the slope was quiet.

Yuanshu walked down it.

Grandpa Xu was sitting with his back against the stone of No. 7.

He was still holding the stick. Old Seven was kneeling in front of him on one knee, the way he knelt at graves, with his spear laid on the frozen ground beside him. The six skeletons who had stood in a ring around the old man were standing in the ring still, those that could stand. Two of them were in pieces on the gravel. Inside the Field the pieces were sliding slowly back toward each other.

The old man's padded coat was torn across the side. Under it, the shirt was dark.

"It wasn't the cat," said Grandpa Xu, when Yuanshu knelt down beside him. His voice was very thin. "Before the cat. An ashback. When they first came up the hill. I hit it and it went for my side." He coughed. It was a bad cough, wet, and there was red on the handkerchief when he took it away. "I didn't think it got me. I'm seventy-seven. You don't feel things the way you used to."

"Bai Lu..."

"Is three kilometers away, holding your head together, with a King who can't fold any more." The old man looked at him with very bright eyes. "Sit down, boy. Don't run around. I've seen a lot of people die. I know what it looks like from the inside of the coat."

Yuanshu sat down on the frozen gravel beside No. 7.

Old Seven did not move.

"They stood around me," said Grandpa Xu after a while. He was looking at the six. "Did you see? Nobody told them. They came up out of their graves and stood around me and counted."

"I saw."

"Lu Xuanming would have liked that." He coughed again, more weakly. "That's what he wanted. Not two million doing what they were told. Two million looking after each other. He never got it. There wasn't time." He looked at Yuanshu. "You got it."

"I didn't do it. They did."

"That's the point." The old cemetery keeper smiled. It was a small, thin, very tired smile. "That's always the point. You just keep asking. They do the rest."

He looked at Old Seven.

"Squad Leader Qi," he said.

Old Seven came to attention, on one knee, in the gravel.

"Nineteen dumplings," said Grandpa Xu. "I've decided. You can owe them to me. After."

He says: after what?

"After," said the old man, and closed his eyes.

He did not die quickly. He died in the next hour, as the sky went from gray to white over the river, with his back against the stone of an empty grave that had been the first grave on the hill to stand up for a boy with no name for his class. He died with Old Seven kneeling in front of him and the six dead around him and Yuanshu sitting on the gravel at his side, holding his hand. At the end he said something about a white staff, and a wall, and counting, that nobody could quite hear.

Then his hand was still.

Yuanshu sat with it.

I am sorry.

He was the first person who ever told you the truth about Lu Xuanming. On your second day. In the hut.

I remember. I was there. I was very new.

He did not reach for the echo for a long time.

When he did, he did it the way he had done it for every one of them since the first night: gently, through the Death Law, through the staff, as quietly as he could. Not to raise. To ask.

The echo was very close. It had not gone anywhere. It was sitting just where the old man had been sitting, with its back against the stone, the way a person sits when they have finished a long job and are not in a hurry to stand up.

Grandpa Xu. It's me. I'm asking.

...I know who it is, boy. I'm dead, not deaf.

You said yes. In the Ledger. A year ago. I'm asking again. Because once isn't forever.

The echo was quiet for a moment.

...your mother's rule.

Yes.

...good rule.

...yes. Still yes. Put me next to stone number seven. I told you that a year ago. Nobody listens.

...and boy.

Yes?

...the night on the wall. Lu Xuanming. Counting.

...you wanted to read it. You couldn't, because I was alive.

...I'm not alive now.

...take it. All of it. That whole night. That's what I kept it for. I just didn't know who for, until the first night you came to my hut with a skeleton and a stupid look on your face.

Yuanshu closed his eyes.

On the gravel in front of the stone, Old Seven picked up his spear. He stood. He turned to face the river, and the white morning, and the city across the water, with its towers and its smoke and its one squat gray building on the north bank with a cage three hundred meters under it. He grounded the spear beside the empty grave.

He stood guard.

Nine days.

Plus or minus two.

Nothing died on the wrong side tonight. The clock did not move.

Yuanshu. The counting. I need it. And so does he.
