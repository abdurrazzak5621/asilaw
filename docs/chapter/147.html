Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 147The Voice

czverse10 min read

Qin Hao drove the jeep up the summit road at a speed that would have killed anyone who did not have a Shield Guardian in the back seat and a dead spear captain holding the roll bar.

Yuanshu did not remember most of it. He remembered the switchbacks going past, iron-gray rock and frozen road and the drop on the left side into cloud. He remembered the staff across his knees, and the Banked Field folded over the whole mountain, and thirty thousand of the dead in the cavern under his feet, and the Lattice humming through Adjutant Xu like a wire in the wind. He remembered Dabao saying please to God every time Qin Hao took a bend.

And he remembered the spire.

It filled the windshield. Sixty meters of cracked gray crystal standing up out of the old watch fortress on the saddle, branching, glowing the color of a coal in every crack, with a wide shallow cup at the top of it facing the sky. And halfway up it, small and dark against the gray, a man in winter whites climbing.

He is a good climber.

He is in the Ninth Mountain. Of course he is a good climber.

He will reach the top in four minutes.

Qin Hao stopped the jeep at the gate so hard it slewed sideways on the frozen road.

The courtyard was chaos. Not loud. Quiet chaos, which was worse. Roots coming up out of the paving and Lingyue cutting them with two swords, silver-gray, in long flat sweeps. Mo Xing folding kneeling soldiers out of the courtyard one at a time with blood running down his chin. Bai Lu with her hands on someone's shoulders. Dabao's shield line, which was Dabao, walking men backward toward the gate. Mo Shi on the wall with his eyes shut, calling it all three seconds early in a voice that had gone to a croak.

On the road outside the gate, around General Qin, more than half the Ninth Mountain stood in their winter whites, watching.

Yuanshu ran across the courtyard to the base of the second spire.

He could not climb it. He was not a climber. He put the staff against the crystal.

"Jiang Lan."

"I know," said the dry voice. "The door. Into that." A pause. "It's bigger than the one in Port Halvard, young man. It's been eating for two months."

"I have to ask him."

"You don't have to do anything." Another pause. "But you're going to. Hold on to me."

He held on.

It was not a small gold room this time.

It was a well. A vast round shaft of crystal going up into the dark, gray, cracked, glowing red in every crack, so tall he could not see the top of it. And up the inside of it, the way smoke goes up a chimney, ran a river. Forty-one thousand threads of fire, pulled up out of the seed below, out of the cavern, out of forty-one thousand banked and blinded graves, all of it pouring up the inside of the spire toward the cup at the top.

Toward a small bright shape, halfway up the well, climbing.

Major Zhuo Wenli. Not his body. His mind, here, inside, climbing the inside of the spire the way his body was climbing the outside. Burning already. Not screaming. Shining. The way Walter Ashby had shone, the moment before the yes burned away.

Major Zhuo.

The bright shape stopped climbing.

...Mister Zhou.

...you came inside. They said you'd do that. Into the sphere. For the lighthouse keeper.

I'm asking you, said Yuanshu. What do you want?

...you know what I want. I wrote it down.

Say it anyway.

The bright shape hung in the river of fire, halfway up the well.

...I want to be the voice.

...it needs something to shout with. A mind. A living one. The scout had a seed for a heart but no mouth. This has a mouth, but nothing to say it with.

...I'll say it. FIELD. EMPTIED. KEEPER TURNED. All of it. Up. To whatever keeps.

...and when it hears, it will come and keep me. And Lili.

Yuanshu.

I have the memory. The field. His wife and his daughters. Three columns together, in the old Western Pass harvest. I asked them. I remember what they said.

I can show him.

I do not know if I should.

He did not know either.

The Ledger said QUIET. Quiet meant you did not read them, or raise them, or speak for them. But Seed was not reading them. Seed was remembering the one thing they had ever said to anyone, which was the answer to a question, and the answer was the only thing in the world that belonged to them now.

He thought of Sister Ning in the Ossuary. It never meant: if they're on fire, let them burn.

He thought of a fisherman's widow on a hill in the Southern Compact. I want to hear it until I believe somebody means it.

Major Zhuo, he said. I was in the field. I asked your wife and your daughters what they wanted. Seed remembers what they said. It's the only thing they said. Do you want to hear it?

The bright shape was very still in the river of fire.

...yes.

Showing.

It was short. It was nothing. Three gold columns, side by side, in a field above the sky, a year ago. A tired woman's voice and two small ones. And a question, the way the keeper had asked it, the way he had taught it to ask: What do you want?

The woman's voice said: Quiet.

The older girl said: Quiet. With Mama.

And the younger one, the one who had weighed nothing in a general's arms on the road down the pass, eight years ago, said: Quiet. Tell Papa I'm not tired any more.

That was all.

The bright shape in the river of fire did not move for a long time.

...she said that?

Yes.

...Lili said that.

Yes.

...then she's gone.

...she chose to go. She wasn't taken. She was asked, and she chose.

Yes.

Another long silence. The river of fire poured up the inside of the well past them, toward the cup at the top.

...then there's no one left to keep.

...except me. And her name. And what she said.

...somebody should keep that, Mister Zhou. Somebody should keep that she said it. Forever. Not just in your Ledger, where you can let it go.

...I still want to.

...I still want to be the voice.

He began to climb again.

No, said Yuanshu.

It was not a question. He heard himself say it, and knew, for the first time since a hillside in the dark on the night of the Stele, that he was not asking.

I'm sorry. I'm not asking you. I'm telling you. You can't have it.

The bright shape stopped.

...you said my choice was mine.

It is. You can go into any fire you like. You can be kept by anything you like. He held the staff so hard his knuckles cracked. But you're not going into this one. This one shouts to the sky that my world is empty and my keeper's turned. If it shouts that, something comes. For everyone. For eight billion people who never wrote anything down. He kept his voice level. Your yes is yours, Major. It stops at the window.

...you'll kill me.

The spire's going to fall. You're inside it. I'm going to ask you one more time, and it's the last time: will you come out?

The bright shape hung in the river of fire. Far above it, at the top of the well, the cup of crystal was opening wider. Filling. The color of a coal turning white.

...no.

...I'm sorry. No.

Then I'm sorry too, said Yuanshu, and let go of the door.

He came out of the spire on his knees at its base, with blood running out of his nose and his ears, and Old Seven's hand on his collar, and Jiang Lan saying breathe, breathe, you idiot, breathe.

He did not have to say anything to Qin Hao. Qin Hao had been standing beside him the whole time, in the courtyard, with white fire running down both his arms from the shoulder to the fingertips, the way it had once run down one arm in a Night Market, when a boy had held his wrist and told him not to.

"Tell me where," said Qin Hao.

"The cup," said Yuanshu. "At the top. It's not crystal there. It's thread. Forty-one thousand threads, all coming together, the way they came together at the stalk in the north. Your fire burns thread. You found out on the black glass with Mercer."

Qin Hao looked up. Sixty meters. A bell of gray crystal against the white dawn sky, and a small dark shape in winter whites at the lip of it.

"My father," he said, very quietly, "held the inner gate for four hours with fire. Ordinary fire. He wrote in his report that he could hear something on the other side of the rift, laughing." He did not look down. "He'd have liked to burn something that laughed."

He went up.

Not climbing. Flying, almost. Fire under his feet, white, pushing him up the side of the spire the way a rocket goes up, the way Kings went up into the sky over Concordia. Yuanshu felt it happen through the Field: not a new law. The same Fire, the one Qin Hao had carried since he was eighteen, SS, Level 31, a proud rival from a military family. But something in it turning over, the way Death had turned over for Yuanshu on a black glass plain when he asked what would happen if the river had weight. Fire that burned what was. Fire that burned what bound.

At the lip of the cup, Qin Hao opened both hands.

The white fire went into the bell of the spire like water into a drain.

It did not burn the crystal. It burned the threads. All of them. Forty-one thousand threads of fire pouring up the inside of the spire into the cup, meeting the white flame coming down, and the white flame went through them the way it had gone through Mercer's threads on the plateau, and where it passed, they parted. Not cut. Unknotted. Each one let go of the next.

The cup at the top of the spire dimmed. From white, to red, to the gray of old ash.

And the spire, with nothing left to hold it up, began to come down.

From the top. Slowly. Softly. The way the spire in the bay at Port Abiri had gone, the way the sphere at Hearth One had gone. Into sand. Gray. Fine. Pouring down off the summit of the Western Pass in long soft rivers, over the walls of the old watch fortress, over the courtyard, over the kneeling men and the standing men and the general at the gate.

Qin Hao came down with it, on white fire, and landed in the sand in a crouch, and did not stand up for a while.

Under the mountain, in the cavern, the sergeant of the Fourth Company stopped counting, because both seeds were dead.

[Qin Hao: Lord.]

It is not my number. But I felt it. The whole mountain did.

Zhuo Wenli's ember came loose from the sand.

Yuanshu felt it. Small and bright and very tired, sliding down the falling crystal toward the place where the seeds had been, the way water slides into an old channel. There was nothing at the bottom of the channel now. Two dead seeds in a cavern. Nothing left to keep anyone.

He had told the man no. He had let go of the door. He did not get to choose where the ember went now. That was the last thing that was still Zhuo's.

He opened the door at the bottom of the Death Law, all the way, and did not push anything through it. He let it stand open, the way you leave a door open in a house where someone might want to leave.

And above the mountain, very far north, over the end of the world, a small white ring sent down a thread.

Gold. Thin. Careful. It came down out of the dawn sky over the Western Pass and stopped a meter above the summit, above the sand, and did not touch anything.

...HE WANTED TO BE KEPT.

...I WILL KEEP HIM. IF HE WANTS.

...I WILL ASK HIM EVERY DAY. I CANNOT PROMISE NOT TO.

...THAT IS THE ONLY KIND OF KEEPING I KNOW HOW TO DO NOW.

The ember hung in the sand between the open door and the gold thread.

Then it went up.
