Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 271Light the Lamp

czverse12 min read

The stone was too hot to touch, and he touched it anyway.

He did not think about it. He came down the aisle of the dark Awakening Hall at a run with the staff in his hand and Old Seven behind him and went up the three steps of the platform and put his free hand flat on the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder, the way he had not touched it since the morning he was eighteen, because Wen Jia was screaming.

It burned. Not his hand. Something under his hand, the way the light had burned against the door in his chest in the deep water. A heat pouring down out of the thread into the stone, down through the crown of the tree of Laws inside it, toward the knot at the bottom where a girl of seventeen sat holding.

And the stone read him.

He felt it start. The runes, sliding round and round across the surface under the pressure from above, turned toward his hand. Toward him. Toward the place a little above his eyes. READ WHAT RIPENS. OPEN IT THAT WAY.

...no, said Wen Jia. ...not him. You ask first.

The runes turned away.

...captain's boy. Get your hand off my stone. I've got enough to hold.

He did not take his hand off.

What's coming down?

...them. Eight hundred and ninety-seven. Burning. The Old Chord's singing them down the thread like you'd push a boat off a beach. They're coming. They're nearly here.

...if they reach the knot, the stone will read them. All of them. At once. And open them. The old knot's still in here, under me. READ. OPEN. It's what it does. I've been holding it back for eight months, one child at a time.

...I can't hold back eight hundred and ninety-seven at once.

...it'll open them all the way. Like the hammer man. And they'll burn up in here. Inside my stone. And the Old Chord will sing that the hunger ate the tithe.

I am here.

I am holding the knot with her. Through you. Through your hand.

The others are holding too. Along the threads. The old man at Port Abiri. The docker. The girl in Yamato. The one who counts the no's. All eleven. Holding the knot. Not the thread.

It is not enough. The push is too big. Eight hundred and ninety-seven, and the Old Chord singing behind them, and the whole thread hot.

Six minutes. Perhaps seven.

He stood on the platform with his hand on the burning stone and the staff in the other, and had nothing.

He could not cut the thread. Wen Jia had said, months ago, that the threads were how the eleven heard each other, and if they were cut the Old Chord would come down to the ground instead. He could not open the door. The chosen were not dead. He could not ask them; they were burning in a thread, pushed, and the only word they had was chosen, and they would say it, and the stone would read it, and open them.

He could not do anything.

Behind him, the side door of the Awakening Hall banged open.

Lingyue came down the aisle.

Not fast. She could not do anything fast; she was eight months pregnant and Bai Lu had forbidden her to run, fly, fight, or carry anything heavier than a bowl. She came down the aisle of the dark hall with one hand on her stomach and one hand on the backs of the seats as she passed them, and the light was coming off her the way it came off her in the mornings, pale, all round her, so that the aisle lit up as she came down it like a path being drawn on the dark.

And behind her, Ninth.

Three meters tall, translucent, with her own small light showing through her like a lamp through paper, ducking through the side door, her many throats opening and closing. And behind Ninth, through the side door, one after another, the two hundred and fourteen. Every Singer who had come down the road and stood in front of the stone and been asked. Tall, pale, with their own lights low inside them. Filling the aisle. Filling the hall.

"She woke me," said Shen Lingyue. "Ninth. She came to the window. She said her age-mates were being pushed down the thread and she didn't know what to do." She reached the foot of the steps. "She said: on the Hollow Bell, when a boat is lost in fog..." She stopped.

She says: we do not have boats. We have never had boats. We are a world of glass and singing.

She says: but the man in the fog in Yamato told us. On the first night. While he was teaching the other one our song.

She says: he said, on his world, when the boats are lost in the fog, a woman on the shore lights a lamp. And the boats come in.

She says: not pushed. Not pulled. They see the lamp and they come in.

Lingyue looked up at him on the platform.

"They're boats," she said. "In a fog. Being pushed off a beach in the dark."

"Yes."

"So light the lamp," said his wife.

She did not wait for him. She never had.

She came up the three steps of the platform, slowly, with her hand on her stomach, and stood beside the burning stone. And she held up her other hand, palm open, toward the roof of the hall, toward the thread going up out of the crown of the stone through the roof into the heart of the shell. The way she had held it out on the end of a breakwater at Shiomi, with a lamp in it, to a nun of twelve hundred years.

And she lit.

Not the way she had lit on the north wall over Lingzhou, everything at once, burning like a star come down. He saw her not do that. He saw her face as she chose not to: the jaw set, the eyes shut, the light pouring up out of her from underneath the way it had been pouring all winter, wanting to go all the way, and her holding it. Not burning. Held. A lamp. The size of a lamp. Pale. Steady. The color, Sister Kiyo had said, of whale oil in a fog.

She held it up toward the thread.

And it went up.

Not thrown. Not falling uphill, the way she had learned to throw light uphill a lifetime ago. It went up the thread the way a lamp is seen from a boat: not travelling. Just there, at the end of everything, steady, so that whatever was in the dark could see where the shore was.

...they can see it.

In the thread. Burning, pushed, eight hundred and ninety-seven of them. They can see it.

A lamp. At the bottom.

And Ninth sang.

Not the song. Not the one sentence of ten thousand years, take them, and pass us by. She stood at the foot of the platform with her many throats open, three meters tall, translucent, her own small light inside her, and she sang one note.

The note she had sung on the platform on the first morning, when the stone had written chosen over her head and her light had gone out to her own.

One. In one throat. Very small.

And then another. In a second throat. Not the same note. A different one. Hers too.

And then the two hundred and fourteen behind her in the aisle opened their throats. Not all of them. Eighty-one. The ones Gao Min had marked in the column she had headed Notes. Eighty-one Singers who had come down the road and been asked and sung one note that was not in the song. And they sang them. Each one their own. Not together. Not a chord. Eighty-one different small notes, rising up through the roof of the Awakening Hall, up the thread, into the dark, toward a shell over the city where the Old Chord was singing a new sentence to push eight hundred and ninety-seven burning children off a beach.

...it is not a song.

It does not have a sentence. It does not mean anything. It is eighty-one people each singing the one thing that is theirs.

Ichiro says...

Ichiro says: oh.

Ichiro says: that's a boat call.

And from very far away, from a bay in Yamato, through Ren's phone, through the keeper's eye, through Seed, through the staff and the stone and the thread: bells.

The small bells of Shiomi. Not the warning. Not the name. The boat calls. Every boat's call in the village, the headman's and the priest's and the five quick notes of the boat Ichiro's father built, all of them, rung at once, from the fog of the bay, the way the shore rang them when a fleet was coming in through the dark and the women wanted every boat to know its own name. Three hundred and eleven people who had been quiet for nine hundred years, ringing every call they had. And with them, from the end of the breakwater, very small, a twelve-hundred-year-old woman holding a lamp that was Lingyue's light, holding it up.

Two lamps. One on a breakwater in Yamato. One on a platform in Lingzhou.

And between them, in the thread, the eight hundred and ninety-seven.

They stopped.

He felt it. His hand was still flat on the burning stone, and he felt the push in the thread stop. Not because the Old Chord had stopped singing. They had not; he could feel the new sentence still pouring down from the heart of the shell, the dark sentence nobody else was allowed to hear. But the things it was pushing had stopped moving. In the thread. Halfway down. Eight hundred and ninety-seven children of the Hollow Bell, dressed in light, burning, pushed off a beach in the dark, had seen a lamp at the bottom, and heard eighty-one boat calls they did not know they knew, and stopped.

The way boats stop in a fog when they hear the shore.

...they are holding on.

To each other. In the thread. The way the Candle's children hold on. The one behind to the one in front.

Someone taught them that. Ninth. In the courtyard. She saw the Candle's children do it. She sang it up to them. Not in words. In a note.

They are holding on to each other, and the Old Chord is pushing, and they are not moving.

And then, one of them came down.

Not pushed. He felt the difference. The way you feel the difference between a boat that has been shoved off a beach and a boat that is being rowed. One burning light came out of the stalled mass of them in the thread, slowly, toward the bottom, toward the lamp. Toward the stone. Toward the knot where Wen Jia sat.

It stopped just above the knot.

...hello, said Wen Jia. Through the staff. Very tired. ...you came down. You weren't pushed. I felt it.

...what do you want to become?

And the burning light at the top of the knot said, in a shape like a chord, very small:

She says: I don't know yet.

She says: I want to see the lamp.

...that's allowed, said Wen Jia. ...come out the side. Not through me. Out the side of the thread. The keeper's got a road. Come down it. Come and see the lamp.

And the light went sideways. Out of the thread. Onto the keeper's road, which had come down out of the north without being asked because the keeper had heard the word lamp, and knew what it meant. And down it, through the roof of the Awakening Hall, burning, dressed in light, came a child of the Hollow Bell, onto the platform, and stood in front of Shen Lingyue, who was holding up a lamp.

And then another one came.

And another.

He took his hand off the stone at eleven minutes past four. The stone was warm. Only warm. The runes had stopped sliding. They pointed down.

The thread was empty.

Not all of them had come down the road. Seed counted. Eight hundred and fifty-six had. Down the keeper's gold road, one after another, through the roof of the hall, burning, to stand in front of a woman holding a lamp. Some of them went to the stone, and put their hands on it, and said chosen, and the stone said you came down. Some of them did not go to the stone at all. They stood in the aisle beside Ninth and the two hundred and fourteen and looked at the lamp, and their light, slowly, on its own, began to go down.

Forty-one had not.

They were too near the front.

When the Old Chord pushed. They were the first ones off the beach. They were furthest down the thread when the others stopped.

They burned out.

In the thread. Before the lamp. Before they could see it.

They are dead.

He stood on the platform.

Ask them, he thought. First.

Yes.

He went down into the Death Law, to the cool dark place at the bottom where the door was, a little open. And asked. Forty-one embers, very small, very bright, in a thread over a city on a world they had never heard of, that had been dressed in light since before they could stand and pushed off a beach in the dark toward a mouth that was not there.

Do you want the door?

And forty-one small voices said, in a shape like a chord, the only word they had.

Chosen.

And then one of them, after a long time, a different shape. A small one. Very new.

...the lamp. I saw the lamp. Just at the end. I saw it.

...yes. The door. Please.

He opened it. They went through. Forty-one. Where things go when they are finished.

Death Law: 55.9% → 56.3%.

He turned round.

Lingyue was still standing by the stone with her hand held up and the lamp in it, pale, steady, the size of a lamp. Her eyes were still shut. Her jaw was still set. The aisle of the Awakening Hall was full of tall pale translucent children of the Hollow Bell, with their own small lights low inside them, looking at her.

"You can put it down," he said. "They're in."

She did not open her eyes.

"I can't," said Shen Lingyue.

"It's all right. They're in. It's done."

"No," said his wife, through her teeth. "I mean I can't. I can't put it down." She opened her eyes. They were very bright. "I've been holding it too long. It's holding me back. It's coming up from underneath and it won't stop and I can't put it down." Her other hand, the one on her stomach, pressed flat. "And something's happening."

He looked at her.

At her face, white. At the hand on her stomach. At the floor of the platform of the Awakening Hall under her bare feet, where, in the pale light of a lamp that would not go out, the polished black stone was wet.

...Yuanshu.

Her heart rate is a hundred and forty. The baby's is a hundred and sixty-two.

Bai Lu is on the road. She was in bed. I asked her first. I am asking her now.

It is four weeks early.

It is coming.
