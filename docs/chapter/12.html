Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 12Heishui

czverse9 min read

Heishui meant black water, and the town had earned the name.

It sat at the bottom of a narrow valley ninety kilometers west of Lingzhou, a long gray smear of concrete houses running along a river the color of strong tea. Coal dust lay on everything. On the roofs, on the parked trucks, on the leaves of the few trees along the main road, so that even the green looked gray. At the head of the valley, three tall towers of rusted steel stood over the mine shafts like the masts of a sunken ship, and on the hillside above them, a long white sign glowed against the dark.

HEISHUI COAL & POWER. 731 DAYS WITHOUT A FATALITY.

They arrived after midnight, in an old delivery van that General Han had found from somewhere, with no markings on it at all.

The woman who had sent the photograph lived in the last house before the mine fence.

She opened the door before they knocked. She had been waiting at the window. She was small and thin, maybe forty, in a cardigan buttoned wrong, and she looked at Yuanshu in the doorway, and then past him, at the tall shape in the dark at the edge of the yard with the gray flame in its chest, and she put her hand on the doorframe to steady herself.

"You came," she said. "I didn't think... I sent it to the wrong address four times."

"Mrs. Pan?"

She nodded. She let them in.

The house was one room and a kitchen. On a small shelf on the wall stood a framed photograph of a broad, cheerful man in a hard hat, grinning so wide you could see a gold tooth flash on the left side. In front of the photo, a stick of incense had burned down to a gray thread of ash.

"Pan Guolin," she said. "Two years ago. The roof came down in Number Three." Her hands were knotted together in front of her. "They said they couldn't get him out. Too dangerous. They said they'd sealed the tunnel and he'd be at peace." She stopped. Her mouth worked. "Then last month my cousin got a job in Number Three. Night shift. He came home white. He said there were men in the tunnels who weren't men. He took a picture." She looked at the photograph on the shelf. "I made him show me. I made him make it bigger. Over and over." Her voice cracked. "And in the third cart, the one bending down. On the left side of the jaw."

"A gold tooth," said Yuanshu.

She nodded and couldn't speak.

Dabao waited with the van. He had insisted on coming, and Yuanshu had let him, on the condition that he stay outside the fence and keep the engine running. Dabao had agreed in a way that suggested he would not.

The fence was three meters of chain link with razor wire on top, and Old Seven simply cut it. One stroke of the spear, top to bottom, and the wire parted with a soft musical twang, like a guitar string.

They crossed the yard in the dark, between piles of coal as tall as houses.

The head of Number Three shaft was a steel shed with a lift cage inside it, hanging from a cable over a square black hole. It was not guarded. It didn't need to be. No one in Heishui wanted to go down Number Three at night anymore.

The cage dropped.

It went down for a long time.

Rock walls slid past the bars, black and wet and close enough to touch. The air grew warmer and thicker and began to taste of metal and wet stone. Somewhere far below, getting louder, came a sound.

A rhythm. Clack-clack. Clack-clack. Steel wheels on steel rails.

And under it, steady, almost too low to hear, a hum. Like a refrigerator in an empty kitchen.

I can feel it.

A shouting core. Running. Somewhere below.

It is much stronger than the one in Bonefield Hollow.

The cage stopped.

The tunnel in front of them was lit.

Not by lamps. Violet light came from deeper in, pulsing, slow, the way the core in the canyon had pulsed: a heartbeat made of light. It washed along the black glistening walls of the tunnel in waves, and in each wave, for a moment, you could see everything.

The rail track, running away into the dark.

The coal carts, a long line of them, each heaped with black rock.

And the workers.

They came out of the dark in a row, pushing the carts. Skeletons. Human. Forty, fifty, more, filing past one after another, bones gray-black with coal dust, bent over the cart handles at exactly the same angle, pushing at exactly the same speed. Clack-clack. Clack-clack. Some still had scraps of overalls hanging from their ribs. One still wore a hard hat, cracked across the crown.

In every eye socket, a hard little point of violet.

They did not look up as the cage opened. They did not look at anything. They just pushed, in the same silent rhythm, toward the far end of the tunnel, where the carts tipped their loads onto a conveyor and turned around and came back empty and went down into the dark again.

Old Seven stepped out of the cage.

He stood in the middle of the track, and the gray flame in his chest flared so bright it threw his shadow up the tunnel wall, and from somewhere deep in his chest came a sound Yuanshu had never heard him make. A low, grinding, bone-on-bone creak.

Like a growl.

I am not going to translate that.

The core was at the end of the tunnel, where it opened into a big chamber.

It was bolted into the rock wall in a housing of steel, like a fuse box, with cables running out of it. A crystal the size of a man's head, pulsing violet. Every pulse sent a wave of light down the tunnels. Every wave carried the command. Yuanshu could feel it, the same as in the canyon. Hammering.

MOVE.

MOVE.

MOVE.

On the steel door of the housing, someone had stenciled a serial number and a company logo. And underneath the logo, burned into the face of the crystal itself, so small you had to be close to see it, was a ring of tiny open mouths.

All screaming.

The same mark.

This one was not placed in a canyon by something wild.

It was installed. With bolts.

Two men sat at a folding table near the conveyor belt, in company overalls, with a thermos and a tablet between them, watching a football match with the sound turned low. Night supervisors. One of them looked up, saw a young man in a gray jacket standing next to the core housing and a skeleton with a gray flame in its chest standing in the middle of the track, and dropped his cup.

"Hey," he said. Then, louder, getting up: "Hey! You can't..."

Old Seven turned his skull toward him.

The man sat back down.

Yuanshu put his hand on the steel housing. It was warm, and it vibrated, faintly, with every pulse.

He didn't try to break it. He didn't try to shout louder. He reached past the core, past the hammering violet threads, down into the gray place where the river ran home. And he opened the door, the way he had in Bonefield Hollow.

Rest.

Lie down. You can stop now. It's over.

Returning.

Down the tunnel, the clacking stopped.

All at once. Fifty carts rolled to a halt on the rails with a long squeal of iron, and in the sudden silence, the workers let go of the handles.

They didn't fall. They lay down.

One after another, down the whole length of the tunnel, the skeletons of Heishui No. 3 knelt beside their carts, and lowered themselves onto the black floor between the rails, carefully, the way an old man lies down on a bed. The violet light in their eyes flickered, and went out, and did not come back.

Behind Yuanshu, the core in its steel box pulsed once more, harder, as if confused. Then its light stuttered, and it went dark.

The hum stopped.

In the silence you could hear water dripping somewhere in the rock. And the small, tinny voice of the football commentator on the supervisors' tablet, saying that someone had missed a penalty.

He found the third cart.

The skeleton beside it lay on its side between the rails, one arm still bent as if holding a handle. A broad skull. Big shoulders. On the left side of the jaw, under the coal dust, something caught the light of Yuanshu's phone.

Gold.

He knelt down beside it, and put two fingers on the cold dusty bone of the wrist, and listened.

The echo was there. But faint. Worn thin. Like a voice from the bottom of a well, a voice that had been shouted at for two years and was too tired to shout back.

The core damaged it.

Two years of forced command. Most of what he was has been worn away.

There is almost nothing left to ask.

"Pan Guolin," Yuanshu said quietly. "Your wife sent me."

For a long moment, nothing.

Then, very faint, a word came up the well. Not a sentence. Not even a thought, really. Just a shape, the last shape left, like the impression a body leaves in a mattress.

"Home."

Yuanshu took off his jacket.

He laid it out on the black floor of the tunnel, and gathered the bones onto it, carefully, one by one, the way you'd pick up something that belonged to someone else. The skull last. The gold tooth winked once in the phone light.

Then he wrapped the jacket around them, and stood, and carried Pan Guolin to the cage.

The sky was getting light when they came out of Number Three.

Mrs. Pan was standing at the cut in the fence in her wrongly buttoned cardigan, with Dabao beside her holding an umbrella over her head even though it wasn't raining, because he didn't know what else to do. She saw the bundle in Yuanshu's arms and made a sound like a small animal.

He put it into her arms.

She sat down in the coal dust right there by the fence and held it, rocking, her face pressed against the dusty jacket, and Dabao turned away and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist and pretended to look at the trucks.

Down in the yard, lights were coming on in the company office. Phones were ringing. Somebody was shouting.

And up the valley road, in the gray morning light, a line of black cars was coming. Five of them, clean and polished, not a speck of coal dust on them anywhere.

They stopped at the gate. A driver got out and opened the rear door of the middle car.

A woman stepped out.

Fifty, perhaps. A pale gray suit. Pearl earrings. She looked up at the shaft tower of Number Three, at the dead core housing visible through the open shed door, and at the cut fence. Then she looked across the yard at Yuanshu, standing beside the weeping woman in the coal dust with no jacket on, and she walked over, unhurried, her heels clicking on the concrete.

She held out a hand.

"Gao Yinping," she said. "Chairwoman, Heishui Coal and Power."

She looked him in the eye, perfectly calm.

"Mister Zhou. You've just destroyed forty million yuan of my company's equipment." She tilted her head slightly. "Shall we talk about it over breakfast?"
