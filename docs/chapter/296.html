Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 296Coming In

czverse13 min read

They heard Shiomi before they saw Blue Star.

It came on the fortieth night of the voyage, when the Candle came out of the last fold at the edge of the Shallows and the stars turned back into the stars he knew. Not through Seed. Not through the net. Through the Lens, a very long way behind, and out of it, and along every thread at once, so that everyone on the ship who had ever been near a stone felt it at the same moment: Iselde on the bridge, and Tam on the observation deck with his ear against the wall, and Seri, and the cousins in the Candle's own walls, who had been the first in the Federation to learn to row, and were rowing it home with their burning turned down.

A bell. Small. Bronze. Rung slowly, in a long falling pattern, from a bay in Yamato with a fog on it.

The coming-in call.

Ichiro asked them to ring it sometimes. So he could hear where the shore was.

He says they have rung it every night since we left Anthos. He says he did not tell you because he wanted it to be a surprise. He says it was very difficult not to tell you. He says he is proud of himself.

He says: that is the shore. Go in.

Tam took his ear off the wall. He was crying, which he did a great deal now, and which he said was all right because he could hear the walls cry too, so he was not the only one.

"It's rung badly," he said.

He says: yes. That is how you know it is a real one.

They came in over the north at dawn.

He stood at the window of the observation deck with the staff in his hand and Lingyue beside him with Xiaodeng in the sling, and watched Blue Star turn under them out of the dark. Blue. Large. The terminator crawling west across a continent he knew the shape of better than his own hand, with the long white smear of the Northern Wastes at the top of it and the gray thread of the river running down through the middle, and on the river, very small, a city with a wall.

Over the north, where it had always been, a small white ring. The keeper. Burning low.

...YOU ARE BACK.

...I SAID I WOULD ASK YOU WHAT YOU WANT WHEN YOU WERE.

...WHAT DO YOU WANT.

"To go down," he said. Out loud. "Please."

...THAT IS A GOOD ANSWER. I HAVE A ROAD.

It had a road. It laid it down from the bow of the Candle in a long slant of gold, out of the north sky and over the Wastes and down, the way it had laid them for him to stalks and Hungers and islands and a cathedral of the Lamplit, and the road ran down through the morning to the north wall of Lingzhou and stopped, very gently, at the Sector Nine battlement, beside a stone.

Everyone was there. Of course they were.

He walked down the last of the road behind Lingyue, who went first, the way she had gone up first, with the baby against her chest and her grandfather's sword on her back and a lamp in her free hand. She stopped a little way above the wall and lifted the lamp, once, and three thousand faces turned up on the battlements of the north wall of Lingzhou, and somebody on the wall lifted one back.

Then they were down, and it was very loud.

Dabao got to them first. He did it the way he did everything that mattered, which was by being enormous at it, and he picked up Bai Lu, who had not expected that, and put her down again, and then turned to Lingyue and the baby and stopped, with his arms out, like a man who had run full tilt at a wall and found it was made of glass.

"Can I," said Hu Dabao. "Is she. Can I."

"You can look," said Lingyue.

He looked. He cried into his collar. His mother, at the bottom of the Sector Nine stairs, under the green canvas roof of the dumpling cart, was already folding a paper boat.

Fat Tan had a thermos. Gao Min had her ledger open and was writing the names of everyone who came down the road, in order, and did not look up. Su Mingli stood at the corner of the battlement with her arms folded and said, to Lingyue, "You didn't fly," and Lingyue said, "Bai Lu said I couldn't," and Su Mingli said, "Good. Somebody has to tell you," and did not unfold her arms, and did not stop looking at the baby.

Tam and Seri came down the road holding hands, barefoot, and stopped at the bottom, and looked at the wall, and the city, and the river, and the dead along the battlements in their rows of nine, and Seri said, very clearly, to nobody: "It's smaller than Anthos." And then, after a moment: "I like it better."

And Qin Hao stood at the parapet in exactly the place where he had stood when they left, with a notebook in his hand, and did not say hello.

"Did you write everything down?" he said. "In order?"

"Seed did."

Every day. Every number. Two hundred and nine pages.

I have marked the places where I was wrong. There are eleven.

Qin Hao looked at the place a little above Yuanshu's eyes.

"Eleven," he said. "Good. I found nine. I want to see the other two." He held out his hand. Yuanshu did not know what he was holding it out for, and then did, and took it, and Qin Hao shook it once, hard, the way his grandfather shook hands on the Western Pass, and let go.

"Welcome back," said Qin Hao, and went back to his notebook.

His sister was writing.

Of course she was. She was at the corner of the vault door on East Market Road, at the top of the stepladder, because she had not come to the wall. She had said she would not. She had said somebody had to write down that he came back at the moment he came back, or it would not be true, and she was not going to write it from a wall where the plaster was bad.

He went there first. Before the Ossuary. Before his mother. He did not ask himself why. He walked down off the north wall through the old quarter with the staff in his hand, and Lingyue walked with him, and Old Seven behind them, one-armed, facing out, and the people of the old quarter came out of their doors to watch them go by and did not say anything, because the old quarter did not, and then Dabao's mother's neighbor said eh, he's back, loudly, to the whole street, and the whole street said eh, and that was the old quarter saying it.

The bank on East Market Road. The heavy doors. The banking hall with the high windows. The stairs down to the vault. And the white plaster round the corner of the vault door, covered from floor to ceiling in square black capitals, column after column, names and names and names, and at the top of the newest column the words he had watched her write from a road of gold four months ago.

GONE UP TO ANTHOS. TO THE ASSEMBLY. TO ASK.

And the list. And under it: COMING HOME. THAT'S NOT A QUESTION.

And under that, in fresh ink, still wet when he came down the stairs, because she had heard the street say eh:

CAME HOME.

Zhou Xiaoyu capped the marker. She climbed down off the stepladder and turned round. She had grown. He saw it at once and could not have said how. Not much. A centimeter. Something in the jaw. Eighteen now. She had turned eighteen while he was crossing the Star Sea. Seed had told him that morning, and he had sent her a mark, and she had sent one back that said I KNOW. I WAS THERE.

She looked at him for a long moment.

"You're not doing the face," said his sister.

"No."

"Good." She looked at Lingyue, and at the baby, who was awake in the sling and looking at the wall of black capitals with the cloudy unfocused interest of a child of four and a half months, and her face did something it had not done since she was small. "Oh," said Xiaoyu. "She's got bigger."

"So have you."

"I've got a stone," said his sister. "I'm supposed to." She held out her arms, and Lingyue lifted the baby out of the sling and gave her to her, and Xiaoyu held her niece in the vault of a bank on East Market Road under a wall of names, very carefully, the way she held the marker. "Hello," she said to Xiaodeng. "I'm your aunt. I write things down. Everything I write is true. So I'm going to be very careful what I write about you."

Xiaodeng looked up at her.

"She's asking you something," said Lingyue.

"I know," said Xiaoyu. "I'll tell her when she's older."

His mother was in the back room of the Ossuary, in her chair, by the window, with the names.

She did not get up. She could not. She did not turn round either, when he came through the door, because she was reading, and she finished the line she was reading first, out loud, the way she had read the names out loud every morning for three years, and then put her finger on the page to hold the place, and looked up.

"You sent one every morning," said Zhou Wanqing.

I did.

"I know you did. I counted." Her voice was very level. "A hundred and twenty-four. You didn't miss one." She looked at the place a little above her son's eyes. "I was ready to be cross with you. I had it planned."

I am sorry.

"Don't be," said his mother. "I'll find something else."

He knelt by the chair. He did not say anything. She put her thin hand over his on the arm of the chair, briefly, and took it away again to hold her place on the page, and he understood that this was the whole of what she was going to say about the four months, and that it was enough.

"I have a message," he said. "From Anthos."

His mother waited.

"From the Matriarch of the House of Anthos. Corvane. The woman in the courtyard." He looked up at her. "She said: tell the woman in the wheelchair I asked my daughter. A hundred and thirty-two days. Every day. She said: tell her I'll keep asking. Tell her she was right." He stopped. "She said: you open your mouth and you find out."

Zhou Wanqing did not move. Her finger stayed on the page.

She had asked a Matriarch of the Lamplit a question, in a courtyard on East Market Road, from a wheelchair, with a blanket over her knees. One question. Have you asked your daughter what she wants? He had been there. He had watched the Matriarch's face do something it had never been asked to do, and then go away, and come back, in the end, as a woman who said Maelis without the grade.

"Did she," said his mother.

"She took her face off. On the morning of the first reading. She gave it to Maelis in a box."

His mother looked out of the window for a long time, at the courtyard, at the dry fountain, where a tall dead shape called Ninth stood looking at the sky, and the sill, where a small still child of four billion years called Hush sat in the morning sun.

"Good," said Zhou Wanqing at last. She took her finger off the page. "Give me the baby. And then go and tell the stone you're back. She's been asking about you every morning. She asks the children first and then she asks me. Through your sister. Whether you've written." She held out her thin arms. "She wants to ask you something."

"I know. She told me first."

"Then don't make her wait," said his mother. "She's waited enough. She's been in a rock for a year. She was seventeen."

He did not go that day.

He meant to. He sat in the courtyard of the Ossuary in the afternoon with the staff across his knees, and Old Seven by the gate, and meant to get up and go to the Awakening Hall, and did not, and Seed did not say anything about it for three hours, which was, he knew, Seed being very patient.

Yuanshu.

I know.

You are doing the face.

I'm not.

You are doing a smaller one. It is a sitting face. It is new. I have not catalogued it yet.

He almost laughed.

She's going to ask me what I want to become.

Yes.

I don't know.

I know you do not. That is why you are sitting in a courtyard.

I would like to tell you something. I worked it out on the voyage. Between the deep water and the Death Law.

Everyone you have ever raised, you asked first. Three years. Every one. And when you asked them, most of them did not know either. Old Seven did not know. He said yes because a boy asked him.

Nobody knows, when they are asked. That is not what the question is for.

The question is for finding out.

He sat in the courtyard and looked at the dry fountain.

She said she wanted to ask me in the morning, he thought. When the children come. In the light.

Then go in the morning.

I will tell her. She will be pleased. She has been practicing.

At one in the morning, his sister went to the Awakening Hall to ask the Collector what it wanted, the way she had every night for a year.

He went with her. He did not decide to. He was awake, and she came through the back room with the marker in her jacket pocket and a flask of tea, and he got up and went, and Old Seven came behind them, and nobody said anything about it.

The hall was dark. The holders had gone home. Three lines of light hung over the platform, the way they had hung for a year. [Class: Scribe] [Chosen.] [Talent: — not marked —]. The warm black stone stood under them with its runes pointing down and a bright blue jacket over its shoulder. And in front of it, cross-legged on the platform, with one dry hand flat on the stone and a lamp in her lap, burning, pale, steady, the body of a nun of twelve hundred years, keeping the other end lit.

...you're back, said Wen Jia. ...the whole net knows. The fisherman told everybody. Twice.

...not now. In the morning. I told you first. I'm going to do it properly.

I know.

Xiaoyu sat down on the bottom step of the platform. She unscrewed the flask. She did not look at him. She put her hand flat on the cold floor of the Awakening Hall, the way she did every night, and looked up into the dark above the high windows, north, past the keeper, past the Shallows, toward something warm and patient and very old and very slow that was walking toward a house it had been invited to.

"It's me," said Zhou Xiaoyu. "It's one o'clock. What do you want?"

He had heard her ask it once before. On Awakening Day. A year ago. He had heard the answer, the one it had given her every night since. Tomorrow.

It did not say tomorrow.

Is he home?

His sister went very still on the step.

She looked at him. He was standing at the foot of the platform with the staff in his hand, with the light of the lamp in a dead nun's lap on his face.

"Yes," said Xiaoyu. To the dark. "He's home. He's right here."

Then I would like to hear him asked.

The girl in the stone is going to ask him in the morning. What he wants to become. I heard her tell him first. I have been listening to her for a year. She is very good.

I would like to hear what he says.

I have never heard anyone answer that. Not once. In all the long time. I only ever kept what they wanted. Not what they wanted to be.

May I listen?

His sister did not answer. She was looking at him.

So was the stone. So was the lamp. So was a tall dead captain with one arm in the side door of the Awakening Hall, facing in.

I am telling you first, said the oldest thing any of them had ever heard. I am close now. Closer than your machine has counted. I would like to be there when he says it.

Then I would like to ask you something. The thing I asked your sister to ask.

Not tomorrow.

Soon.
