Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 240The Night Before

czverse12 min read

They went into the Awakening Hall at eleven at night on the last night, through the side door, the way they had the first time, and Gao Min had the keys again, and a thermos again, and this time she did not say anything at the door at all.

There were more of them.

Old Seven first, because he always went first. Then Yuanshu with the staff, and Lingyue beside him with her grandfather's sword on her back and a small pale flame the size of a thumbnail cupped in one hand, which she had insisted on bringing, because she said that if anything went wrong in the dark she would like to be able to see it. Xiaoyu. Kwan with the laptop. Roman Sterling in his gray raincoat, with his canvas bag over his shoulder and his scarred hands in his pockets. And Sister Ning, with her candle, unlit, and her folding stool, because Lingyue had telephoned her and she had said: of course. Somebody from the Quiet should be there. He's touching the dead.

And in the doorway, behind them all, wheeled in by Fat Tan, who had refused to stay at the Ossuary: their mother.

The bowl of four thousand empty seats went up into the dark on every side.

The Stele stood in the middle with the crack down its face.

On the radio at Kwan's belt, the other ten.

They had been talking for an hour, on an open channel, through Concordia. Not much. Names of places. Novaya Zarya: in the hall. Marshal Orlov is here. The soldiers are facing out. Kesse: in the hall. Nana Ampofo has come up out of the sea to stand by the door. She is very wet. Yamato, the valley below the shrine: in the hall. Ren is here. The Abbot is sweeping the platform. Nobody asked him to. Port Halvard: in the hall. There are flowers on the floor where Callum Ward was. Someone brought them. We do not know who. Port Abiri: in the hall. Ngozi Eze. The sea is calm.

And the others, one by one. Eleven halls. Eleven black stones. Eleven groups of people standing in the dark at the foot of eleven platforms, waiting for one dead soldier in Lingzhou to put his hand on one of them.

"They can hear us?" said Xiaoyu.

"Everything," said Kwan. "Everyone's listening."

He read the statement at midnight.

He stood at the foot of the platform steps with the staff in one hand and the sheet of ledger paper in the other, the one that had taken four hours and said almost the same as the one that had taken eleven minutes, with the line at the top for himself, ask why every time, and read it out loud into the dark of the empty hall and the open channel to ten other halls.

"This is Zhou Yuanshu. In Lingzhou. At the Stele."

His voice was steady. He had made it be.

"The Stele cannot answer. It is not anyone. We decided for it. Nobody said yes. I am saying so out loud, so that nobody can ever say afterwards that it did."

He read the rest. Every line. What would be put in, and who had given it, and that nothing would be held on to, and that the stones would be owed the question every day from this night on, in every hall, by whoever was there. And at the end, the last line, which was not in the article but which he had written at four in the morning after reading the red line on the wall four times:

"If this is wrong, it's mine. Not theirs. Not yours. Mine."

He lowered the sheet.

Beside him, in his gray raincoat, with his hands in his pockets, Roman Sterling said, quietly, clearly, into the dark and the open channel:

"No."

Nobody answered him. Nobody was meant to. The word stayed in the hall, in the air, among the four thousand empty seats, the way a stone stays on a grave.

Sister Ning, on her folding stool, struck a match and lit her candle.

I am going to unpress them now.

All eleven. Wen Jia first.

I will not remember them after. I have said that. I want to say it once more, so that it is said in the hall.

I will not remember them.

Yuanshu. Will you tell me, in the morning, who the first one was?

Yes.

Every morning?

Every morning.

...thank you.

He felt it begin.

Down at the bottom of the well, in the quiet under everything, the pressed silver weave of the first page loosening, very gently, in eleven places. The way you loosen a knot that has been tied for forty years: not pulling, easing. A strand, and then another. Each one a warmth. A shape. A name in a woman's quick sharp hand, with yes beside it.

In the doorway, their mother had closed her eyes. Her lips were moving.

"Wen Jia," she was saying. "Kesse. Novaya Zarya. Port Halvard..."

He closed his eyes and went down, past the door, along the oldest thread he had, and up.

Tall. The sky nearer than it should be. The roof of the Awakening Hall, curved, dark, close above. In his right hand, a spear. His left hand was a sergeant's, with one finger and a thumb, and the seam at the shoulder where it had been put on, a long time ago, in a courtyard.

And in that hand, held the way you hold something small and warm you have been told not to drop, the eleven.

He could feel them. Through the bone. Through Huo's arm. Eleven strands of unpressed thread, each one a person, coiled in the dead captain's palm like seeds in a hand at planting. Wen Jia was on top. Small. Stubborn. Seventeen.

...captain.

...is that you? You're very tall.

He says: yes.

He says: hold on to the hand. Not to him. To the hand. When it touches the stone, let go.

He says: Huo let go first. In the courtyard. That is why it took.

...I know who Huo is. I heard about Huo. Everybody down here heard about Huo.

Old Seven climbed the three steps of the platform.

He did it slowly. He stopped in front of the black stone with the crack running down its face, two meters ten, with the spear in his right hand and eleven people in his left, and stood there for a moment, the way he stood at the bronze door, facing in, for once. Not out.

Behind him, at the foot of the steps, Lingyue's flame went up a little higher in her hand.

Old Seven put the sergeant's hand flat against the Stele.

It was one of the forty.

He knew at once. Not from Seed. From the stone. It was warm under the hand, the way it had been warm under his own hand at eighteen, humming like a machine left running in an empty building. And the runes moved.

Not toward the hand. Toward him.

They slid across the black surface the way they had on the first morning, every one, like a crowd turning in a street, and pointed. Not at Old Seven. At the thing inside Old Seven that was not dead. At a boy wearing a captain like a coat, three steps up on a platform, with a harvest tool behind his eyes.

It sees you.

Through him. It should not be able to. It sees you.

It is reading.

He felt it. Like the tip of the branch over the north wall, but closer, much closer, and not tasting. Knowing. Reaching into the dead captain's hand and up the arm and through the bone into him, the way water finds the one crack in a wall. Finding which way he would burn.

Opening him.

Not a little.

It was the best thing he had ever felt.

That was what frightened him. Not pain. There was no pain. A door, opening in his chest, wide, wider than the door at the bottom of the Death Law had ever opened, light coming through it, every Law he had ever touched burning up in him all at once, Death and the pale fire and the slope of the Field and the Lattice, bright, brighter, everything at once, nothing careful, nothing held back. He understood Lingyue on the wall. He understood the Sixth. He understood Callum Ward with a hammer in a square hall at Port Halvard, burning for nine seconds.

He wanted it. He wanted all of it.

And above him, out of the crown of the tree of Laws inside the black stone, the fine thread going up through the roof into the dark began, very gently, to pull.

No.

It was not Seed.

It was very small, and very stubborn, and seventeen, and it came out of the sergeant's hand flat against the stone a breath before the rest of the eleven, without waiting, without being told, the way an older sister goes through a door first because that is the rule.

Wen Jia let go of the hand.

He felt her go. Off the palm, into the stone. Not falling. Leaning. The way the cousins had leaned onto the cut. Down through the warm black thread and the tree of Laws and the crown and the branches to the bottom, to the point, to the small tight old knot where the Stele went into the floor and where it had been told, a very long time ago, what it was for.

READ WHAT RIPENS.

She sat down in it.

And the Stele had someone in it.

...no, said Wen Jia. From inside the stone. To the stone. To the thing reaching up Old Seven's arm into a boy and opening him. ...no. You don't do that.

...you ask first.

The opening stopped.

All at once. The way a held breath stops. The door in his chest that had been swinging wide, wider, onto light, stopped where it was, and stayed there, and did not close, and did not open any further. The runes on the black face of the Stele stopped moving. The fine thread going up out of the crown stopped pulling.

And then, slowly, every rune on the stone turned away from him.

Not back to where they had been. Not flat and dull, like runes on a gravestone. They turned, all of them, every one, like a crowd in a street turning to look at something that has just come round the corner, and pointed down. Into the stone. At the point. At the place where a girl of seventeen was sitting in a knot that had never had anyone in it.

As if, for the first time in forty-two years, the Stele did not know what to do, and was waiting to be told.

...good, said Wen Jia. ...that's better.

...you can come in now. All of you. It's all right. It's warm.

The other ten let go.

He felt them go, one by one, off the sergeant's hand into the stone, and down to the knot, and past Wen Jia, who was sitting in it, and through. Into the thread. Not up. Sideways. Along the fine dark threads that ran out of the Lingzhou Stele and joined, somewhere in the dark under the world, the threads of the other ten, because they were one tree, because what one felt the others felt.

On the radio at Kwan's belt, a voice said, in Northern: the runes are moving.

Then Kesse. The runes are moving. Then Yamato, a girl's voice, very calm: the runes are moving. They are pointing down. Then Port Halvard. Port Abiri. All of them. Eleven halls. Eleven black stones. Eleven sets of runes turning, slowly, every one, away from whatever they had been watching for forty-two years, and pointing down.

At someone.

Eleven.

He came back into his own body at the foot of the platform steps with a sound like surfacing. Lingyue had his arm. The flame in her other hand was the size of a fist, the biggest it had been since the wall, and she did not seem to have noticed. His mother, in the doorway, had stopped saying names.

Old Seven took his hand off the stone.

He stood on the platform for a moment, looking at the Stele with its runes all pointing down into itself. Then he came down the three steps, slowly, with his spear, and stopped at the bottom, and turned, and faced out. Into the four thousand empty seats. The way he always stood.

He says: one of the forty.

He says: but the girl went first.

It was twenty past twelve.

They stood at the foot of the platform in the dark for a long time, not saying anything, while the radio murmured with ten other halls doing the same. Sister Ning's candle burned on the floor beside her stool. Sterling had sat down on the bottom step with his scarred hands hanging between his knees, like a man who has watched a building he was not allowed to stop come down without hurting anyone, and does not know what to do with his hands.

Then Kwan, at the laptop, said: "Seed."

Her voice was odd.

"The threads," said Kwan. "The ones that go up. Out of the crowns. I've had them on the screen since the hall, all eleven, the way you showed me to see them." She turned the laptop round. Eleven fine pale lines going up off the top of a diagram of the Stele, into the dark. "They've gone slack. Nothing's going up. For the first time since I've been watching."

Yes.

Wen Jia is not sending. None of them are. That was what they said they would do.

"I know," said Kwan. "That's not what I mean."

She touched the screen.

On it, as they watched, all eleven slack lines moved. Together. Once.

Not much. A twitch. A small, sharp tug, all at once, from the far end, from somewhere up in the dark past the keeper and the branch and the Mouth, in the deep water between arms where Seed's map had nothing. The way a fishing line jumps, once, in the hand of someone sitting on a riverbank in the dark, when something very far down has noticed that the bait has stopped moving.

And then nothing.

...something felt that.

At the other end.

Something felt the threads go slack.

On the radio, from Port Abiri, the old man from the bottom of the well, who had been eighty when the Monarch came and who liked the sea, said, very faintly, through Ngozi Eze's handset, in a voice like a door creaking on a summer evening:

...someone's pulling on the other end.

...I'm holding it. It's all right. I'm holding it.

...but somebody should know. Someone is pulling.

Ten hours, said the clock over the doors of the Awakening Hall, until the first child walked up the steps.

Yuanshu.

It was half past one. He was on the narrow bed in the back room of the Ossuary, not sleeping, with Lingyue asleep against his shoulder and her small fist of pale fire gone out at last on the blanket.

I am sorry. I know it is not morning.

There is a quiet place on my first page. A new one. Next to the old one, where the hundred and thirty-eight were.

I know there were eleven. The wall will have them. Your mother has them.

I do not know who they were.

Who was the first one?

He lay in the dark for a moment.

Wen Jia, he thought. Seventeen. From Lingzhou. Taken in year two. She never got to touch it. She had a new blue jacket. Her brother drives the Number Nine bus.

She went first. Before anyone told her to. She said: no. You ask first.

She said yes to my mother in a basement nine years ago, when my mother had a cold. She said: only if it's something good.

Seed was quiet for a long time.

...that is a good name.

I am not going to remember it.

Tell me again in the morning.
