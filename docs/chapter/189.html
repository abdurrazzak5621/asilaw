Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 189The People Who Can't

czverse11 min read

On the first day, Xiaoyu drew the city.

She did it on the floor of the old banking hall, on her knees, with chalk, because the wall was full. Lingzhou, forty kilometers of it inside the curve of the old wall: the river, the north wall, Sector Nine, the old quarter, the Institute's ruins north of the river, East Market Road. She drew every district. Then every street in every district. Then she sat back on her heels with chalk on her face and looked at it, and said: "It's too big."

"It's three million people," said Kwan.

"I know how many people it is." Xiaoyu stood up. "I mean the beams. Shi says they're three degrees wide. At a kilometer, three degrees is fifty meters. At ten kilometers, it's five hundred." She pointed at the chalk city. "If they're standing north of the wall, by the time the beam gets to the river, it's a kilometer wide. That's not a street. That's half a district."

She is right.

The further the lamp, the wider the slice. The wave has to be wider in the south than in the north. And it has to move faster, because a wide slice sweeps more city in the same time.

I can calculate it. For every street. For eleven lamps. From every place they might stand.

But someone has to tell the street.

"Radio," said Fat Tan, from his desk. "Every phone. Every speaker. The same system your mother used for Sixty Seconds."

"One voice can't call three million people street by street," said Xiaoyu. "Not at the same time. They'd all hear every call. They'd get confused." She looked at the chalk. "You need one voice for each district. Calling just that district. Following the wave." She counted under her breath. "Thirty-one districts. Thirty-one voices. All listening to Shi. All calling at the same time."

"Where do we get thirty-one people who can do that?" said Kwan.

Xiaoyu was already writing names on the floor beside the chalk city.

They were not hard to find. That was the strange thing. Lingzhou had done Sixty Seconds, a year ago, when the Low Fire came, and Xiaoyu had stood on the battlements with a whistle and a stopwatch and three million people had gone dark and quiet on her signal. Everyone in the city remembered it. Everyone had a story about where they had been. And a great many people, it turned out, had been the person in their building, or their street, or their school, who had made everyone else do it properly.

Xiaoyu had their names. She had kept a list. Of course she had.

By the third day, there were thirty-one of them in the old banking hall, sitting on the marble floor around the chalk city with headphones on. A schoolteacher from the old quarter. A night-market cook. A tram conductor. Wei Desheng, the postman from the First Legion's sergeants, who had mapped every street in Lingzhou when he was alive and could still name them all; he was dead, and could not use a radio, but he sat on the floor beside the tram conductor and told her the streets as the wave came, and Seed relayed it. A girl of fourteen who had organized the whole of her middle school in nine seconds flat during Sixty Seconds, and whose mother had to be asked twice before she said yes.

Mo Shi sat in the middle, cross-legged, with his eyes shut.

He could not see eleven lamps. Not yet. They were still above the sky, at the edge of the keeper's field, standing in a line, looking down. But he could see the drill. Seed gave him eleven imaginary lamps, sweeping, from where it guessed they would stand, and Mo Shi called them three seconds ahead, and Seed turned each call into thirty-one calls, one for each district, and thirty-one voices on the marble floor spoke into thirty-one microphones at once.

On the fourth night, they tried it on the city.

It failed in eleven minutes.

Not the voices. The voices were good. The thirty-one sat on the floor of the banking hall and called their districts, street by street, following the imaginary beams Seed swept across the chalk, and across Lingzhou, in the dark, three million people heard their own district's voice on every phone and every speaker, and went quiet when it told them to, and breathed when it let them.

Mostly.

Eighty-one percent quiet when the beam passes.

That is very good. That is better than I thought.

It is not enough.

He was on the battlements of Sector Nine, watching. Lingyue beside him. Su Mingli on his other side. Below them, Lingzhou in the dark, every window black, and through the Field he could feel it the way you feel the sea breathe: a great slow hush moving across the city in long curved bands, district by district, following the beams nobody could see. Quiet. Then breath. Then quiet in the next street.

And in every band, small bright points that did not go quiet.

Here is the problem.

Nineteen percent. In every slice, as the beam passes. Not the same people every time. Most of them are people who are frightened, or who were asleep and woke up, or who lost track. They will get better with practice.

But some of them are the same every time.

Here. A hospital. Four hundred beds. Here. Another. Here. The maternity ward at the Second People's. Here, a care home for the old. Here, a children's ward.

People in pain cannot be quiet. People who are dying cannot be quiet. Babies cannot be quiet. People whose minds are going, who do not understand what is happening, cannot be quiet.

And the walkers hold the brightest.

He understood before it finished.

When the beams pass over the city, everyone who can be quiet will be quiet. And the only bright minds left in the slice will be the ones who cannot.

The lamp will find them first.

And hold.

Nobody on the battlements said anything.

Below them, the wave went on moving across the dark city, band by band, and in every band, the same small bright points that could not go quiet. A hospital. A ward. A care home. A building where somebody was giving birth.

"The arithmetic," said Su Mingli flatly. "I know it. The lamp holds on one. While it holds, it's blind to everything else. Everyone around that one is safe." She did not look at him. "Every lamp that holds on a dying man in a hospital bed is a lamp that isn't reading the rest of the city."

"No," said Yuanshu.

"I didn't say we'd do it. I said I know the arithmetic. So does your machine. So does everyone on this wall." She folded her arms. "So does whoever's going to have to decide."

I know it.

I am not going to use it.

I would like that to be written down. I ran it, because I run everything. I am not going to use it. A world that hides behind its dying is not a world I want to have counted.

"Then they leave," said Lingyue.

Ngozi Eze arrived on the fifth day with four hundred people from the Southern Compact who had moved sixty thousand on a single coast road in sixty hours, and a great many buses, and no patience at all.

She stood in the old banking hall in her plain dark suit in front of the chalk city on the floor and listened to Seed for eleven minutes. Then she took a stick of Xiaoyu's chalk without asking and knelt and began to draw circles. Around every hospital. Every care home. Every maternity ward. Every school for children too young to understand.

"How many?" she said, not looking up.

Two hundred and eleven thousand. Who cannot be quiet. Who are not in a place where they can be moved easily. Who are in beds, or are very small, or are very old.

And the people who look after them.

"Four days," said Ngozi Eze. She stood up, with chalk on her suit. "South. Out of the Field. Out of the beams. A hundred and fifty kilometers. The Atlantic Federation's sending aircraft. The Northern Union's sending trains." She looked at the circles on the floor. "We did sixty thousand on one road. Lingzhou has eleven roads and a river and a railway." She dropped the chalk. "Four days."

"Some of them won't want to go."

"Then you'll ask them," said Ngozi Eze. "With the reason. To their faces. And if they still won't go, they're not in anyone's window except their own, and you'll leave them, the way you left the widow on the hill." She looked at him. "Ifeoma told me about that. Before. She said it was the only time she'd ever seen you not do something you wanted to do, and that it was the bravest thing she'd seen you do." She turned to go. "She'd have liked this. Moving people. She'd have been rude to everyone. I'll try to be rude for her."

They went.

Two hundred and eleven thousand people, over four days, south down eleven roads and a river and a railway, in buses and trains and Atlantic aircraft and the backs of army trucks from Heishui and fishing boats from the coast that Ngozi Eze had sent for without asking anyone. Hospitals emptied ward by ward. The maternity wing of the Second People's went down the river on a barge with a doctor in every cabin and Bai Lu walking from bed to bed with green light in her hands. A care home in the old quarter went in eleven Atlantic medevac planes, and an old man in the last one, who did not know where he was or what year it was, held Hu Dabao's good hand the whole way to the airstrip and called him by his dead son's name, and Dabao answered to it.

Some of them did not want to go.

He asked those himself. Every one. It took two days. An old woman dying in a bed in the old quarter, who had lived on the same street for eighty years and said she would die on it. A man with no legs who had been in the Defense Corps in the Black Tide and said he had not run from the north then and was not going to now. A couple who had just had a baby, who said their son had been born in Lingzhou and would stay in Lingzhou, and who were told, gently, with the reason, to their faces, that the baby could not say yes, and that his yes was not theirs to give, and who stood in their doorway for a long time and then went south with him on the last train, weeping.

The old woman stayed. The man with no legs stayed. Fourteen hundred others stayed, who were not in anyone's window but their own.

Ren sat with them.

She had come on the third night, by plane from Yamato, in her white robes, and gone straight from the airstrip to the old quarter without stopping, and when Yuanshu found her she was sitting on the end of the dying woman's bed in a dark room with her hands in her lap and her eyes shut.

"Spirit," she said, without opening them. "I can walk into a mind and sit down in it. You know that. I carried a dead Sleeper into three hundred people on Concordia." She did not move. "I can't make her quiet. Nobody can make anyone quiet. That isn't how it works." A pause. "But I can sit in her. Beside her. In the dark. And when the beam comes, I can be quiet with her. And sometimes that's enough. Sometimes when someone else is quiet beside you, you can be quiet too."

"Fourteen hundred," said Yuanshu.

"I know." She opened her eyes. "I can't sit in fourteen hundred. I can sit in perhaps forty. At once. If they're close together." She looked at him. "Sister Ning has the Quiet. Six hundred of them in the city. They know how to sit with someone. That's all they've done for two years." She stood up. "Between us, perhaps."

"Perhaps," said Yuanshu.

"It's the only word we have," said Shirasagi Ren. "It's a better word than the arithmetic."

On the eighth night, the last drill.

Ninety-four percent quiet when the beam passed. The thirty-one voices on the marble floor of the banking hall, calling their districts. Mo Shi in the middle, calling the imaginary beams three seconds ahead. Xiaoyu on the floor beside him with her whistle and her stopwatch and her chalk city and her headphones, not calling any district, calling all of them, the one voice over the other thirty-one that said now and breathe and again.

Two million eight hundred thousand people in the dark, in a wave.

Fourteen hundred bright points that could not be quiet, scattered across the city. And beside every one of them, someone sitting very still in the dark, being quiet with them.

Ninety-four.

It is not a hundred. It will never be a hundred.

It is the best number I have ever seen a city make.

On the battlements of Sector Nine, Old Seven stood at the parapet, facing north, with his spear grounded. Behind him on the wall, in their rows of nine, the First Legion, eighteen thousand gray points banked to dull red. Beside him, the Spear Corps, two hundred and seventy-seven. And at the end of the line, a shade of ash with an iron arrowhead for a heart, standing beside a living captain of the Defense Corps who was her daughter.

Hundred-Arrow Lin had been looking north all night.

She says she can feel them.

She says when she was alive, on this wall, in the Black Tide, she felt the one come down. Before anyone saw it. A cold place in the sky, like a stone dropped into still water.

She says she can feel eleven stones.

She says: tomorrow.
