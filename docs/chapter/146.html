Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 146The General's Order

czverse8 min read

General Qin Zhenyue walked up the summit road at dawn, alone, with his cap on.

He did not take a radio. He did not take his grandson, who stood at the last switchback below the fortress with both fists clenched and white fire flickering between his fingers, and did not follow. He walked up the last two hundred meters of frozen road with his one hand at his side and his left sleeve pinned up empty at the shoulder, slowly, the way he had walked up the terraces between his soldiers' graves, and stopped in front of the shut gate of the old watch fortress, and looked up at the walls.

Four hundred soldiers of the Ninth Mountain Battalion looked down at him.

"Ninth Mountain," said the general.

He did not shout. He had never needed to. His voice was gravel in a bucket, and it carried in the thin cold air the way it had carried across this saddle for thirty-one years.

"I'm not going to give you an order," he said. "I came up here to give you one. I've been thinking about it all night. What I'd say. Stand down. Open the gate. Come home. I've given that order before. On this mountain. To better men than you, and worse." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm not going to give it. You'd disobey it. You'd be right to. It's not mine to give. You've chosen."

Nobody on the walls moved.

"So I'm going to ask," said General Qin. "The way the boy asks. I learned it from him. I'm eighty-two, and I learned something from a nineteen-year-old who's shorter than he looks on television." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "Ninth Mountain. I'm asking you to come down and stand with me. Not to go home. Not to give up what you want. Just to stand here, at the gate, beside your general, for one hour. And then you can go back up, if you like, and nobody will ask you twice."

Under the mountain, three hundred meters down and eighty more, the Fourth Company of the Western Pass broke through.

Yuanshu felt it from the mouth of the inner gate. He was standing in the tunnel entrance with the staff planted on the old ore-cart rails and the Banked Field folded over the whole mountain, and Old Seven beside him, and the Spear Corps in their rows in the dark behind him. Through the Lattice, through Adjutant Xu, through a sergeant of the Fourth Company who counted out loud in a mountain accent, he felt a pick go through the last half-meter of cracked rock into empty space.

Into a cavern.

They are in.

It is big, Yuanshu. Bigger than the round chamber. The seeds have been eating the rock too. They have made themselves a room.

I can see them through the Fourth Company's eyes.

So could he.

Two shapes in the dark. A hundred meters apart, on the floor of a cavern their own growth had carved out of the mountain. Not peach-sized. Not the size of a man. Each of them as big as a house, gray, cloudy, cracked all over with fine dark lines like old ice, lit from inside the color of a coal. And out of the top of each one, up into the rock of the cavern's ceiling, a trunk of crystal as thick as a tree, going up. Toward the summit. Toward the courtyard where four hundred people knelt in rings with their heads bowed.

And from every side of both seeds, out into the walls, into the floor, into the dark, roots. Thousands of them. Running away through the stone of the mountain to forty-one thousand graves.

They were not moving. They could not see the Fourth Company standing in the hole in their wall, banked to dull red points.

"Pale fire," said Yuanshu. "Every point. Every pick. Go."

The dead went in.

Inside the Field, every edge they carried burned silver-gray: rusted bayonets, pick heads, the blades of shovels, the iron-shod butts of rifles. A hundred and sixty soldiers of the Fourth Company came down out of the hole in the cavern wall in their nines and went at the nearest seed the way Old Seven had gone at the spire in Port Abiri, the way the Spear Corps had gone at the Bridge-Breaker. To the seams. To the cracks. To the dark lines in the gray crystal where it had grown too fast and not quite closed.

Behind them, out of the hole, came the rest. Thirty thousand.

The first seed felt it.

Not saw. Felt. The way you feel a needle go into your skin when you are not looking. The pale fire went into its cracks and frost spread across its surface, and the coal-color inside it flared, and every root that ran away from it through the mountain went taut at once, like the strings of an instrument.

It knows.

It is pulling. Every root. Everything it can reach.

The graves are banked. It cannot find them.

So it is reaching up.

At the gate of the fortress, the first soldier came down.

He was young. Twenty, perhaps. A corporal with frost in his eyebrows and his rifle slung across his back. He came down the steps from the wall into the courtyard, past the rings of kneeling men around the two gray points of crystal rising out of the paving, and walked to the gate, and opened the small door in it, and stepped out onto the frozen road.

He stood in front of General Qin and came to attention.

"Corporal Deng," said the general. "Third platoon. You married the cook's daughter. I was at the wedding. You cried."

"Yes, sir."

"Stand here."

The corporal stood beside him.

Then another came. A sergeant, older, gray-bearded. Then two together. Then five. Not the whole battalion. Not most of it. But they came, one at a time, down off the walls and out through the little door in the gate, and stood on the frozen road beside an old man with one arm, because he had asked them to, and they had loved him for thirty-one years.

Inside the courtyard, in the rings around the spires, most of the kneeling men did not move.

On the gatehouse roof, Major Zhuo Wenli watched the soldiers go out through the door one by one, and did not stop them, and did not say anything at all.

The roots came up through the courtyard at 06:41.

Seed called it four seconds early. Mo Shi called it three. Lingyue was already on the wall.

They came up out of the paving around the two spires, gray and branching and fast, cracked with dark lines, the way they had come up through the floor of Hearth One. Not toward the soldiers on the road. Toward the rings. Toward the men kneeling around the spires with their heads bowed, the nearest warm things on the mountain, who had come here asking to be the first thing it ate.

Some of them looked up. Some of them did not.

"Now," said Mo Shi.

Mo Xing folded.

Not a meter. Not a stone from one hand to the other. He had been folding for a year, since a crater on Concordia in the rain, until his nose bled, until he could fold a thread three hundred meters out over the sea. He folded a man. A kneeling sergeant of the Ninth Mountain, from the paving of the courtyard to the frozen road outside the gate, beside the general, in the time it takes to blink. And then another. And another. His nose was bleeding by the third. He did not stop.

"Left ring," said Mo Shi, white-faced, eyes shut. "Two seconds. The tall one. Then the one with the scarf. Then..."

Lingyue came down off the wall into the courtyard with two swords.

Inside the Field, both burned pale. Her own, and her grandfather's, a King's blade, a hundred kilos of it in her left hand. She did not cut the men. She cut the roots. She went between the rings of kneeling soldiers in a line of silver-gray and cut every gray branch that came up out of the paving toward them, the way she had cut a heron out of the air above a dead grandmother, and the pale fire ran over the kneeling men like water over stone and did not leave a mark.

Bai Lu came after her.

She did not cut anything. She put her small hands on the shoulders of the kneeling men, one after another, and green light ran out of her palms into them, and the thing in their faces that wanted so badly to be taken went quiet. Not gone. Eased. The way she had once eased the guilt of a woman lying on crushed glass in a river of the dead. They sat back on their heels and blinked, like people waking up in the wrong room, and Dabao put his shield between them and the roots and walked them backward toward the gate.

The first seed is cracking.

The Fourth Company is in its heart. Their sergeant is counting.

Yuanshu. The second seed.

It is not fighting. It is growing.

He felt it. Under the mountain, in the cavern, the second seed had stopped pulling its roots. It had let them go. All of them. Forty-one thousand roots in forty-one thousand graves, let go at once, the way a man lets go of everything in his hands to grab one thing.

All of it went up.

Up the trunk of crystal out of the top of the seed, through the rock of the cavern's ceiling, through four hundred meters of mountain, into the second spire in the courtyard.

The second spire grew.

In the courtyard, in the gray dawn, the point of crystal that had been rising slowly out of the paving for two days went up like a tree in a dream. Ten meters. Twenty. Forty. Cracked gray crystal pouring up into the sky out of the old watch fortress on the saddle, branching, the color of a coal glowing in every crack, until it stood above the walls and the tower and the summit, sixty meters of it, a spire on a mountain of graves, in the dawn.

At the top of it, the crystal opened.

Not a mouth. Something like a bell. A wide shallow cup of cracked gray, facing the sky.

It is going to speak.

It does not have the whole word. The first seed is dying and it had half. But it has enough.

FIELD. EMPTIED. KEEPER TURNED. CAUSE. And the knot.

It needs a voice. It needs something to shout with.

On the gatehouse roof, Major Zhuo Wenli put his tablet down on the stone, very carefully, and took off his cap, and folded it, and put it beside the tablet.

Then he walked to the base of the second spire, and put his hands on the crystal, and began to climb.
