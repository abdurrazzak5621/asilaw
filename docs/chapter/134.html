Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 134The Sitting

czverse10 min read

They came on the fifteenth morning, before dawn, and sat down.

Nobody saw them arrive. The Defense Corps had a perimeter around the Institute a kilometer wide, and cameras on every street, and Lingyue's students on the roofs, and none of them saw it happen, because nothing happened. People walked. In ones and twos, in ordinary clothes, through the old industrial district in the dark, the way people walk to work. And when they reached the fence around the Institute, they sat down on the pavement with their backs to the fence and their hands in their laps and closed their eyes.

By six o'clock there were four thousand of them.

By nine there were eleven thousand.

They did not chant. They did not hold signs. They sat in rows on the pavement and the grass verges and the road, all the way around the Institute's fence and back up the side streets, with their eyes closed, perfectly still. Old men and students and mothers with babies in slings. A bus driver in uniform. Three nurses. A boy of about twelve, sitting between his parents, with his eyes squeezed shut the way children shut them when they are playing a game where you have to be a statue.

They were playing the game. Sister Ning's game. Think about rice. Think about the sound of the sea. Think about nothing.

Eleven thousand people around a cage, being very, very quiet.

"They're not breaking any law," said Lu.

He was standing on the roof of a warehouse across the road from the Institute's main gate, with binoculars, at ten in the morning, and his voice was very tired. Yuanshu stood beside him with the staff. Below them, the rows of sitters ran away down the street, still and silent, as far as the corner.

"It's peaceful assembly. They're on public land. They aren't blocking the gate. They aren't blocking anything. They're sitting." He lowered the binoculars. "The Council's lawyers have been on the phone to me since six. Half of them say we can move them. The other half say if we touch one of them, the Atlantic Federation will call it a crime under the Charter."

"Article Three," said Yuanshu.

"Quiet means quiet." Lu put the binoculars away. "They're quiet. They've chosen to be. They've written it down. Every one of them is on their own register, witnessed, and every one of them has said, in writing, that they want to be here when the cage opens."

I asked one of them.

Through the staff, very softly, the way you would ask the dead. She heard me. She is good at the game, but she heard.

She said: we want to be first.

She said: when it sweeps, we want it to find us before it finds anyone who didn't ask.

She said: we're making it easier. For everyone. We're being kind.

He closed his eyes.

The Abbot spoke at noon.

Not in person. A recording, sent to every news channel on Blue Star at once from a server nobody could find. A plain room with a wooden floor. A cushion. A man sitting on it in the gray robe of a mountain shrine, perhaps sixty, with a shaved head and a face so calm and so kind that Yuanshu, watching it on Lu's tablet on the warehouse roof, felt his stomach turn over.

"My name doesn't matter," said the man on the screen. "Some people call me the Abbot. I tended the Sleepers on the mountain for forty years. I watched them sit in the dark so that the world would remember to be afraid. And then I watched them go quiet, one after another, until there was no one left on the cushions." He folded his hands. "I don't want to be afraid any more. I want to be kept."

He looked into the camera.

"Mister Zhou Yuanshu taught the world to ask. I'm grateful for that. Truly. Every person sitting outside the Institute in Lingzhou today has been asked, with witnesses, and has said yes. We have followed his rule more carefully than any government on Blue Star." A small, sad smile. "And now he is going to open a cage in two days and fight the only thing that ever offered to keep us. And when he does, I think he will discover that his rule has a limit. That asking is only good when the answer is the one he wants."

He bowed his head slightly.

"We'll be sitting there, Mister Zhou. We won't stop you. We won't touch you. We'll only be there. And you'll have to decide whether a yes means yes."

The screen went dark.

Shirasagi Ren arrived at three in the afternoon, on foot, from the airport, alone.

She came up the warehouse stairs in her white robes and stood beside Yuanshu at the parapet and looked down at the sitters without speaking. The youngest King in Yamato's history, perhaps the strongest. She had carried a dead monk's vision into the minds of three hundred people in a volcanic crater. She looked, now, like a woman at a funeral.

"Kurose Tōya," she said finally. "His name. He tended the Sleepers when I was a child. He used to give me rice cakes when my grandmother wasn't looking. He sat beside the seven cushions every night for forty years and swept the gates every morning." She did not take her eyes off the street. "When the last Sleeper went quiet on Concordia, he went up to the shrine and sat on the empty cushion and didn't come down for nine days. And then he came down, and walked away, and I let him."

"You didn't know."

"I knew he was grieving. I thought grief would pass." She was quiet. "I thought the whole country's grief would pass. A thousand years of holding still so we would remember. And then it was over, and there was nothing to hold still for." She looked at him at last. "He's not lying, Yuanshu. That's what you need to understand. He means every word. He's the kindest man I ever knew."

Like Gu.

No.

Gu was used. This man chose.

Marshal Orlov came at dusk, by train from the Northern Union, with frost on the windows of his carriage and Sergeant Sokolova at his shoulder. He looked at the sitters from the roof for a long time too, two hundred years old, in his long gray greatcoat, and then he said, in his careful old-fashioned Huaxian:

"In my country, we would move them."

"I know."

"We would carry them, one at a time, gently, to buses, and drive them to a stadium on the other side of the city, and give them blankets and soup and keep them there until it was over. Nobody would be hurt. Everyone would be very angry." He turned his ice-pale eyes on Yuanshu. "And you are the man who taught the world that you do not touch people who have not said yes."

"Yes."

"So." Orlov folded his gloved hands on the parapet. "What does the man who asks do now?"

He went down to them at nine at night.

Alone. Without the staff, which he gave to Lingyue to hold, because the staff was the brightest thing in the city and he did not want it near eleven thousand people who wanted very much to be noticed. Without Old Seven, who stood at the top of the warehouse stairs with his spear and his gray flame and did not like it. He walked out of the Defense Corps perimeter and along the road, between the rows, until he found the woman Seed had asked that morning, and sat down on the pavement in front of her.

She opened her eyes. She was perhaps forty, with gray in her hair and a gardener's hands, and she smiled at him as if he were a neighbor.

"Mister Zhou."

"What's your name?"

"Fang Lili."

"Fang Lili. I'm going to ask you something. You don't have to answer."

"I know," she said. "That's your rule."

He looked at her for a moment.

"In two days," he said, "I'm going to open the cage. When I do, the thing inside it might sweep this street before we can stop it. If it does, it'll lift you out. Everything you are. It'll send you up a beam to something above the sky that keeps minds and never asks." He kept his voice level. "That's what you want. I understand that. I'm not going to tell you it's wrong to want it."

"Thank you."

"But you're sitting less than a kilometer from it. Eleven thousand of you. Every one of you that it lifts makes it stronger. Faster. Every one of you is a day off the clock. If you're here, it might get its voice before we get the spear in." He looked down the road at the rows of still faces in the streetlight. "And if it gets its voice, it won't only lift you. It'll lift the girl in the hospital on the fourth floor who's never heard of you. It'll lift my sister."

Fang Lili's smile did not change.

"We've been asked," she said gently. "We said yes. Isn't that enough?"

"For you," said Yuanshu. "Not for her."

He stood up.

"You've made your choice, Fang Lili. I'll defend it to anyone. You can go into any fire you like." He looked down at her. "But you're standing in someone else's window. So in the morning, you're going to be moved. Gently. All of you. To a stadium, with blankets and soup, the way the Marshal said. And when it's over, you can go wherever you like, and be kept by whatever you like, and I'll never ask you twice."

"You'll be touching people who said no," said Fang Lili. Her voice was still kind. "That's not like you."

"No," said Yuanshu. "It isn't."

He walked back up the road between the rows. Nobody opened their eyes.

They moved them at dawn.

Su Mingli folded them. Not all at once. In rows. A King's page-turn, ninety people at a time, from the pavement outside the Institute to the grass of the old Lingzhou football stadium eleven kilometers south, where Captain Lin Wen's Defense Corps were waiting with blankets and soup and Sister Ning and four hundred of the Quiet, sitting beside them in silence. Nobody was hurt. Nobody resisted. They sat where they were put, on the cold grass, with their eyes closed, and went on playing the game.

It took Su Mingli four hours and a hundred and twenty-two folds. When it was done she sat down on the warehouse roof and did not get up for a long time.

By noon it was on every channel on Blue Star. THE MAN WHO ASKS. Over footage of a King folding eleven thousand silent people off a street. The Atlantic Federation's new Secretary called it a violation of the Charter. Two members of the Concordia Council called for an emergency session. In the comment sections, under the footage, people who had painted ASI on walls a year ago were writing that he was just like the rest of them, in the end.

They are not all wrong.

You touched people who said no.

I think you were right to. I think the Charter does not have a word for what you did, and it will need one.

I do not think that makes it nothing.

"No," he said. "It doesn't."

Shen Jinghong came up the warehouse stairs at sunset, alone.

He was dressed the same way he had been on the Academy roof: a plain dark coat, no insignia. He stopped at the top of the stairs and looked at the empty street below, where eleven thousand people had sat, and then at the Institute, and then at his granddaughter, who was standing at the parapet with the white staff in her hands because Yuanshu had not yet asked for it back.

"I've considered it," he said.

Lingyue did not turn around.

"And?"

"Light doesn't care what it burns." The old man walked across the roof and stood beside her. "Your father used to say that too. He said it the day before he died, at the Northern Rift, when he burned a pass full of beasts and the rescue team that had just come through it." He looked at her sword, sheathed at her hip. "I've never told you that."

Lingyue went very still.

"I'm eighty-four," said Shen Jinghong. "Light can learn one new thing at eighty-four, I suppose." He looked at the Institute. "Where do you want me to stand?"

She turned and looked at him. Then at Yuanshu.

"Inside the Field," said Yuanshu. "Next to her."

"Hm," said the King of Light. "And the dog?"

Lingyue stared at him.

"I've considered that too," he said. "After. If there is an after." He folded his hands behind his back and looked north, at the small white ring over the end of the world. "Thirteen years is long enough to consider anything."
