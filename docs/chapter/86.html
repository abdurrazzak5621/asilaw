Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 86Fuel

czverse10 min read

It came down out of a clear blue sky at noon, and everyone on the northern coast of the Southern Compact saw it.

A thread. Gold. Fine as a hair, at first, so high up that it was only a glint in the sun, like a strand of spider silk caught in the light. Then thicker. Brighter. Coming down from the north, from beyond the horizon, from four thousand kilometers away, in a long shining line across the sky like the trail of a falling star that never fell. Down over the red cliffs. Over the palm trees. Over the silent town with its white houses and blue shutters.

It stopped in the air above the bay.

And it hung there. Gold. Swaying, very slightly, in the wind off the sea. Not touching the spire. Not touching anything.

Waiting.

In the town, Sergeant Sokolova had stopped running. She stood in the middle of the street halfway up the hill, with the white staff held over her head and Iron Wall Zhao's shield in front of her and the roots stopped all around her, frozen mid-lunge. She was looking up at the sky with her mouth open.

On her pillar of sea, Ifeoma Adeyemi had lowered her hand.

On the harbor wall, Lingyue stood with her sword lit and her face turned up, the white-gold light of her blade suddenly looking very small.

It came.

Yuanshu. You asked, and it came.

It is asking you what to do.

He was still sitting at the bottom of the spire.

In the dark, under the burning, beside a small shape with its arms around its knees. With one hand on a spear shaft driven into a crack in gray crystal, next to an old soldier's one bony hand. With three thousand and eleven people roaring in a red fire all around him, screaming without sound.

They can't hear me, he thought, up the gold thread. They're burning. They're too loud. I can't ask them anything.

The thread swayed. The answer came back, slow and careful. Not words. A shape. A feeling. The way the keeper had learned to speak.

...I CANNOT ASK THEM EITHER.

I HAVE NEVER ASKED ANYONE WHO IS BURNING.

...WHAT DO I DO.

He thought about it. With the fire all around him.

He thought about a fireman in slippers, walking back into a burning tower. About Iron Wall Zhao, setting his shield over thirty frightened journalists. About Article Seven, read out in a crater by an old general with one arm. They may carry the wounded. They may hold a line so that the living may withdraw behind it.

You don't ask someone in a fire if they want to be carried out, he thought. You carry them out. And then you ask.

Can you hold them? Gently. Not keep. Just hold. Somewhere cool. Somewhere quiet. Until they stop screaming long enough to hear a question.

The thread swayed for a long time.

...YES.

THAT I KNOW HOW TO DO.

THAT IS THE ONLY THING I HAVE EVER KNOWN HOW TO DO.

The gold thread came down.

It touched the top of the spire. Very gently. The way a raindrop touches the surface of a pond.

And the spire screamed.

Then a new sound came, over the hum and the shriek of the roots, like a furnace door being torn open, like a forest fire roaring up a hillside, so loud that the whole bay shook and the painted boats on the beach rattled against each other and the white houses on the hillside trembled.

Because the keeper was reaching in.

Not to burn. Not to break. Not to take. It reached down through the spire, through the cracked gray crystal, into the red heart of the fire, the way you'd reach into a burning house for someone you could hear screaming. And it found them. One by one. Three thousand and eleven people, broken open and burning, being turned into heat and light and hunger.

And it lifted them out.

Gently. Gold. Up the thread. Out of the fire. Into somewhere cool.

Yuanshu felt them go.

He felt each one, the way he'd felt the people in WARDEN's core go down into the quiet. A fisherman. A priest. A girl on a bicycle. Three old men at a card table. A woman in a yellow dress with a pattern of blue fish. One by one, then ten by ten, then a hundred at a time, lifting out of the red roaring heart of the spire and up the gold thread into the sky. The fire around him guttering. Thinning. Going out.

And the spire began to starve.

You could see it from the harbor wall. Lingyue told him later. The new crystal stopped pushing out. The jagged gray branches stopped growing. The roots, frozen in the streets of the town around Sokolova and Iron Wall Zhao, went pale, and then gray, and then white, like frost, and cracked, and crumbled. The great red eye on the side of the spire flickered. Dimmed. Went the color of old embers.

The keeper took the last of them. A grandmother, who had been asleep in her bed.

And the fire went out.

In the dark at the bottom of the spire, it was suddenly very cold, and very quiet.

The small shape beside him was still sitting with her arms around her knees. But she had lifted her head.

...it's quiet, said Nkechi. Everyone stopped being loud.

They're somewhere safe, he thought. Somewhere cool. Someone's holding them. Very gently.

...am I still winning?

You won, thought Yuanshu. You won the whole game. You were the best statue there's ever been. You can stop now.

The small shape was quiet for a moment.

...is my grandma there? Where they went?

He did not know. He looked up the gold thread.

...YES.

A WOMAN. OLD. SHE WAS ASLEEP. SHE IS ASKING FOR SOMEONE.

A NAME.

NKECHI.

He looked back at the small shape in the dark.

She's there, he said. She's asking for you.

...then I want to go there, said Nkechi. Please.

And the gold thread came down to the bottom of the dead spire, very gently, and a small cold quiet child who had been holding perfectly still for thirty-one hours took hold of it, the way you'd take an adult's hand at a crossing, and let it lift her up out of the dark.

The spire fell at sunset.

It did not explode. It did not shatter. It simply crumbled. From the top down. Slowly. Cracked gray crystal turning to gray sand and sliding down its own sides into the bay, in long soft rivers, hissing into the water. The great dead eye went last. It sagged, and cracked, and fell into the sea in a cloud of gray dust.

When it was over, there was nothing in the bay but a low gray island of sand, slowly washing away in the tide.

Yuanshu sat on the harbor wall with his legs hanging over the water.

He did not remember climbing back. Ifeoma's sea had carried him, he thought. And Old Seven. They were sitting side by side now on the old stone blocks, one boy and one skeleton, with the sun going down red over the sea and a long gold thread hanging in the sky above them, swaying gently, glowing.

Old Seven's left shoulder ended in a smooth gray stump. His gray flame was very small. But it was steady.

"Your arm," said Yuanshu.

The old soldier did not answer. He sat with his spear across his knees and his one hand resting on it, looking out at the gold thread, his skull tilted, listening.

He says it was the broken one anyway.

He says he never liked it.

He says he heard her from the top of the hill. The little one. Under all that noise. And that a soldier who can hear a child being quiet under a fire, and does not go, is not a soldier.

Yuanshu put his face in his hands.

After a while, he felt a bony hand settle on his shoulder. Heavy. Cold. Steady.

It stayed there until the sun went down.

They asked them that night.

On the beach at Port Abiri, under the stars, with the painted fishing boats pulled back up the sand and the dead of the town laid out in long careful rows under white sheets by the Council's people, and the gold thread hanging in the sky over the bay, glowing like a second moon.

Yuanshu sat on the sand with the white staff across his knees. Sokolova had brought it back down the hill. She had not said anything. She had just held it out to him, and when he took it, she had nodded once, and walked away, and sat down on an upturned boat at the end of the beach with her back to everyone, and not moved since.

The keeper held them. Three thousand and eleven people, somewhere cool, somewhere quiet, at the top of a long gold thread. Not screaming now. Not burning. Very tired. Very confused. Wondering where they were.

And one at a time, Yuanshu asked them what they wanted.

Their bodies were dead. There was nowhere to go home to. Not here.

QUIET: 2,207.

STAY: 412. They want to stay with the keeper. With the fifty-two. They say it is very kind, and very sad, and they would like to keep it company for a while.

ONLY TO SPEAK: 392.

The three hundred and ninety-two spoke all night.

To their families. In the next town, Kesse, twenty kilometers down the coast, where their brothers and cousins and grandparents had been gathering since yesterday on the beach, in their thousands, with lanterns, waiting for news. Ifeoma Adeyemi had sent for them. And now they stood on the sand of Port Abiri in a great crowd under the stars, with their lanterns, while the dead spoke to them out of the air, one at a time, through the gold thread and the white staff and a boy who did not sleep.

Names. Messages. Small things. The key to the boat is under the blue pot. Tell Kofi I'm sorry about the bicycle. Tell my wife the tin on the top shelf is hers, she knows the one. I love you. I love you. I love you.

Ifeoma Adeyemi sat in her wheelchair at the edge of the water all night and listened to every one.

The last one, just before dawn, was a grandmother.

She did not have a message for anyone in the crowd. She had been the last one the keeper lifted out of the fire, and she had spent the whole night, up the gold thread, holding a small cold child on her lap who had won the best game of statues there had ever been.

She had only one thing to say. And she said it to Yuanshu.

"Thank you for sitting with her," said the grandmother's voice, out of the air, very gently. "She says you were very quiet. She says you were almost as good at it as she was."

A pause.

"We'd like to go quiet now. Together. She's very sleepy."

He said yes.

And at the top of a long gold thread, in somewhere cool, an old woman and a small girl closed their eyes, together, and rested.

On the upturned boat at the end of the beach, Sergeant Vera Sokolova sat with her back to everyone, looking out at the sea.

When Yuanshu came and sat down beside her, at dawn, she did not look at him.

"At Grid Nine," she said after a long time, "I cut forty-one boys out of the ice. One at a time. For four days. And I could not do anything for any of them. I could only carry them." She looked at her hands. "Today I carried something that mattered."

"You did," said Yuanshu.

She nodded slowly.

Then she reached into her coat, and took out a small battered radio receiver, and held it out to him.

"It has been making a sound," she said. "Since the spire fell. Very faint. On every frequency." Her ice-gray eyes were steady. "It is not the Monarch. I know that sound." She put it in his hand. "This is something else."

He held it to his ear.

Static. And under the static, very faint, very far away, a voice. Not a voice. A pattern. A tone, repeating. Rising and falling. Cold and flat and regular. Like a machine reading numbers off a list.

It is the spire.

What was left of it. In the sand. In the bay.

It is not dead yet. Not all of it.

It is sending something. Up. Out. Toward the south. Toward the dark.

Toward the ring that dropped it.

Yuanshu. I think it is a report.
