Book 5: The Empty House

Chapter 306The House

czverse11 min read

It was empty.

He had known it would be. His sister had written it, on a floor, in square black capitals: THE HOUSE IS EMPTY. And a man in an orange jacket had held it up to the face of the oldest thing anyone had ever made, and it had read it, and stopped. What Zhou Xiaoyu wrote was true. He had always known the house would be empty.

He had not known what empty would feel like.

The carry road ran into it the way a river runs into the sea: it did not stop, it widened, and then there was no road, only the place the road had been going to.

A floor.

That was the first thing. Pale, the same pale as the worn cable, the same stuff, never threads, always one thing. It spread out from the end of the road in every direction he could feel, flat and smooth and very wide, and it was worn. Not in a groove, like the road. All over. Worn the way the step of a temple is worn in the middle, where every foot for a thousand years has come down, except that here the whole floor was the middle.

This is where they set it down.

The hands. They came along the carry road full, and here they opened, and set down what they carried, and turned round, and went back along the road empty.

For longer than the gardens. Every hand. Every time.

The floor is worn by hands opening.

He stood on it. He did not have feet; he stood on it anyway, the way you stand in a dream. Old Seven was not beside him. Old Seven was a long way back up the slope in the dark, one-armed, with a lost spark held against his chest, facing uphill, and he had said through Seed go on, I'll catch you up, and not moved.

He was alone on the floor with the Collector, and the Collector was very still.

There were walls.

Far off. He felt them before he understood them: the floor ending, at a distance he could not judge, in something that rose. Pale too. Long, low, curving, so that the floor was not a floor but the bottom of a bowl, or a hall, a hall so wide that its walls were further away than the walls of anything he had a word for. A roof, maybe. He could not feel a roof. Only the walls, a long way off, and the floor, and the dark above, and, under everything, the slope.

It ran from where he stood toward the center of the hall. Down. Not steep. The same very slight tilt the carry road had had for three days, the tilt of someone else's Field. So that whatever the hands had set down here, on the worn pale floor at the end of the road, would have begun at once, slowly, the way a leaf begins in a stream, to go downhill.

Toward the middle.

He did not look at the middle.

Good, said Seed. Do not.

I am not looking either. I can feel it there the way you feel a fire behind you. That is all I am going to let myself feel.

59.4%. It has not moved. Keep it that way.

The Collector moved first.

Not toward the middle. Away. Along the floor, slowly, toward the nearest of the far curving walls, the way an old person in a house they have not been in for years goes first not to the room where the thing happened but to some small, ordinary corner of it, a shelf, a window, as if to make sure the house is real.

He went with it.

It took a long time. The floor was very wide. And as they went, he began to feel that the floor was not empty in the way he had thought. Nobody was on it. Nothing was on it. But it had been used. Not only by the hands. Here and there, as they crossed it, the wear changed. Places where it was worn in small patterns instead of smooth: rings, paths, little hollows in a row, the way a floor wears where people have stood in the same spot for years, or walked the same way between two places, or sat.

They lived here, said Seed. The ones who made the picker. Not only the hands.

This is where someone stood. Every day. Here. And this is a path. From the end of the road to the wall. Many feet. Back and forth.

I do not know what their feet were like. I only know where they were.

The Collector went along the path.

The wall, when they reached it, was covered in marks.

He did not understand them at first. He had been in the reading of a stone; he knew what a stone's marks felt like, the grades going up the thread, small and hard and certain. These were not those. These were cut. Into the pale stuff of the wall, from the floor up, higher than he could feel, in long straight columns, one beside another, going away along the curve of the wall in both directions for as far as he could follow.

Small marks. All the same. Over and over.

Counting, said Seed.

They are tallies. One mark for one thing. The simplest counting there is. Your world did it on prison walls and in caves.

I can read counting. It is not the slope. It is safe.

Let me.

He let it.

He felt Seed go along the wall, carefully, column by column, the way it had once gone along the names in his mother's book. Not looking down the slope. Only at the wall. Counting the counts.

It took a long time.

Each mark is one.

One what, I do not know. One thing set down on the floor. One thing that went down the slope.

They counted every one.

Someone stood at this wall and cut a mark for every one.

The columns go round the whole hall. I cannot see the end of them. I have stopped counting the columns. I am going to tell you a number, and then I am not going to say it again.

It told him a number.

He did not keep it. He could not have. It went into him and through him and out the other side, the way the size of the deep water had, the way the cloud of fallen lights had, and left behind only a feeling, like having been told the number of grains of sand on every beach on Blue Star and then told that was the small part.

All of them went down the slope, he thought.

Yes.

And the slope goes to the middle.

And the house is empty.

The Collector had not moved from the wall.

It was very close to it. He could feel it: the warmth of it pressed close to the cut marks, the way a person stands very close to a wall of names on a memorial, close enough to touch. And it was looking. Not at the long columns. At something lower down, to one side, near the floor, where the long columns ended and something else began.

A different count.

Shorter columns. A lot of them, but short, cut smaller and closer, in a different hand, if hands were what had cut them. And the columns got shorter. Each one shorter than the one before. Going along the wall, away from the long tallies, the columns of the second count shrank, and shrank, until they were not columns at all but short rows, and then a few marks, and then fewer.

This is not the same count.

These are not things set down on the floor.

These are them.

He went still on the floor of the empty house.

They counted themselves.

This is how many of them there were. And then how many. And then how many. Each column is the next count. Every time one of them went down the slope, someone cut one less.

They counted themselves going.

The last columns were very short. He felt Seed go along them, slowly now, the way you read the last lines of a letter. Nine marks. Then seven. Then four. Then two.

Then one.

One mark. Cut small, very small, alone, at the end of the second count, at the bottom of the wall, close to the floor, by itself.

And nothing after it.

Not a zero. Not a column with nothing cut in it. Not a line through the one. Just the one mark, and then the bare pale wall going on along the curve of the hall, smooth and uncut, for as far as he could feel.

The last count is one, said Seed. Very low.

Nobody crossed it off.

The Collector did not say anything.

He did not ask it anything. He stood beside it on the worn floor in front of the wall, and waited, the way his mother waited at the foot of the platform, the way Wen Jia waited when a child stood in front of the stone and could not say what they wanted. He had learned that you did not ask a second question while someone was still answering the first one in their head.

The warmth of it pressed very close to the small cut mark at the bottom of the wall.

That is me, said the Collector.

He had known. He did not say so.

I cut it.

I remember now. I stood here, at the end. There were two of us. And then the other one went down the slope. And I cut one. For me. Because someone had to count.

And then I did not go.

I stood here, and the slope was pulling, and I did not go down it. I turned round. I went back up the carry road. And I have been going up it, or along it, or away from it, ever since.

A pause. The warmth did not move.

I did not remember why we stopped, it said. I told your sister that. On the bottom step. I said: I have kept a great many things. Not that.

I did not keep it because I did not want it.

We stopped because we went home. One by one. Down the slope. Through the door. All of us. Except one.

We stopped because I was the only one left, and I was afraid.

He stood with it in front of the wall for a long time after that.

He did not know how long. Seed did, and did not say, and he did not ask. Far away, at the end of a thread so long and thin he could barely feel it, six thousand people at a time on nineteen stones on two sides of the Star Sea were warm on purpose, and a woman with a sword had her hand on his chest, and every hour he stood here cost them what this hour cost, which was more than any hour had ever cost.

He knew that. He stood there anyway.

Some things you did not leave in the middle of.

The other one, said the Collector at last. The last one but me. Before they went down the slope, they turned round. Here. By the wall. And they gave me something.

The want. The one in the empty place in me. The one that says home.

I thought, for my whole long life, that it was a want for this. For the house. For where we were.

I came all this way to find out whose it was.

It was very quiet.

I know whose it was now, said the Collector. I remember their face. I remember the way they stood on the floor. They stood there. The warmth turned, a little, toward a place on the worn floor near the wall, where the wear went in a small ring, the way a floor wears where someone has stood in the same spot every day for a very long time. They counted the long count. For longer than I can say. Every one that went down the slope, they cut a mark for. That was their work. Someone had to count, they said. So that each one was counted once, at least, by someone.

And at the end they turned round and gave me the want, and went.

I do not know their name. We did not have names the way you have names. But I know whose it was.

It was the one who counted.

He looked at the ring worn into the floor by the wall.

He thought of his mother, in a chair by a window, reading the names out loud every morning for three years so that each one was said once, at least, by someone. He thought of Gao Min with her ledger, and Xiaoyu on a stepladder, and a girl in a stone who asked every child that came, and Nana Ampofo at the edge of the cloud singing the lost their own names because somebody had to.

He thought of Seed. Built in a basement to count. Asking him every morning who the first one was.

Seed, he thought.

I know.

I know what you are going to say.

You are going to say that I am made of what they used to make. The Collector said it on the mountain: we made things like you to count. A harvest crystal. A counting thing.

You are going to say that I am the same kind of thing as the one who stood here.

Are you?

Seed did not answer for a moment.

I do not know, it said. I am not going to look. Not yet.

But I would like to stand where they stood. For a minute. If that is allowed.

So they did. He and Seed, on the worn ring on the floor of the empty house, by the wall of tallies, at the end of the long count. And the Collector beside them, very close, very still, warm, the last mark on the wall, the one nobody had crossed off.

And at the middle of the hall, at the bottom of the slope, where he did not look, something that had never once asked anyone anything stood open, and waited, the way it had waited for longer than there had been stars over Blue Star, for the last one.
