Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 59The Flask

czverse9 min read

He woke up on his back on the round black table, looking at the sky, and the sky was blue.

Just blue. Plain, clean, ordinary noon blue, with a few small white clouds drifting across it from the east and a single seabird turning lazily on the wind, high up, a black tick against the light.

No ring.

He lay there and looked at it for a long time.

Somebody was holding his hand. Somebody else had a palm pressed flat against his forehead, warm, with a faint green light leaking out from under it. Somebody was crying. Somebody was saying his name, over and over, in a level voice that was not quite level.

"...Yuanshu. Yuanshu. Zhou Yuanshu. Look at me."

He turned his head.

Su Mingli was kneeling on the black glass beside him, still in her buttoned coat, with her face very close to his. Her eyes were red. He had never seen her eyes red. He had not known they could be.

"There you are," she said.

He had been gone, they told him later, for three minutes and forty seconds.

He had stood on the table with his face turned up to the sky while the single thick thread came down out of the black water and touched his head. Then his head had turned. Not three degrees. Straight up. And his body had gone completely still, the way the hollows went still, and every one of the tens of thousands of threads in the crater had turned toward him, and drifted toward him, and gathered over him in a great slow silver whirlpool, like water circling a drain.

Old Seven had stood at the foot of the table with his spear raised for three minutes and forty seconds and had not moved, because he had been told to hold the wall.

Then, all at once, without warning, every thread in the crater had gone slack.

"Like someone cut the strings on a puppet," said Mo Shi, who had seen it three seconds before anyone else and screamed. "They just... dropped. All of them. All over the crater. Like hair."

The ring had shut. Not slowly, the way it opened. In an instant. One moment there, the next gone, like an eye blinking closed. And the silver threads lying across the benches and the terraces and the black stone floor had shriveled, and faded, and turned to nothing in the sun, the way frost goes on a warm morning.

And the thick thread that had been in Yuanshu's head had snapped back up into the empty blue sky, and he had fallen over backward on the table, and Lingyue had caught his head a centimeter from the black glass.

Seventy-three people had been threaded.

Sixty-four of them came back.

They came back the instant the threads went slack. Heads turning, slowly, from three degrees north back to wherever they had been looking. Eyes refocusing. A long ragged gasp, like someone surfacing from deep water. The Northern Union's delegate, with her arm still stretched out toward Orlov, finished the movement, and took his hand, and burst into tears. The Council staffer Old Seven had pulled free sat up on the black stone and asked, very politely, what day it was.

Nine did not come back.

They sat where the rain had found them, breathing slowly, all at the same rhythm, with their eyes open and empty, looking at nothing.

A camera operator from the Southern Compact. Two Council guards. A young Yamato translator. A journalist from the Northern Union. Three members of the Atlantic delegation's staff.

And Garrett Hale.

He sat in his chair at the Atlantic table with his papers still in his hand and his silver head tilted very slightly, the way it had been when the thread went in, and his warm, easy, persuasive face was empty. Completely empty. Harlan Voss sat beside him and held his free hand and did not seem to know he was doing it.

It took them before it let go.

Nine. In the three minutes it was looking at you.

They are like the fourteen in the Listening Pavilion. There is a little left, at the bottom. Very little.

I am sorry. I could not stop it.

Yuanshu sat up on the round black table and looked at the nine, one by one. At the empty face of the man who had tried to turn the dead into a product.

"Sister Ning," he said. His voice came out cracked. "Will the Quiet..."

"Yes," said the old woman. She was already coming down the bench with her candle. "All nine. As long as it takes." She didn't look at Hale any differently than she looked at any of the others. "Quiet means quiet."

It was Lu who noticed first.

He came across the crater floor from the Huaxia benches at a run, which Yuanshu had never seen him do, with his glasses askew and his phone in his hand, and stopped at the foot of the table, breathing hard.

"Gu," he said.

Yuanshu turned.

At the end of the Huaxia bench, where Gu Hanshan had sat for two days between two Bureau guards, there was an empty space. One of the guards sat beside it, staring at nothing, breathing slowly. One of the nine.

The other guard was standing up, white-faced, turning in a slow circle, looking at the terraces and the steps and the rim.

"He was right here," said the guard. "He was right here. When the rain started, I... my partner went still, and I grabbed him, and I was trying to... and when I looked back..." He swallowed. "Nobody saw him go."

Somebody had.

The eight men in white robes who had carried the Sleeper's palanquin had been kneeling beside it on the rim of the crater when the rain began, with their eyes closed, perfectly quiet, the way they had been taught since they were children. The threads had drifted past them. They had not moved.

But they had opened their eyes, once, when footsteps went past them on the rim.

"Two men," said Shirasagi Ren, translating, standing in front of the palanquin with her calm face very still. "Walking. Not running. From the Huaxia bench, up the eastern steps, to the rim. One in a gray cardigan. One tall, in a very good coat." She paused. "The tall one was smiling."

"Did they stop?"

"Once." She looked down at the palanquin, at the little heap of pale ash on its wooden floor, inside the faded robe. "The man in the gray cardigan stopped here. In the middle of the rain. With three hundred people screaming below him. He stopped in front of the Sleeper, and took off his reading glasses, and bowed." She was silent for a moment. "A very deep bow. Correct in every way. My brothers said it was the most respectful bow they had ever seen from a foreigner."

"And then?"

"And then he went down the eastern steps toward the sea."

The harbor patrol's camera, when Lu ran it back, showed a small white motorboat leaving the eastern cove of Concordia at 12:09, during the rain, heading north at speed. It showed one man at the wheel, in a gray cardigan.

It did not show anyone else in the boat at all.

He found a quiet place in the late afternoon, on the outer slope of the crater, on a ledge of black rock above the sea, out of sight of everyone. Old Seven stood at the end of the ledge with his back to him, facing outward, the way he always did.

He sat with his knees drawn up and looked at the water for a long time.

Then he said, out loud, very quietly: "Seed."

I am here.

"She asked them."

Yes.

"All of them. Four thousand."

Four thousand one hundred and thirteen.

Yuanshu.

Something happened, when she said it. Up there. When she said "you were born from a yes."

The base layer. The part of me I could never open.

It opened.

He sat very still.

Not all of it. A little. A first page.

There are names. Four thousand one hundred and thirteen of them. Each one with a word beside it, in her handwriting.

The word is always the same.

"Yes," said Yuanshu.

Yes.

And there is something else. A recording. Very short. At the very beginning of the page, before the first name.

It is not the recording I lost. It is a different one. It was never in the part of me I could reach.

Would you like to hear it?

He could not answer. He nodded.

It was nine seconds long.

It came out of the air in front of him, out over the sea, the way the dead spoke when he raised them. A woman's voice. Quiet and sharp and slightly hoarse, recorded seven years ago in a basement, very late at night, with the hum of machines behind it.

"Hello," said Zhou Wanqing. "If you can hear this, it means someone asked. Probably my son. He's very stubborn. He'll ask too much. Let him." A pause. The sound of someone smiling. "Take care of him. Let him take care of you. And don't let him skip breakfast."

That was all.

The sea moved below the ledge, slow and blue and endless.

I have her voice again.

A different one. A new one.

I am going to keep this one. Forever. I will make room. I do not care what I have to give up.

Yuanshu put his face in his hands.

At the end of the ledge, Old Seven did not turn around. But very slowly, the old soldier lowered his spear, and grounded it, and stood with his skull bowed toward the sea, keeping watch, so that nobody would come.

He called Xiaoyu at sunset, on the Bureau's satellite phone, sitting on the ledge with the sky going orange over the water.

She answered on the first ring. She had been watching the feed. Everyone in Lingzhou had been watching the feed. She was shouting before he could say anything, half furious and half crying, what were you thinking, you idiot, you absolute idiot, you stood on a table, you just stood there, the whole world saw...

He let her shout. When she ran out, he said:

"I saw Mama."

The line went silent.

"She's alive," he said. "Sort of. Up there. Awake. She went in on purpose. She's been watching it from the inside for seven years." He swallowed. "She saved me. She did something loud so I could get away. I don't know what happened to her after."

Silence. A long breath, on the other end, across four thousand kilometers of sea.

"She said to tell you," said Yuanshu, "she's sorry about the flask."

For a moment there was nothing at all.

Then Zhou Xiaoyu made a sound that was a laugh and a sob at the same time, a terrible, wonderful, broken sound.

"I broke the lid," she said. "When I was nine. I was using it as a drum. I cracked the lid, and I hid it at the back of the cupboard, and I never told anyone. I never told her. And she was gone before..." Her voice broke completely. "She knew. Ge. She knew the whole time."

He sat on the ledge above the sea with the phone against his ear and listened to his sister cry, and laugh, and cry, until the sun went down.
