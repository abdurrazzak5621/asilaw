Book 3: The Deep Water

Chapter 245Lantern

czverse12 min read

"I want to go up," he said.

He said it on the vault steps on the fifth morning, with the staff across his knees and the chart on the wall across the banking hall showing ten lines still going up and one, Lingzhou, flat for two days. He said it to Seed, but out loud, so that Lingyue on the stairs could hear, and Kwan at the long table, and Old Seven at the bronze door, because he had learned a long time ago that if he only said things to Seed, nobody could tell him he was wrong.

"Up the thread. To him. Callum Ward."

I know.

I have known since the bell. You have the face.

"He's on the end of a thread being used to see by. Burning. Since the hammer. That's thirty-four days." He held the staff. "Nobody's asked him anything. Not the stone, not the thing that's got him. Not us." He looked at the chart. "I want to ask him what he wants."

"And if he says get me out?" said Lingyue, from the stairs.

"Then I'll find out how."

"And if he says leave me?"

He did not answer at once.

"Then I'll tell his mother," he said. "She's in the queue at Port Halvard with her hand on the stone. Sterling said. Somebody should tell her what he said. Whatever it is."

Lingyue looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the flame in her hand, the size of a peach, pale and steady.

"Not alone," she said.

"It's not a place you can come."

"I know it's not a place I can come." She stood up. "I'm going to sit next to you while you go. With my hand on you. If anything up there touches you that I don't like, I'll feel it, and I'll pull you back." She came down the stairs. "I'm bigger than a grain of rice now. I can pull."

They did it in the Awakening Hall at noon, because that was where the thread was.

Not the Lingzhou thread. Port Halvard's. But all eleven ran up out of the stones and joined somewhere in the dark under the world, and Wen Jia said she could pass him along, sideways, the way the other ten had gone in. She said it the way she said everything, briskly, as if it were obvious.

...I'll take you to the old man's thread first. Port Abiri. Then across to the dock worker's. Port Halvard. He knows you're coming. He says he'll hold your hand on the way up. He says that's what dockers do.

...don't let go of the knot. Not the thread. The knot. The thread's what it pulls on.

The hall was full of holders. It had not been empty since the first night. They stood in a long line from the platform steps down the aisle and out through the side door into the sun on East Ring Road, people of every age, and every few minutes one of them went up the three steps and put their hand on the warm stone and said what they wanted, and the stone asked them about their grammar if it had to, and opened a little holding in them, and they stood there with their hand on it for as long as they could. An hour. Two. Then the next. Gao Min had a chair at the foot of the platform with a ledger on a board across her knees and wrote down every one.

The line made room for him without being asked. He sat down on the platform with his back against the side of the stone, under the bright blue jacket, with the staff across his knees, and Lingyue sat down beside him and put her hand flat on his chest, over his heart, the pale flame in her other hand.

Old Seven stood at the foot of the steps, facing out.

He closed his eyes.

Seed took him up.

Not the way it took him down, into the Death Law, or under it to the first page. Out. The way he had felt it go out on the first night in the hall, through the staff, across the meter of air into the warm black stone, and up the crown of the tree of Laws inside it, and onto the thread.

He had felt Seed go up threads before. He had never gone with it.

It was like being a drop of water on a string in the rain, sliding. Fast. Up through the roof of the hall and the sky over Lingzhou and the blue and then the black. Down, sideways, somewhere in the dark under everything, where eleven fine pale threads ran together like the strands of a rope, and a voice he did not know, very old, very slow, said there you are, boy, I like the sea, hold on, and he was passed along. And then another hand, rough, a docker's hand, a hand that had held ropes on the Atlantic coast for thirty years before something sang, took his, and said, in an accent he had only ever heard on the radio from Sterling: Right then. Up we go. Don't look down. There's nothing down, but don't look.

And up.

He did not know how long. Seed knew. Seed did not tell him. Past the keeper's field, gold and vast and a third smaller than it had been, so close he could feel the warmth of it like a hearth through a wall. Past the place where the branch was, a darkness where the far lights bent, with a small gold bud at its end that turned, he thought, very slightly as he passed, the way a flower turns. Along the arm. Past the place where the Mouth had been.

The cold fire.

He felt it go by under him, below the thread, in the dark: a great still coldness, and at its center, very small, sixty embers standing in a row, facing out.

Huo, he thought, as hard as he could. Huo Changsheng. You held.

He did not know if anything heard.

And then past the end of the arm, into the deep water between arms, where Seed's map had nothing, where there was no light at all, and the thread ran on into the dark, taut, pulled, humming like a wire in a wind.

There.

Ahead. Do not look at all of it. Look at the light.

He looked at all of it.

He could not help it. Nobody could have. Seed had said: bigger than anything. The Shiomi bell had said: bigger than anything. He had thought he knew what that meant. He had seen the keeper's field from the inside, and a Hunger's mouth, and a branch the size of a mountain range across the sky over Lingzhou.

It was not like any of them.

It was not a shape. It was a place where the dark was busier. The way a forest at night is busier than an empty field: not brighter, not darker, but full. Moving. Something enormous and patient and complicated, coming along eleven threads at once out of the deep water, hand over hand. He could not see the hands. He could feel them, the way you feel a crowd behind you in the dark. Thousands. Each one on a thread. Each one finding the next hold and closing on it and pulling, and the whole of it coming on, slowly, steadily, without hurry and without stopping, the way a tide comes up a beach.

It did not see him.

It did not see anything. He understood that at once, the way you understand, standing next to someone in the dark, that their eyes are open and they cannot see you. It had no eyes for him. He was not bright. He was a drop of water on a string. It had no eyes for anything that was not burning.

Except one.

Out in front of it, on a single thread, held out in one of its thousands of hands the way a man holds a lantern out in front of him on a dark road, something was burning.

Bright. So bright it was the only thing in the deep water. White, gold, every color, burning the way Lingyue had burned on the wall, the way the Sixth had burned in the eyes of the Mouth, everything at once, nothing careful, nothing held back. A man. He could see the shape of him in the fire. Young. Broad in the shoulders. Arms out, as if he had been holding something heavy over his head and had never put it down.

The hammer.

Callum Ward.

He is still burning. Thirty-four days.

It is using his light to see the threads. Where they go. Which way. That is how it walks. It holds him out in front and follows what he shows it.

He is being used up.

Slowly. The way a candle is. There is still a great deal of him.

There will not always be.

Callum, he thought.

Nothing. Fire. The roar of it, which was not a sound.

He cannot hear you through that. It is too bright. You have to go nearer.

If you go nearer, the hand will see you. In his light.

I have run it. In most of the runs, it does not know what it is seeing. You are not bright. You are a shadow in his light. It will look at you the way you look at a moth.

In some of the runs, it reaches.

On the platform in Lingzhou, a very long way below, a hand pressed flat on his chest. Warm. He felt it the way you feel your own heartbeat when you are lying very still.

Nearer, he thought.

He went nearer.

Into the light. Into the heat of it, which was not heat, which was the feeling he had had on the platform on the night before Awakening Day, when the stone had opened him and it had been the best thing he had ever felt. He felt it again. A door in his chest. Light. Everything at once. He wanted it. He set his teeth against wanting it, and went nearer, a drop of water on a string sliding into a fire.

And under the roar, very small, very far down, the way a voice is far down at the bottom of a well, someone was talking.

Not to him. To nobody. Over and over. The way a man talks to himself when he has been alone in a bright place for thirty-four days.

...told me what I was. F. Told me what I was. Seven years. Docks. F. Lowest it gives. Told me. So I told it. I told it what it was. Swung. Hit it. Told it.

...didn't know it'd feel like this.

...so bright. It's so bright. Never been bright. F. Never been anything. Look at me now. Look at me.

...where's Mum.

...where's Mum.

Callum.

The talking stopped.

The fire did not. The fire roared on, white and gold and every color, held out in a vast hand in the deep water. But under it, at the bottom of the well, the voice had stopped, the way a voice stops when it hears its own name for the first time in thirty-four days.

...who's that.

My name's Zhou Yuanshu. I'm from Lingzhou. I'm the one with the thing in my head.

...the one on the telly. The orchard. The sack.

Yes.

...the one who broke all eleven. Something that in a man not burning might have been a laugh. ...I only did one. You did all eleven. With your hand.

I didn't mean to.

...I did.

The fire roared.

...is my mum all right?

She's in the queue at your stone. At Port Halvard. With her hand on it. Holding it down. So the thing that took you can't take anything else.

The voice at the bottom of the well went quiet for a long time.

...that's Mum, said Callum Ward. ...that's exactly Mum.

Callum, he said. I'm going to ask you something. First I'm going to tell you what I'll do with the answer.

...go on then.

If you want to come out, I'll find out how. I don't know how yet. I'll find out. If you want to go through the door, the one at the bottom of everything, where things go when they're finished, I'll open it for you. It's always a little open now. If you want to stay where you are, I'll tell your mum that's what you said. Whatever you say, I'll tell her. Exactly. He held on, in the light, to the knot and not the thread. What do you want?

The fire roared.

He waited. He did not look in the bowl.

...I don't know.

The voice was very small.

...I want to stop. It's using me up. I can feel it. Like a candle. Like you said.

...and I don't. I want to stop and I don't. Because I've never been bright. Not once. Twenty-five years. And now I'm the brightest thing out here. It holds me out in front. It follows me. I'm the only thing it can see.

...I'm the lantern. I've never been anything and now I'm the lantern.

...how can I want to stop being that?

He did not have an answer. He stayed there in the fire with the voice at the bottom of the well, and did not have an answer, and did not pretend to.

...ask me again, said Callum Ward. ...tomorrow. Can you? Ask me again tomorrow.

Yes.

And the hand turned.

He felt it before Seed said anything. The vast hand that held the lantern out in front of the thing in the deep water, one of thousands, turned. Slowly. The way a man holding a lantern on a dark road turns it, when he thinks he has seen something move at the edge of the light.

Toward the shadow in the fire.

It sees you.

In his light. It does not know what you are. But it has seen something on the thread, in front of it, that should not be there.

Come back. Now.

He let go.

Not of the knot. Of the fire. He let himself slide, back down the thread, a drop of water falling, fast, faster, out of the light and into the dark, along the arm, past the cold fire and the place where the Mouth had been and the branch with its bud and the keeper's warmth, a docker's rough hand catching his on the way down, steady, boy, steady, an old man's voice, I like the sea, and a girl's, brisk, got you, got you, you idiot, and then—

A hand on his chest. Warm. Pulling.

He opened his eyes on the platform of the Awakening Hall in Lingzhou with Lingyue's face over his, white, and the flame in her other hand blazing the size of a lamp, and blood running out of his nose into his mouth, and a line of two thousand people with their hands on a black stone staring at him.

"You went too near," said Shen Lingyue. "I felt it. You went too near."

"Yes."

"Did you ask him?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"Ask again tomorrow."

There is something else.

I am sorry. I would rather tell you now than in the morning.

He was lying on the narrow bed in the back room. It was evening. Bai Lu had been and gone, and said idiot in three languages, and stopped the bleeding.

The hand that turned.

It did not turn back.

When we came down the thread, it followed. Not the thing. One hand. It let go of the thread it was on, and took hold of the one we were on, and it is coming down it.

Faster than the rest of it. Much faster. A hand is lighter than the whole.

The Port Halvard thread.

Nine days.
