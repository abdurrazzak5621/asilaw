Book 4: Other Orchards

Chapter 290This Is Not Your Face

czverse12 min read

She wrote eleven things and crossed out all of them.

She told him afterward. Not in a mark; in person, months later, on the vault steps, with the marker in her hand, because she said some things were not for marks. She said she had sat on the floor of the platform of the Awakening Hall in front of the warm stone from two in the morning until a quarter to five with a sheet of ledger paper on her knee and the body of a twelve-hundred-year-old nun beside her holding a lamp, and written eleven things, and crossed them all out.

YOU ARE NOT YOUR GRADE. Crossed out. She said it was true but it was the kind of true you argue with.

THE LADDER IS GONE. Crossed out. She said it was true but it was not hers to say.

WHAT DO YOU WANT? Crossed out. Ichiro already asked that. On the ninth night. Everyone.

SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU. Crossed out. That was Seri's. It was only true for people in walls.

Seven more. Crossed out.

At a quarter to five, Wen Jia spoke from the knot. Through no staff; there was no staff, it was on Anthos with her brother. In letters. In the air over the stone, in the hand that was not the stone's, small, so that only a girl sitting on the floor of the platform could read them.

[You're doing the face.]

Xiaoyu looked up.

[Your brother's face. The step face. Six hours.]

[Your mother's coming. I told the bus driver. He's bringing her.]

Zhou Wanqing came into the Awakening Hall at five in the morning in her wheelchair, pushed by the driver of the Number Nine, who had gone to the Ossuary in the dark without being asked and knocked on the bronze door and said to Fat Tan, the stone says the girl needs her mother, and Fat Tan had not argued.

His mother did not say anything for a while. She sat at the foot of the platform steps and looked at her daughter on the floor in front of the stone with eleven things crossed out on a sheet of ledger paper.

"What's it for?" she said at last.

"Every face in the Federation," said Xiaoyu. "The discs. Round everyone's throat. They were all cut from the Lens. Ichiro can send a mark to all of them at once. Billions. Ge says it has to be true. Mine are." Her voice cracked a little. "I don't know what to say to billions of people. I've only ever written names."

Her mother looked at her.

"What did you want to be told," said Zhou Wanqing, "when you were fifteen? The year before the stone. When you couldn't sleep because you were afraid it would say Scribe, Talent D, and everyone would go oh."

Xiaoyu did not answer.

"You told me," said her mother. "At breakfast. Eleven months ago. You said: what if I put my hand on it and it says what I am, forever, because a rock said so." She folded her thin hands on the blanket. "What did you want someone to tell you?"

Xiaoyu sat on the floor of the platform with the marker in her hand.

"That it wasn't me," she said slowly. "Whatever it said. That it was just a rock."

"And?"

"And that I wasn't anything yet." She looked at the sheet of paper. "Until I said."

Her mother was quiet for a moment.

"I said that once," said Zhou Wanqing. "On a slope. In the cemetery. To a boy who came down a road of light and asked if being an F was all right here."

"I know," said Xiaoyu. "I wrote it down."

She wrote it at ten past five.

Not on the paper. On the floor of the platform, in front of the warm black stone with the blue jacket on its shoulder and its runes pointing down, in square black capitals, the way she had written THE HOUSE IS EMPTY and SOMEONE CAN HEAR YOU SINGING. Big. So that it was written. So that nobody could say afterward it was not there.

Two lines.

THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE.

NOBODY IS ANYTHING UNTIL THEY SAY WHAT THEY WANT TO BE.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it.

Then, under it, smaller, because she said a mark should say who sent it, the way Iselde's had, and the way the House is empty had not, and she had always minded that:

— ZHOU XIAOYU. SCRIBE. NOT MARKED. WHO WAS THERE.

...that's it, said Wen Jia, from the knot. Out loud, this time; there was no staff, but there were letters, and the letters said it, and then said it again. [That's it. That's the one.]

...I'm going to tie it now. Properly. The way the small one with the legs showed me.

...it's going a long way. Further than anything I've ever sent. Up my thread, into the net, through the knot in the deep water, and down into the Lens. And then out of the Lens into everything it was ever cut into.

...billions.

...I'm a bit scared.

"Me too," said Xiaoyu.

...good, said the girl in the stone. ...you're supposed to be. That means it's real.

She tied it.

And sent it.

On Anthos, it was dawn.

He was on the observation deck of the flagship, at the window, with the burning world below and the bowl of black stone on the terminator, open to the sky, where the Assembly would sit in four hours. He had not slept. Lingyue was asleep against the window with Xiaodeng in the sling. Old Seven was in the doorway. In the bow of the ship, in a disc of black stone the width of a courtyard, a lamp burned and a fog-ringer waited.

It is coming.

I can feel it in the Lens. Ichiro has it. He says: oh. He says: oh, she's good. He says: she's better than me.

He is going to send it.

He is asking you first.

Yes, he thought. Send it.

He says: right then.

He says: everybody. Look at your face.

He did not feel it go. It was too big. It went out of the Lens the way light goes out of a lamp: not travelling, just there, everywhere at once. Into every disc of black stone ever cut from the root. On every fine chain round every throat. On eight thousand five hundred and eighty-eight worlds.

He saw some of it.

Seed showed him. What it could. Through the Lens, through the faces, the way it had once seen through the cells of the Candle. Glimpses. A thousand. A million. Too many to hold, so it held a few and let the rest go past.

A Paragon of the House of Teller, on a terrace above a burning city, with her hand at her throat, looking down at a disc of black stone in her palm in which, for the first time since the ninth night, light was moving. Not the old light. Not like water. Two lines, in square black capitals that were not in her language and that she could read anyway.

A C in the yards at Anthos, a man of fifty with grease on his hands, standing in a corridor with his face in his palm, reading it, and then taking the chain off over his head and putting the disc on a shelf, carefully, the way you put something down that you might want later, and walking away from it.

A child of eleven on a world Seed did not have a name for, in a reading-hall, the day before her reading, crying, holding up the small blank disc her parents had bought her for the morning, the one that would be cut from the stone and marked tomorrow, which had something written in it now that nobody had cut there.

A dock worker on Corraine. A Fleet Captain of the House of Iron in the ninth tier of an empty Assembly, alone, at dawn, with a disc with two gold marks in his hand and his shoulders shaking.

And a woman of sixty-one in deep red, standing beside a black stone in a reading-hall below the House of Anthos, where she had come at dawn because tomorrow was a reading morning and she had said in the Assembly that she would stand there, with her daughter beside her, twelve in the body and twenty-three inside, holding her hand. Both of them looking down at the discs in their palms. The Matriarch's, with three gold marks. Maelis's, which had been Iselde's, crossed out in front of the Assembly, given to her sister, kept in a box.

Both of them saying the same thing. Two lines. True.

THIS IS NOT YOUR FACE.

NOBODY IS ANYTHING UNTIL THEY SAY WHAT THEY WANT TO BE.

Yuanshu.

It was not a question.

Ichiro asked them on the ninth night. This was not that. This was a mark. The kind the stones used to send. Except it did not say how bright. It did not say F or E or SSS. It did not say where on the Ladder.

It said the thing at their throat was not them.

And it was true. When they read it, they knew it. The way the one who picks knew the house was empty.

I am counting.

I cannot count it.

I think nine thousand years of faces just found out they are not faces.

The Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds sat at nine.

He walked into the bowl of black stone with the others. Old Seven, one-armed, at the front. The Third Battalion in a line. Lingyue with the baby and the sword and the lamp. Tam and Seri. Bai Lu. Iselde. The First of the Ladder in a C's gray coat. Down the aisle, to the platform at the bottom, the place where you stood to be told what you were.

The four thousand seats were full.

And not one of them was burning.

He stopped on the aisle.

Four thousand seats, that had been lit yesterday, every one, in the colors of its House or its world. Dark. Not off. Not hidden. Turned down. The way a lamp is turned down by someone who has decided they do not need it all the way up to be seen. Four thousand people sitting in the bowl of black stone in the morning light that came down through the open top of it, in the colors they had been born in, not burning.

And at four thousand throats, on four thousand fine chains, four thousand discs of black stone, with light moving in them. Two lines.

Some of the chains were not round throats any more. Some were in hands. Some were on the seats beside their owners. Some were not there at all.

The Speaker stood up.

Aurelle Teller. In white, at the front of the lowest tier, in the seat of white stone. Old. Very old. And at her throat, on its fine chain, a disc with four gold marks, and light in it, moving.

She did not sit down again. She stood above the platform and looked down at the boy at the bottom of the aisle, and at the man in the gray coat beside him.

"The twenty-nine Houses," she said, "asked the Assembly to break the Lens, restore the Ladder, and choose a new First."

She said it without a grade.

"The Assembly will now decide."

She looked up, at four thousand seats, turned down.

"The Speaker is not going to tell you what you are," said Aurelle Teller. "A girl on an orchard in the Shallows who is not marked says that nobody is anything until they say what they want to be. The Speaker read it in her face this morning, and it was true." She paused. "So the Speaker is going to ask."

She turned to the first tier.

"House of Anthos," she said. "What do you want?"

And Corvane Varr-Anthos stood up in the fourth tier, in deep red, with her daughter's crossed-out disc in one hand and her own in the other, and said, so that every seat heard it:

"Ask every child first."

It took eleven hours.

Not a vote. The Assembly of the Lamplit Worlds had never voted; it had decreed, by precedence, in the order of the Ladder. Today there was no order. There was no Ladder. There was a Speaker of great age standing in a bowl of black stone, without her grade, asking every House and every world, one at a time, in no order at all except the order they happened to be sitting in, what it wanted.

And every one of them answered. Out loud. In front of the others.

He did not say any of them back. Nobody asked him to. He stood on the platform at the bottom of the Assembly with the staff in his hand and listened, the way a fog-ringer in a stone had told him listening was half of talking.

Three thousand six hundred and twelve said: ask every child first. Or something that meant it. Open the arrays. Let the stones ask. Let the cores out. Let them go home rowed.

Three hundred and sixty-one said: I don't know. And the Speaker said: then the Assembly will ask you again tomorrow. And wrote it down.

Twenty-seven said: restore the Ladder.

At eight in the evening, Aurelle Teller sat down for the first time in eleven hours, very slowly, the way old people sit down when their knees decide for them.

"The Assembly has been asked," she said. "The Assembly has answered."

She looked down at the platform.

"The Lens will not be broken. There are two people in it. They were asked. They said yes. If anyone wants them out, they may ask them."

"Every child on every world of the Federation will be asked what they want to become before any stone reads them. Until someone chooses to sit in each stone and ask, a living person chosen by that world will stand beside it and ask first."

"The arrays of every House that answered will be opened. Asked first. Rowed home."

"And there will be no First."

She paused.

"The Speaker would like it noted," said Aurelle Teller, "that she has been Speaker of the Assembly for a hundred and nine years and has never once been asked what she wants."

The bowl of black stone was silent.

"The Speaker would like to be asked," said the oldest Paragon of the Lamplit Worlds. "Tomorrow."

The twenty-seven did not stay.

They stood up when she finished, all at once, in the tiers where the twenty-nine Houses sat: eleven Paragons and sixteen Fleet Captains, the ones who had said in front of everyone, the day before and again today, that what they wanted was to be first. They stood up in the bowl of black stone with their faces at their throats, lit, two lines in each of them, true, which they had read at dawn and could not unread.

And they walked out.

Not in a line. Not holding each other's shoulders. One at a time, up the aisles, in their colors, burning, the only ones in the Assembly still burning, out through the doors at the top of the bowl into the evening of Anthos.

They are going to their ships.

Twenty-seven of the twenty-nine.

The C's on those ships are talking. Tam can hear them.

They are going to the Lens.

Old Seven, at the front of the platform, turned his skull. Not toward the doors. Up. Through the open top of the bowl. At the sky over Anthos, where a cathedral that had been the flagship of the House of Severin hung in orbit with a disc of black stone the width of a courtyard in its bow, and a lamp in the disc, and a fog-ringer.

He says: they are going to try to break it.

He says: they read the mark. They know it is true. They cannot unread it.

He says: so they are going to try to break the thing it came through.

He says: that is what you do, when you cannot unread something.

He says: you try to break the stone.
