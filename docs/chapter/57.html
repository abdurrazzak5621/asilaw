Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 57Rain on Concordia

czverse9 min read

The first thread touched a journalist on the top terrace.

Yuanshu saw it happen. A young man with a camera around his neck and a candle in his hand, standing at the rope with his face turned up to the widening ring, his mouth open. A single silver strand came down out of the black water in the sky, slow as a falling feather, and touched the top of his head, and went into it.

He stopped.

The candle in his hand tipped, and fell, and rolled down the terrace step, still burning.

His head turned. Three degrees. North.

Then the rest of the rain arrived.

It came down over the crater like a curtain being dropped.

Thousands of threads. Tens of thousands. Fine and silver and shining in the noon sun, falling out of the ring in long slow sheets, drifting, swaying, settling. Over the press terraces. Over the benches. Over the round black table. Over three hundred people who had, ten seconds ago, been holding candles and crying with joy.

Someone screamed. Then many people.

"Quiet!" Sister Ning was on her feet on the Huaxia bench with her candle held high. "Quiet! Don't think! Think about rice!"

It was no good. Yuanshu could feel it. Three hundred minds blazing with terror, all at once, so loud that the old woman's voice was a candle in a hurricane. Down the bench, the twelve of the Quiet sat with their eyes closed and their candles straight, perfectly still, and the threads drifted past them as if they weren't there. But everyone else...

A Council staffer in a gray uniform stopped mid-run. Turned. Three degrees.

The Northern Union's delegate, reaching for Orlov's hand, froze with her arm out.

A woman on the press terrace. A man on the steps. A guard at the edge of the floor.

All over the crater, heads were turning north.

The Kings moved.

Su Mingli did not raise her hands. She simply opened her eyes, and the air above the Huaxia benches bent. The light curved. The silver threads drifting down toward the witnesses' row slowed, and bowed, and slid sideways off an invisible slope like rain off a roof, and fell harmlessly onto the black stone beyond the benches, where they lay twitching like hair.

Behind the Northern Union's flag, Marshal Orlov breathed out, and the air around him turned white. Frost spread across the stone at his feet in a ring, and the threads falling into it froze in midair, and hung there, glittering, and snapped like icicles.

Shirasagi Ren knelt on the floor of the crater with both palms flat on the stone and her eyes closed, and around the Yamato bench, a faint pale dome rose out of the ground, like breath on glass, and the threads touched it and slid away.

And the Atlantic Federation's King, the heavy-shouldered man with the shaved head and the silver beard, roared, and swept one arm across the air, and a wall of force went out from him like a shockwave and blew a hundred threads to tatters.

For three seconds, it looked like it might be enough.

Then the ring opened wider, and the rain doubled.

"Yuanshu!"

Lingyue was coming down the terraces toward him at a dead run, her coat flying, the cloth torn off her sword. She hit the crater floor and drew in the same motion, and the blade lit white-gold like a strip of the noon sun.

A thread drifted down toward the Southern Compact's delegate, who was frozen in his chair with his hands over his ears.

Lingyue cut it.

The light went through the silver strand like a hot wire through cobweb. It parted with a sound like a violin string snapping. The cut end above recoiled up toward the sky, writhing. The cut end below fell across the delegate's shoulder and shriveled into nothing.

The delegate gasped and doubled over, clutching his head, as if someone had slapped him awake.

"It works!" Lingyue was already moving, already cutting. "Light burns it! Mo! Bai Lu!"

They came down the terraces behind her.

Mo Shi had his eyes closed and both hands pressed to his temples. "Left, three seconds, the old man! Right, five, the girl with the camera!" And Mo Xing, beside him, white-faced, folded space: a ripple in the air, and a thread falling toward an old man's head bent sideways into nothing and came out a meter above the sea, three hundred meters away, beyond the crater rim. Again. Again. Every fold made him stagger.

Bai Lu ran from turned head to turned head along the benches, pressing her palms to temples, green light flowing into them. "It doesn't take them out," she was sobbing. "It doesn't take them out, it just makes them hurt less!"

And the dead walked through the rain as if it were nothing at all.

Old Seven went down the center of the crater with his spear, and the threads did not touch him. They fell through the space around him, past his ribs, through the gray flame in his chest, and he didn't even notice them. He walked to the Council staffer frozen in the middle of the floor, and reached up with one long bony hand, and took hold of the silver thread above her head, and pulled.

It came away in his fingers like a strand of hair. The woman collapsed, gasping.

They cannot see him.

They cannot see any of the dead.

Lu Xuanming wrote it in the notebook, the last night of the Black Tide. "The dead have nothing it wants. They could walk right up to it, and it didn't even look at them."

Iron Wall Zhao came over the rim of the crater from the guesthouses at a run, a broad skeleton under a great empty shield of rusted iron, and dropped down the terraces and set himself in front of the press section, and raised the shield over thirty frightened people like a roof. The threads fell through it. But behind it, under its shadow, the journalists crouched and closed their eyes and went, at last, a little quieter.

And on the rim of the crater, a shape of ash and dark air with an iron arrowhead for a heart drew a bow that wasn't there, and Hundred-Arrow Lin began to shoot.

Black arrows. Dozens. Each one cutting a thread a meter above a head. Each one perfect.

It was not enough.

Yuanshu stood in the middle of the crater floor beside the round black table, with the rain falling all around him, and saw it with terrible clarity. Three Kings holding three domes. A girl with a sword. Twins folding space until they bled from the nose. A healer. Three dead soldiers. A dozen silent nuns.

And tens of thousands of threads.

Around the edges of the crater, where the Kings couldn't reach, more heads were turning every second. Fifty. Sixty. On the upper terraces. On the steps. Among the Council's guards. The Atlantic delegation, half outside their King's wall, frozen in their chairs. Garrett Hale, standing with a sheaf of papers in his hand, his silver head turned three degrees north.

Seventy-three.

Seventy-three people threaded.

It has not started to pull yet.

It is listening. Learning them. The way it did in Yamato. It learns, and then it cuts.

We do not know how long that takes.

Where's it looking? What does it want?

A pause.

I have been watching the threads. Most of them drift. They fall where the noise is.

But some of them do not drift.

Some of them are searching. Sweeping back and forth across the crater floor. Slowly. Like a hand feeling along a wall in the dark.

Yuanshu, they are sweeping toward the sixth chair.

It came here to watch the hearing. And to find the one it could not find in Lingzhou. The one who makes the dead stand up. The one who let its harvest go home, yesterday, out of that black box.

It cannot find you because you are quiet.

So it is listening to everyone else, very hard, until you are not.

He understood.

He stood in the middle of the crater in the silver rain and understood all of it at once, the way you understand a fall in the instant before you hit the ground.

It was hurting them to find him. Every head that turned, every thread that went in, it was putting them there to listen for him. To learn the shape of the room he was in. To hear him when he finally broke.

And he was going to break. Everyone did. Nobody could hold the quiet forever.

No.

I know what you are thinking.

I can hear it, even through the quiet. Yuanshu. No.

If it finds me, it stops looking. If it stops looking, it stops threading them.

You do not know that.

It's the best thing I've got.

It will learn you. The monk said. "If the thread ever finds you, it will learn you very fast."

We do not know what happens to someone it learns that fast.

Then you'll be there, thought Yuanshu. Asking it questions it's never heard.

A long, long pause. The rain fell.

...yes.

I will be there.

He climbed up onto the round black table.

The polished volcanic glass was cold and slippery under his shoes. From up here he could see the whole crater: the three shining domes, the white-gold flash of Lingyue's sword, the frost around Orlov, the great iron shield over the press, the arrows flicking out of the sky, and everywhere, everywhere, silver rain, and heads turning north.

On the Huaxia bench, Su Mingli saw him. Her eyes went wide. Her mouth opened.

He didn't wait for her to say it.

He let go of the quiet.

All of it. All at once. Not gently. Not the way you set something down. The way you throw something as hard as you can. Everything he'd been holding for three days came out of him in a single blast. The green flask. The gray crystal. Oh love, we want to stop. Four hundred days. A boy's little finger curling in the dark. A monk turning to ash. A woman in the back of a car in the rain. Fear and grief and fury and a love so big it didn't fit inside his chest.

And a name, with it. Shouted, inside, with the whole of his mind, at the black water in the sky.

I'M HERE.

Every thread in the crater stopped.

Tens of thousands of silver strands, all at once, hanging motionless in the noon air above three hundred people, like a held breath.

Then, together, all of them, they turned.

Not three degrees.

All the way.

Toward the boy standing on the round black table in the middle of the crater, with his arms at his sides and his face turned up to the sky.

On the rim of the crater, Hundred-Arrow Lin lowered her bow that wasn't there. At the foot of the table, Old Seven's gray flame went white, and he took one step forward, spear rising.

"Hold the wall," said Yuanshu, without looking down. "All of you. Hold it. Protect them. That's the rule."

The old soldier stopped.

And out of the black water at the center of the ring, slow and silver and thick as a rope, a single thread came down.
