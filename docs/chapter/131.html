Book 2: The Star Sea

Chapter 131The Adjutant

czverse10 min read

He read the night at noon, in the keeper's hut, with the door open and Sister Ning sitting on the step.

The hut had not changed since he was twelve and swept the cemetery paths for pocket money. One room. A cot. A kettle on a gas ring. A wooden table with cigarette burns in it and a calendar on the wall from a hardware shop that had closed four years ago. On the shelf over the cot, a photograph in a cheap frame: a young soldier in a Black Tide-era uniform, thin and serious, standing on a wall beside a tired man with a white staff.

Grandpa Xu's body lay on the cot under his own padded coat. Yuanshu sat on the wooden chair beside it with the staff across his knees and one hand on the old man's folded hands.

Sister Ning lit her candle. She did not usually light it. She lit it now and set it on the step beside her, and the flame stood up straight in the still air.

"Go on," she said. "He asked you to."

Memory Reading. Consent given before death, and again after.

Going in.

The first thing was cold.

Not the cold of the hut. The cold of the north wall of Lingzhou thirty-one years ago, at the end of the last night of the Black Tide, with the wind coming off the Wastes and the sky in the north a lens of black water. Grandpa Xu had been forty-six. He had been standing on the battlements with a field radio on his back that had stopped working an hour earlier, beside a man who did not need a radio.

Lu Xuanming was fifty-two.

He did not look like the posters. He did not look like a monster or a hero or a man who was about to raise two million of the dead. He looked like a man who had not slept in three days, which was what he was, with gray in his beard and a white staff in his hand that had a small skull at the top with silver around its eyes. His eyes were closed. His lips were moving.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Below the wall, the dead of three cities were standing up.

Yuanshu watched it through Grandpa Xu's eyes, and Seed watched it through Yuanshu's, and for a long time neither of them said anything.

It was not what the history books said. The books said Lu Xuanming had raised two million in an instant, with a single word, in a wave of violet light. The books had not been on the wall.

He raised them nine at a time.

Nine. A pause. Nine more. Each time he counted to nine, nine more of the dead stood up somewhere in the dark below the wall, in the streets, in the river towns, in the fields. And each time they stood, he said something to them. Not an order. Grandpa Xu had been close enough to hear. Two words, under his breath, the same two every time.

Count them.

And the nine who had just stood up turned, each of them, to the nine nearest them, and began to count. And when the next nine stood, the first nine counted them too. And the next. And the next. Like stitches. Like a net being knotted, one knot at a time, each knot tied to its neighbors, until the net was so large that one tired man on a wall could not possibly have held it, and did not have to, because he was only holding the top.

I have it.

Yuanshu. I have the shape.

It is not a chain of command. It is a mesh. Every node watches nine. Every node is watched by nine. He holds one node, and through it, all of them.

It is how he carried two million. It is how a man with an ordinary class and a ten-percent Death Law did the impossible.

Modeling.

The model was beautiful. Seed laid it out behind his eyes, a lattice of light, nine by nine by nine, so clean and so efficient that his own load, holding twenty-four thousand dead one by one, looked like a man trying to carry water in his bare hands.

With this, your load for the First Legion and the cemetery falls from seventy-four percent to under forty.

You hold the top. They hold each other.

He almost said yes. He almost told Seed to build it into the Legion right now, today, at noon, in a dead man's hut.

Then he kept watching the memory.

It went on past the part the books knew. Past the faceless one falling out of the sky. Past the dead tearing it apart. Past dawn, gray over the end of the world, and Lu Xuanming sitting down on the battlements with his back to the stone, the way Grandpa Xu had sat down against stone No. 7 a few hours ago.

He was still counting. His lips were still moving.

Then he stopped.

He had not died. Not then. Grandpa Xu remembered that clearly, the way you remember the moment a clock stops ticking in a quiet room. Lu Xuanming had stopped counting because he was too tired to go on, and his head had dropped forward onto his chest, and he had fallen asleep sitting up on the wall with the white staff across his knees.

And below the wall, in the dark, two million of the dead went on counting.

Nine. And nine. And nine. Each node watching nine. Nobody holding the top. Nobody to say stop.

When Lu Xuanming woke up at noon, two hundred thousand of them had already started walking north.

...oh.

Yuanshu sat very still in the wooden chair in the keeper's hut.

The march.

The two hundred thousand in the valley. Walking north for thirty-one years, because nobody told them they could stop.

Sergeant Huo said: you don't break ranks. Nobody told us we could stop.

It was not an order that kept them walking. It was the lattice.

Every node was watching nine. And none of the nine had ever been asked if they wanted to be watched. So none of them could stop, because stopping meant leaving the eight beside them. They held each other in place. Forever.

The model is beautiful. And the model is the reason two hundred thousand of the dead walked to the end of the world.

He had nearly built it into the First Legion an hour ago.

He sat and looked at the old man under the padded coat on the cot, and thought about what it would have meant. Twenty-four thousand dead knotted together in a net with him at the top. And then one night he would be too tired, or hurt, or asleep, or dead, and the net would go on holding itself, and nobody inside it would be able to let go.

Seed. What's wrong with it? Exactly.

Thinking.

...the knots.

In his lattice, each node counts its nine. Count means: keep track of. Hold in place.

None of them could say no to being counted. They were never asked. He did not have time.

So the lattice has no way out. A node cannot leave without tearing the eight beside it.

Then change the knot.

To what?

He thought about the six skeletons who had stood around Grandpa Xu at stone No. 7. Nobody had told them to. They had come up out of their own graves and made a ring around the only living man on the hill, and when the cat came over the stone, all six of them had turned at once.

Not because they were counting.

Because they were asking. Each of them, of the ones beside them. Are you all right. Are you still here. Do you need me.

Not count, he thought. Ask.

Every node asks its nine. Every morning. Are you still here? Do you still want to be? If one of them says no, the eight let go of it. Gently. And close the gap. And nobody is ever held in place by anything except their own yes.

A long silence behind his eyes.

...that is slower.

It costs more than his lattice. Not as much as holding them one by one. Your load would be about fifty percent, not forty.

And it has a way out.

Every node can leave. If you fall asleep, the lattice does not keep walking. It asks itself if it wants to keep walking. And it can say no.

Build that one.

Building.

[New technique: The Asking Lattice. Every soldier asks its nine. Every one can leave.]

It is not his. It is yours. He would have built it if he had had time.

The Law Core took the rest of the afternoon.

He built it the way he had learned to build them in a cemetery a year ago, on the stone of a dead captain who had flinched away from a perfect model of his own shield. Not on the echo. With it. He sat in the keeper's hut and asked Grandpa Xu what he had been, and Grandpa Xu told him.

He had awakened at eighteen as a Signaler. An ordinary class. Sound Law, eleven percent, the kind that lets you hear a radio through a wall and make yourself heard across a battlefield without shouting. He had been posted to the north wall as a radio operator, and then as an adjutant, because he was the only signaler who could stand next to a necromancer without being sick. He had carried orders for Lu Xuanming for nineteen years. He had never once carried one he agreed with.

What do you want it to be? Yuanshu asked him.

...a relay. That's what I was. That's all I was. I passed things along.

...but I'd like to pass along the right things this time.

So they built a relay.

Sound Law, eleven percent. A signaler's whole life of listening for the one voice in a battle that mattered and carrying it to the people who needed to hear it. And the memory of a tired man on a wall, counting, and the memory of what happened when he stopped. Seed held the shape. Yuanshu held the door. Grandpa Xu, from the echo, told them when they had it wrong, which was often, and in language that made Sister Ning raise her eyebrows on the step.

At dusk it was done.

[Law Core forged: The Ninefold Relay.]

[Sound Law 11% + Death Law 31.2%.]

[Bound: Adjutant Xu. Commander. Permanent.]

He is the knot at the top of the Asking Lattice. You ask him. He asks the commanders. They ask their nine.

Load, with the Relay and the Lattice: fifty-one percent.

You can fight again.

Grandpa Xu stood up off the cot at sunset.

He was not a big skeleton. He had been a small old man, and he was a small old skeleton, with a slight stoop in the spine and bone hands that were thick across the knuckles. There was a gray flame in his chest, low and steady. He looked down at himself for a while.

Then he looked at the shelf over the cot, and the photograph in the cheap frame, of a young soldier and a tired man on a wall.

He picked up his wooden cane from where it leaned against the table. He did not need it. He took it anyway.

Old Seven was waiting outside the hut.

He had stood there all afternoon, at the door, beside Sister Ning on the step, with his spear grounded and his gray flame very small. When the small stooped skeleton came out of the hut with the cane in its hand, Old Seven came to attention.

The two of them stood in the doorway of the keeper's hut, in the last of the light, looking at each other.

Adjutant Xu says: Squad Leader Qi.

Old Seven says: Adjutant.

Adjutant Xu says: nineteen dumplings.

Old Seven says: he will need to find a way to eat them first.

Adjutant Xu says that is not his problem.

Sister Ning laughed. It was the first time Yuanshu had ever heard her laugh. It was short and dry and surprised, and she put her hand over her mouth afterward, and then took it away.

"He's going to be insufferable," she said.

"He was always insufferable," said Yuanshu.

On the step, the candle had burned down to a stub. She pinched it out.

Eight and a half days.

Plus or minus two.
