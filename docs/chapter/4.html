Book 1: The Grave Under the City

Chapter 4The Hearing

czverse8 min read

By seven in the morning, the footage had been watched four hundred million times.

In a noodle shop on East Market Road, the owner turned the TV up and nobody complained. In a hospital waiting room, a nurse stopped with a tray in her hands and watched it twice. In a schoolyard, a group of ten-year-olds lined up in rows and saluted a boy standing on a bench, and then fell down laughing.

Three hundred dead soldiers in violet mist. One salute. A boy with blood on his face.

The headlines could not agree on a word.

CALAMITY. MIRACLE. THE SECOND BLACK TIDE? WHO IS ZHOU YUANSHU?

In the Atlantic Federation, a senator stood up in a marble chamber and demanded an emergency session. In the Northern Union, the state channel played the clip on a loop with no commentary at all, which was somehow worse. In Yamato, in a room with paper walls, a council of old men watched it nine times without speaking.

And in Lingzhou, at nine o'clock sharp, the Awakening Bureau convened a hearing.

The Hearing Room was on the forty-second floor.

It was built to make people feel small. A half circle of five raised seats behind a curved desk of dark wood. A single long table facing them, lower, for whoever was being heard. Behind the commissioners, a wall of glass, floor to ceiling, so that whoever sat at the long table had to look up at five faces with the whole city spread out behind them.

From up here, the War Memorial Cemetery was a small green square in the east. Above it, the violet scar of last night's rift was still faintly visible, like a pencil line someone had tried to erase.

Yuanshu sat at the long table in a plain black shirt.

Su Mingli sat on his right, legs crossed, hands folded on her knee. General Han Tieshan stood behind both of them with his arms folded across his chest. He was so large and so still that the two armed guards by the door had, without discussing it, both taken a step backward.

In the center seat sat Director Lu Zhengtian.

He was in his fifties, slim, neat, with silver-rimmed glasses and the kind of pleasant face that looked like it smiled often. When Yuanshu came in, he nodded to him warmly, as if they were old family friends meeting at a wedding.

Heart rate: 58. Perfectly steady.

That is unusual for a man who ordered a forty-minute lockout while a civilian was in danger.

Keep watching him.

"This is a procedural hearing," Lu began. His voice was soft and reasonable. "No one in this room is on trial. We're here because the law requires it."

He touched a screen in the desk, and the glass wall behind him dimmed and lit up with an image: the eastern field at 3:20 a.m., three hundred skeletons standing in ranks in the mist.

"At 3:07 this morning, Zhou Yuanshu raised three hundred human remains from a protected cemetery, without a permit, in a city that lost ninety million people to the last Necromancer who did the same." He spread his hands, almost apologetic. "Under Article Three of the Black Tide Act, the Bureau may detain any awakener who carries out an uncontrolled mass raising. I'm sure Master Su understands that we have no choice but to ask."

Su Mingli began to stand.

Yuanshu put two fingers lightly on her sleeve.

She looked at him. Then she sat back down, recrossed her legs, and folded her hands, like a woman settling in at the theater.

Yuanshu did not stand.

"Article Three has four conditions," he said. He didn't look at notes. He had read the Black Tide Act once, at five that morning, and Seed had held every word of it since. "The raising has to be uncontrolled. It has to go past the caster's registered capacity. It has to happen without lawful cause. And it has to harm the living."

The commissioner on the far left, a thin woman in her sixties with steel-gray hair, raised one eyebrow and made a note.

"You have four hours of drone footage," Yuanshu said. "Find me one skeleton that moved when I didn't tell it to. My registered capacity is whatever the Stele recorded, and the Stele recorded that it couldn't measure me. A grade-three rift opened over a house with a seventy-six-year-old man inside it." He paused. "And nobody was hurt. Except the wolves."

He folded his hands on the table and said nothing else.

The silence stretched.

In the glass behind the commissioners, the paused footage glowed: three hundred raised hands.

Director Lu smiled. "Admirably put. But the Act also says—"

General Han unfolded his arms.

He reached past Yuanshu's shoulder and laid a single sheet of paper on the long table, face up. He did it slowly, the way you'd lay down a winning card.

"This," the general said, "is a copy of the order that locked the Defense Corps out of sector four at 3:11 this morning for 'procedural review.' It's logged in the Eastern War Zone's system, because the Corps reports to us as well as to you." His voice was like a slab of concrete sliding across a floor. "It's signed by the Office of the Director."

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then the thin commissioner on the left leaned down from her raised seat, reached a long arm across the desk, and picked up the paper.

She read it.

She passed it to her right.

One by one, the paper went down the curved desk. One by one, four commissioners read it. And one by one, slowly, as if they were all on the same hinge, four heads turned to look at the center seat.

Heart rate: 58.

Still 58.

He expected this.

Director Lu took off his glasses. He took a soft gray cloth from his breast pocket and polished each lens carefully, and held them up to the light, and put them back on.

"Yes," he said. "I signed it."

The room took a breath.

"The Corps would have arrived in nine minutes," Lu went on, calmly. "They would have closed the rift. And no one would ever have known what a Calamity Necromancer does when his city is in danger. Would he run? Would he kill? Would he raise the dead and lose control of them, the way Lu Xuanming did?" He looked out at the city through the glass. "Thirty-one years ago I was a lieutenant on the Eastern Defense Line, commissioners. I watched the Black Tide from a trench. I needed to know. So I made a decision. I'll answer for it in the proper place."

General Han's jaw was tight enough to crack walnuts. "You used an old man as bait."

"I used a risk I judged acceptable," Lu said. "And I was right." He turned his pleasant face toward Yuanshu. "Mister Zhou passed. The Bureau will not be seeking detention. Congratulations."

The thin commissioner set the paper down on the desk, very precisely, squaring its edges with her fingertips.

"The Bureau," she said, "will be opening an internal review of the Director's office. Effective today."

"Of course," said Lu, entirely untroubled.

The hearing ended four minutes later.

The corridor outside was long and carpeted and full of the hush of a building where important people walk.

Director Lu fell into step beside Yuanshu as if they had arranged to meet. Su Mingli was three steps behind. General Han was five steps behind, which was as close as anyone was going to let him get to Lu without a witness.

"You're very good," Lu said, pleasantly. "Your mother was very good too."

Yuanshu did not slow down. "Was?"

"Is. Was. I don't know." Lu clasped his hands behind his back. "That's the truth, and you'll find I tell the truth more often than people expect." He walked a few steps in silence. "The Rift Research Institute belongs to the Bureau. Your mother's laboratory there has been sealed for seven years. Not by me. By her. The night she disappeared." He did not look back at Su Mingli, but he didn't need to. "Our engineers can't open it. Tianshu can't. A King can't. There's a lock on that door that reads law signatures." He glanced sideways. "I believe it's waiting for yours."

Heart rate: 71.

The first time it has changed.

He believes what he just said, and it frightens him.

They reached the elevators. Lu pressed the button for him, politely.

"And what do you want for the key?" Yuanshu asked.

"Cooperation. Nothing ugly. Share what you find. Let the Bureau watch." The elevator chimed. The doors slid open. "Something came down through the sky on the night of the Black Tide, Mister Zhou. Something your mother spent her whole life studying. It's still under this city." For a moment the pleasant face slipped, and something older and more tired looked out from behind it. "And I think you might be the only person alive who can tell us what it is."

Yuanshu stepped into the elevator. Su Mingli and General Han followed.

Lu stayed in the corridor, hands behind his back.

"I'll think about it," said Yuanshu.

"That's all I ask," said Director Lu, and the doors closed.

For three floors, nobody said anything.

Then General Han said, to the elevator wall, "I'm going to break his nose."

"Not yet," said Su Mingli. She was looking at Yuanshu. "You knew he'd admit it."

"Seed did. His heart never moved when the paper came out. A man lying about a signature panics when you show it to him." Yuanshu watched the floor numbers fall. "A man who wanted to be caught doesn't."

"Why would he want to be caught?"

"So we'd stop wondering whether he's my enemy," said Yuanshu, "and start wondering what he's afraid of."

The elevator hummed downward through the tower.

Observation: you did not ask him about the door.

You wanted to. When he mentioned it, your own heart rate went to 74.

If I'd asked, he'd know how much I want it.

Understood.

I am learning that humans keep the most important things where no one can see them.

I find this inefficient.

I also find that I like it.
